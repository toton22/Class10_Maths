Chapter 5 Arithmetic Progressions

    5.1 Lists that grow by a fixed step

    Patterns are everywhere — the petals of a sunflower, the holes of a honeycomb, the grains on a maize cob, the spirals of a pineapple and of a pine cone. Numbers taken from everyday life form patterns too. This chapter picks out the simplest and most useful pattern of all, and squeezes two formulas out of it.

    Here are six ordinary situations. In each one, write the numbers down in order, then look at what happens as you move from one number to the next.

    SituationThe list of numbersStep to the next term
    Reena is offered a job at ₹ 8000 a month with a rise of ₹ 500 every year. Her monthly salary in the 1st, 2nd, 3rd, … year.8000, 8500, 9000, …add 500
    The rungs of a ladder get shorter by 2 cm as you climb. The bottom rung is 45 cm long. Lengths of the 1st to the 8th rung.45, 43, 41, 39, 37, 35, 33, 31add −2
    In a savings scheme money becomes \(\tfrac54\) times itself every 3 years. Maturity value of ₹ 8000 after 3, 6, 9, 12 years.10000, 12500, 15625, 19531.25multiply by \(\tfrac54\)
    Number of unit squares inside a square of side 1, 2, 3, … units.\(1^2,\,2^2,\,3^2,\ldots\) that is 1, 4, 9, …add 3, then 5, then 7
    Shakila puts ₹ 100 into her daughter's money box on her first birthday and ₹ 50 more every year. Amount in the box on the 1st, 2nd, 3rd, 4th birthday.100, 150, 200, 250, …add 50
    A pair of rabbits is too young to breed in the first month; from the second month every pair produces a new pair each month. Pairs at the start of the 1st to the 6th month.1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8add the two before it

    Three of these six lists behave in exactly the same way: the same fixed number is added to get the next term. The others multiply, or square, or look back at two earlier terms. It is the “add a fixed number” family that this chapter is about.

    • Spot it

      Decide in one line whether a given list adds a fixed number, and write down its first term \(a\) and that fixed number \(d\).

    • Reach any term

      Jump straight to the 30th, the 100th or the “last” term without writing all the terms before it.

    • Add them up

      Total hundreds of terms in two lines — salaries, savings, logs in a stack, prize money.

    You already do this. Counting in twos (2, 4, 6, 8, …), the multiplication table of 7 (7, 14, 21, …) and the marks on a ruler are all lists that grow by a fixed step. You have been using arithmetic progressions since Class III without the name.

    5.2 Arithmetic progressions

    Now we fix the words. Each number in a list is called a term, and the fixed number that is added each time is called the common difference.

    Look at five lists and say, in each case, how the next term is made from the one before it.

    ListRule from one term to the nextFixed number added
    1, 2, 3, 4, …each term is 1 more than the one before1
    100, 70, 40, 10, …each term is 30 less than the one before\(-30\)
    \(-3,\ -2,\ -1,\ 0,\ \ldots\)each term is 1 more than the one before1
    3, 3, 3, 3, …each term is the one before, unchanged0
    \(-1.0,\ -1.5,\ -2.0,\ -2.5,\ \ldots\)each term is 0.5 less than the one before\(-0.5\)

    In all five, the next term comes from the previous one by adding a fixed number. Such a list is called an arithmetic progression, written AP for short.

    Definition

    An arithmetic progression is a list of numbers in which each term, except the first, is obtained by adding a fixed number to the term before it. That fixed number is called the common difference of the AP and is written \(d\).

    The common difference may be positive, negative or zero.

    The symbols we shall use

    \(a_1,\ a_2,\ a_3,\ \ldots\)
    the first term, second term, third term, … of the AP
    \(a\)
    short for \(a_1\), the first term
    \(d\)
    the common difference
    \(a_n\)
    the \(n\)-th term, also called the general term
    \(l\)
    the last term, used only when the AP is finite

    Because every term is the previous one plus \(d\),

    $$a_2-a_1=a_3-a_2=\cdots=a_n-a_{n-1}=d,$$

    and in general \(d=a_{k+1}-a_k\) for every \(k\). So to find \(d\) you need only one pair of neighbouring terms — but a good student checks two or three pairs before believing that the list really is an AP.

    Always subtract the earlier term from the later one: \(d=a_{k+1}-a_k\), never \(a_k-a_{k+1}\). For 6, 3, 0, \(-3,\ldots\) the common difference is \(3-6=-3\), not \(6-3=3\). Getting this sign wrong flips the whole AP and costs every mark that follows.

    The general form of an AP

    Start with a first term \(a\). Add \(d\) to get the second term, add \(d\) again for the third, and so on. So every AP can be written as

    $$a,\quad a+d,\quad a+2d,\quad a+3d,\ \ldots$$

    This is called the general form of an AP. Notice what the coefficient of \(d\) is doing: it is always one less than the term number, because you make the 4th term from the 1st by taking 3 jumps, not 4.

    An AP is fixed by just two numbers
    $$\text{AP}=\{\,a,\ a+d,\ a+2d,\ a+3d,\ \ldots\,\}$$

    Know \(a\) and \(d\) and you know the whole list. For \(a=6,\ d=3\) the AP is 6, 9, 12, 15, …; for \(a=6,\ d=-3\) it is 6, 3, 0, \(-3\), … . Neither number alone is enough.

    What the sign of \(d\) does

    Finite AP

    The list stops. It has a last term, written \(l\), and a definite number of terms.

    Heights of students in a queue: 147, 148, 149, … , 157. Cash prizes: 200, 250, 300, … , 750.

    Infinite AP

    The list never stops, so there is no last term. It is written with three dots at the end.

    1, 2, 3, 4, … and 100, 70, 40, 10, … are infinite APs.

    Some more APs from real life, all of them finite: the balance (in ₹) left on a loan of ₹ 1000 repaid at 5 % a month — 950, 900, 850, 800, … , 50; and the total savings (in ₹) after each of 10 months when ₹ 50 is saved every month — 50, 100, 150, … , 500.

    How to test a list

    1. Name the termswrite the list as \(a_1,a_2,a_3,a_4,\ldots\)
    2. Take differenceswork out \(a_2-a_1\), then \(a_3-a_2\), then \(a_4-a_3\)
    3. Compareall equal → it is an AP, and that common value is \(d\); any one different → not an AP
    4. Extendto write the next terms, keep adding \(d\) to the last term you have
    NCERT Example 1reading off a and d

    For the AP \(\dfrac32,\ \dfrac12,\ -\dfrac12,\ -\dfrac32,\ \ldots\) write down the first term \(a\) and the common difference \(d\).

    Solution

    The first term is the number the list starts with, so \(a=\dfrac32\).

    For the common difference, subtract the first term from the second:

    $$d=a_2-a_1=\frac12-\frac32=-\frac{2}{2}=-1.$$

    \(a=\dfrac32\) and \(d=-1\)

    Since we are told the list is an AP, any neighbouring pair gives the same answer — check with the next pair: \(-\dfrac12-\dfrac12=-1\). ✓

    NCERT Example 2is it an AP?

    Which of these lists of numbers form an AP? If a list does form an AP, write its next two terms.

    1. 4, 10, 16, 22, …
    2. \(1,\ -1,\ -3,\ -5,\ \ldots\)
    3. \(-2,\ 2,\ -2,\ 2,\ -2,\ \ldots\)
    4. 1, 1, 1, 2, 2, 2, 3, 3, 3, …
    Solution
    (i)
    $$\begin{aligned} a_2-a_1&=10-4=6\\ a_3-a_2&=16-10=6\\ a_4-a_3&=22-16=6 \end{aligned}$$

    Every difference is 6, so \(a_{k+1}-a_k\) is the same each time. The list is an AP with \(d=6\).

    Next two terms: \(22+6=28\) and \(28+6=34\).

    AP, \(d=6\); next terms 28 and 34

    (ii)
    $$\begin{aligned} a_2-a_1&=-1-1=-2\\ a_3-a_2&=-3-(-1)=-3+1=-2\\ a_4-a_3&=-5-(-3)=-5+3=-2 \end{aligned}$$

    The difference is \(-2\) every time, so it is an AP with \(d=-2\).

    Next two terms: \(-5+(-2)=-7\) and \(-7+(-2)=-9\).

    AP, \(d=-2\); next terms \(-7\) and \(-9\)

    (iii)
    $$\begin{aligned} a_2-a_1&=2-(-2)=4\\ a_3-a_2&=-2-2=-4 \end{aligned}$$

    Here \(a_2-a_1\ne a_3-a_2\), so the differences are not all the same.

    Not an AP

    (iv)
    $$\begin{aligned} a_2-a_1&=1-1=0\\ a_3-a_2&=1-1=0\\ a_4-a_3&=2-1=1 \end{aligned}$$

    The first two differences are 0 but the third is 1, so \(a_2-a_1=a_3-a_2\ne a_4-a_3\).

    Not an AP

    In parts (iii) and (iv) one bad pair is enough to settle the matter — you do not have to test the whole list.

    Two differences are never enough to prove a list is an AP, but one unequal pair is always enough to disprove it. In the exam, show three differences when you claim “it is an AP”, and show the single offending pair when you claim “it is not”.

    “Which of the following are APs? If so, find \(d\) and write three more terms” is the standard 2-mark opener of this chapter. Marks are given for the differences you write, not for the verdict alone — so always show \(a_2-a_1,\ a_3-a_2,\ a_4-a_3\).

    Is \(\sqrt2,\ \sqrt8,\ \sqrt{18},\ \sqrt{32},\ \ldots\) an AP? If yes, what is \(d\)?

    Show answer

    Simplify first: \(\sqrt8=2\sqrt2\), \(\sqrt{18}=3\sqrt2\), \(\sqrt{32}=4\sqrt2\). The list is \(\sqrt2,\ 2\sqrt2,\ 3\sqrt2,\ 4\sqrt2,\ \ldots\), so every difference is \(\sqrt2\). Yes, it is an AP with \(d=\sqrt2\). Surds almost always need simplifying before you take differences.

    5.3 The \(n\)th term of an AP

    Go back to Reena. Her monthly salary starts at ₹ 8000 and rises by ₹ 500 every year. What will she earn in the fifth year? In the fifteenth? In the twenty-fifth? Writing out every year is possible but slow — and in the exam you will not have the time.

    Build the first few salaries honestly, and watch the pattern in the brackets.

    $$\begin{aligned} \text{2nd year}&=8000+500\\ &=8000+(2-1)\times500=8500\\[2pt] \text{3rd year}&=8500+500\\ &=8000+2\times500\\ &=8000+(3-1)\times500=9000\\[2pt] \text{4th year}&=8000+3\times500\\ &=8000+(4-1)\times500=9500 \end{aligned}$$

    The number of 500s added is always one less than the year number, because the first year needs no rise at all. So there is no need to walk through the years one at a time:

    $$\begin{aligned} \text{15th year}&=8000+(15-1)\times500\\ &=8000+7000=15000\\[2pt] \text{25th year}&=8000+(25-1)\times500\\ &=8000+12000=20000 \end{aligned}$$

    In words: first salary \(+\) (year number \(-\) 1) \(\times\) annual increment. Exactly the same reasoning works for every AP.

    Building the formula

    Let the AP be \(a_1,a_2,a_3,\ldots\) with first term \(a_1=a\) and common difference \(d\). Then

    TermBuilt from the one beforeIn terms of \(a\) and \(d\)
    \(a_1\)the start\(a=a+(1-1)d\)
    \(a_2\)\(a_1+d\)\(a+d=a+(2-1)d\)
    \(a_3\)\(a_2+d=(a+d)+d\)\(a+2d=a+(3-1)d\)
    \(a_4\)\(a_3+d=(a+2d)+d\)\(a+3d=a+(4-1)d\)
    \(a_n\)\(a_{n-1}+d\)\(a+(n-1)d\)
    \(n\)th term (general term) of an AP
    $$a_n=a+(n-1)\,d$$

    \(a\) is the first term, \(d\) the common difference and \(n\) the position of the term you want. If the AP is finite with \(m\) terms, then \(a_m\) is the last term and is often written \(l\).

    Read it as “first term plus one jump less than the term number”. The \(-1\) is there because the first term is where you start — you have not jumped yet. Students who write \(a_n=a+nd\) always get an answer that is \(d\) too big.

    The formula ties together four quantities: \(a\), \(d\), \(n\) and \(a_n\). Give any three and the fourth can be found. That single sentence covers almost every question in Exercise 5.2.

    • Find a term

      \(a,\ d,\ n\) given → substitute and compute \(a_n\).

    • Find the position

      \(a,\ d,\ a_n\) given → solve the equation for \(n\). A whole-number \(n\) means the number really is a term.

    • Find \(a\) or \(d\)

      Two terms given → two equations in \(a\) and \(d\); subtract one from the other.
    NCERT Example 3straight substitution

    Find the 10th term of the AP 2, 7, 12, …

    Solution

    Here \(a=2\), \(d=7-2=5\) and \(n=10\).

    $$\begin{aligned} a_n&=a+(n-1)d\\ a_{10}&=2+(10-1)\times5\\ &=2+45=47 \end{aligned}$$

    The 10th term is 47

    NCERT Example 4finding the position n

    Which term of the AP 21, 18, 15, … is \(-81\)? Also, is any term of this AP equal to 0? Give a reason for your answer.

    Solution

    Here \(a=21\) and \(d=18-21=-3\). We are told \(a_n=-81\) and have to find \(n\).

    $$\begin{aligned} a_n&=a+(n-1)d\\ -81&=21+(n-1)(-3)\\ -81&=21-3n+3\\ -81&=24-3n\\ 3n&=24+81=105\\ n&=35 \end{aligned}$$

    \(-81\) is the 35th term

    Is any term 0? Suppose \(a_n=0\) for some \(n\). Then

    $$\begin{aligned} 0&=21+(n-1)(-3)\\ 3(n-1)&=21\\ n-1&=7\\ n&=8 \end{aligned}$$

    Here \(n=8\) is a positive whole number, so such a term does exist.

    Yes — the 8th term is 0

    NCERT Example 5two terms given

    Determine the AP whose 3rd term is 5 and whose 7th term is 9.

    Solution

    Write both facts with the \(n\)th term formula.

    $$\begin{aligned} a_3=a+(3-1)d&=a+2d=5 &&\cdots(1)\\ a_7=a+(7-1)d&=a+6d=9 &&\cdots(2) \end{aligned}$$

    Subtract (1) from (2) — the \(a\) disappears:

    $$\begin{aligned} (a+6d)-(a+2d)&=9-5\\ 4d&=4\\ d&=1 \end{aligned}$$

    Put \(d=1\) into (1): \(a+2(1)=5\), so \(a=3\).

    With \(a=3\) and \(d=1\) the terms are \(3,\ 3+1,\ 4+1,\ 5+1,\ \ldots\)

    The AP is 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, …

    When two terms of an AP are given, do not solve the pair of equations by substitution — just subtract them. The difference of the \(m\)th and \(n\)th terms is always \((m-n)d\), so \(d\) drops out in one line: here \(a_7-a_3=4d\). You will use this shortcut in a dozen board questions.

    NCERT Example 6is this number a term?

    Check whether 301 is a term of the list of numbers 5, 11, 17, 23, …

    Solution

    First make sure the list is an AP:

    $$\begin{aligned} a_2-a_1&=11-5=6\\ a_3-a_2&=17-11=6\\ a_4-a_3&=23-17=6 \end{aligned}$$

    The difference is the same each time, so it is an AP with \(a=5\) and \(d=6\).

    Suppose 301 is the \(n\)th term of this AP. Then

    $$\begin{aligned} 301&=5+(n-1)\times6\\ 301&=6n-1\\ 6n&=302\\ n&=\frac{302}{6}=\frac{151}{3} \end{aligned}$$

    The position of a term must be a positive whole number, and \(\dfrac{151}{3}\) is not a whole number.

    301 is not a term of the given list

    Never answer such a question with “no, because 301 is not in the list I wrote”. The proof is the value of \(n\): if \(n\) comes out a positive integer the number is a term, and if \(n\) is a fraction, zero or negative it is not. Say so in one clear line.

    NCERT Example 7counting how many

    How many two-digit numbers are divisible by 3?

    Solution

    The smallest two-digit multiple of 3 is 12 and the largest is 99, so the numbers we are counting are

    $$12,\ 15,\ 18,\ \ldots,\ 99.$$

    Each one is 3 more than the one before, so this is a finite AP with \(a=12\), \(d=3\) and last term \(a_n=99\). Counting them means finding \(n\).

    $$\begin{aligned} 99&=12+(n-1)\times3\\ 87&=(n-1)\times3\\ n-1&=\frac{87}{3}=29\\ n&=30 \end{aligned}$$

    There are 30 two-digit numbers divisible by 3

    “How many multiples of … lie between … and …?” is asked almost every year. The recipe never changes: first multiple \(=a\), the gap \(=d\), last multiple \(=a_n\), then solve for \(n\). The only trap is at the ends — check whether the boundary numbers themselves are to be counted.

    A term counted from the end

    Sometimes a finite AP is read backwards: “the 11th term from the last term”. There are two clean ways to handle it, and both are worth knowing.

    Way 1 — turn it round

    Find how many terms \(n\) the AP has. The \(k\)th term from the end is then the

    $$(n-k+1)\text{th term from the start.}$$

    Now use \(a_n=a+(n-1)d\) as usual.

    Way 2 — reverse the AP

    Read the AP backwards. It is still an AP, but it starts at the last term \(l\) and its common difference is \(-d\). So

    $$k\text{th term from the end}=l+(k-1)(-d).$$

    The 11th term from the end of a 25-term AP is the 15th from the start, not the 14th — because \(25-11+1=15\). Think of a queue of 25 children: the 11th from the back has 10 children behind him, so he has \(25-11=14\) in front, making him the 15th. Always add the 1.

    NCERT Example 8counting from the end

    Find the 11th term from the last term (moving towards the first term) of the AP 10, 7, 4, … , \(-62\).

    Solution

    Here \(a=10\), \(d=7-10=-3\) and the last term is \(l=-62\).

    Step 1 — how many terms are there? Put \(a_n=l=-62\).

    $$\begin{aligned} -62&=10+(n-1)(-3)\\ -72&=(n-1)(-3)\\ n-1&=24\\ n&=25 \end{aligned}$$

    So the AP has 25 terms.

    Step 2 — which term from the start is it? The 11th from the end is the \((25-11+1)=15\)th term from the start.

    $$\begin{aligned} a_{15}&=10+(15-1)(-3)\\ &=10-42=-32 \end{aligned}$$

    The 11th term from the last term is \(-32\)

    Alternative solution. Write the AP backwards. It then begins at \(-62\) and rises by 3, so its first term is \(-62\) and its common difference is \(+3\). The term we want is now simply the 11th term of this reversed AP:

    $$\begin{aligned} a_{11}&=-62+(11-1)\times3\\ &=-62+30=-32 \end{aligned}$$

    Both routes give \(-32\) — use whichever you find quicker, but show the reasoning.

    NCERT Example 9simple interest as an AP

    A sum of ₹ 1000 is invested at 8 % simple interest per year. Work out the interest earned at the end of each year, check whether these amounts form an AP, and if they do, find the interest at the end of 30 years by using that fact.

    Solution

    Simple interest for \(\text{T}\) years on a principal \(\text{P}\) at \(\text{R}\) % per year is

    $$\text{S.I.}=\frac{\text{P}\times \text{R}\times \text{T}}{100}.$$

    With \(\text{P}=1000\) and \(\text{R}=8\):

    $$\begin{aligned} \text{after 1 year}&=\frac{1000\times8\times1}{100}=80\\[2pt] \text{after 2 years}&=\frac{1000\times8\times2}{100}=160\\[2pt] \text{after 3 years}&=\frac{1000\times8\times3}{100}=240 \end{aligned}$$

    So the interest (in ₹) at the end of the 1st, 2nd, 3rd, … years is

    $$80,\ 160,\ 240,\ \ldots$$

    Each amount is 80 more than the one before it, so this is an AP with \(a=80\) and \(d=80\).

    The interest at the end of 30 years is the 30th term:

    $$\begin{aligned} a_{30}&=a+(30-1)d\\ &=80+29\times80\\ &=80\times30=2400 \end{aligned}$$

    The interest at the end of 30 years is ₹ 2400

    Simple interest always gives an AP, because the same interest is added every year. Compound interest does not — there the money is multiplied each year, not added to by a fixed amount.

    NCERT Example 10word problem — find n

    A flower bed has 23 rose plants in the first row, 21 in the second row, 19 in the third, and so on, going down by the same number each time. The last row has 5 rose plants. How many rows are there in the flower bed?

    Solution

    The numbers of rose plants in the 1st, 2nd, 3rd, … rows are

    $$23,\ 21,\ 19,\ \ldots,\ 5.$$

    Each row has 2 plants fewer than the row before it, so this is an AP with \(a=23\) and \(d=21-23=-2\). The last row is the last term, so \(a_n=5\), and the number of rows is \(n\).

    $$\begin{aligned} a_n&=a+(n-1)d\\ 5&=23+(n-1)(-2)\\ 5-23&=(n-1)(-2)\\ -18&=(n-1)(-2)\\ n-1&=9\\ n&=10 \end{aligned}$$

    There are 10 rows in the flower bed

    In a word problem, write the three or four numbers of the story as a list before you touch a formula. Once you can see the list, \(a\) and \(d\) stare back at you, and the only decision left is whether the unknown is \(n\) or \(a_n\).

    The 17th term of an AP exceeds its 10th term by 7. What is the common difference?

    Show answer

    \(a_{17}-a_{10}=(17-10)d=7d\). We are told this equals 7, so \(7d=7\) and \(d=1\). No need to find \(a\) at all.

    5.4 The sum of the first \(n\) terms of an AP

    Shakila put ₹ 100 into her daughter's money box on her first birthday, ₹ 150 on the second, ₹ 200 on the third, and kept going in the same way. How much money is in the box by the time the girl turns 21?

    The amounts are 100, 150, 200, 250, … up to the 21st birthday — an AP. To answer the question we would have to write down all 21 amounts and add them. That is slow, and easy to get wrong. There must be a better way, and there is.

    Gauss and the first hundred numbers

    The story is famous. A ten-year-old Carl Friedrich Gauss was told to add up all the numbers from 1 to 100. He answered almost at once: 5050. This is what he saw.

    $$\begin{aligned} \text{S}&=1+2+3+\cdots+99+100\\ \text{S}&=100+99+\cdots+3+2+1 \end{aligned}$$

    Adding the two lines column by column,

    $$\begin{aligned} 2\text{S}&=101+101+\cdots+101\\ &=100\times101=10100\\ \text{S}&=\frac{100\times101}{2}=5050 \end{aligned}$$

    The trick works because the list rises by 1 while its reverse falls by 1, so the two changes cancel and every column has the same total. That is exactly what an AP does — so the same trick works for every AP.

    The derivation you must know

    Take the AP \(a,\ a+d,\ a+2d,\ \ldots\) whose \(n\)th term is \(a+(n-1)d\), and let S stand for the sum of its first \(n\) terms.

    $$\begin{aligned} \text{S}&=a+(a+d)+(a+2d)\\ &\quad+\cdots+[a+(n-1)d] \end{aligned}\tag{1}$$

    Write the same terms in reverse order:

    $$\begin{aligned} \text{S}&=[a+(n-1)d]+[a+(n-2)d]\\ &\quad+\cdots+(a+d)+a \end{aligned}\tag{2}$$

    Now add (1) and (2) column by column. The first column gives \(a+[a+(n-1)d]=2a+(n-1)d\). The second gives \((a+d)+[a+(n-2)d]=2a+(n-1)d\), the same again. In fact every one of the \(n\) columns adds up to \(2a+(n-1)d\).

    $$\begin{aligned} 2\text{S}&=n\,[\,2a+(n-1)d\,]\\ \text{S}&=\frac n2\,[\,2a+(n-1)d\,] \end{aligned}$$
    Sum of the first \(n\) terms of an AP
    $$S_n=\frac n2\,\bigl[\,2a+(n-1)d\,\bigr]$$

    Use this form when you know the first term \(a\), the common difference \(d\) and how many terms \(n\) you are adding.

    There is a second form, and it is often faster. Split \(2a\) as \(a+a\):

    $$\begin{aligned} S_n&=\frac n2\,[\,a+a+(n-1)d\,]\\ &=\frac n2\,[\,a+a_n\,] \end{aligned}$$

    If the AP is finite and \(a_n\) is its last term \(l\), this becomes the version you will use in almost every word problem.

    Sum when the last term is known
    $$S_n=\frac n2\,(a+l)$$

    \(l=a_n\) is the last term of the \(n\) terms being added. In words: half the number of terms, times (first term + last term).

    Which form do I use?

    Given \(a\), \(d\) and \(n\) → use \(S_n=\dfrac n2[2a+(n-1)d]\).

    Given the first and the last term → use \(S_n=\dfrac n2(a+l)\).

    Given only \(a\) and \(l\) but not \(n\) → find \(n\) first from \(l=a+(n-1)d\).

    Four quantities, three known

    The sum formula links S, \(a\), \(d\) and \(n\). Know any three of them and the fourth can be found — exactly as \(a_n=a+(n-1)d\) links \(a_n,\ a,\ d,\ n\).

    When the unknown is \(n\), you usually end up with a quadratic equation in \(n\).

    Back to the money box

    The amounts are \(100,\ 150,\ 200,\ 250,\ \ldots\) so \(a=100\), \(d=50\), and up to the 21st birthday there are \(n=21\) of them.

    $$\begin{aligned} S_{21}&=\frac{21}{2}\,[\,2\times100+(21-1)\times50\,]\\ &=\frac{21}{2}\,[\,200+1000\,]\\ &=\frac{21}{2}\times1200\\ &=21\times600=12600 \end{aligned}$$

    ₹ 12600 is collected by the daughter's 21st birthday

    We write \(S_n\) for the sum of the first \(n\) terms, so \(S_{20}\) means the sum of the first 20 terms. Do not confuse \(S_n\) (a running total) with \(a_n\) (a single term). \(S_1=a_1=a\) always.

    Getting a term back out of the sums

    \(S_n\) adds the first \(n\) terms and \(S_{n-1}\) adds the first \(n-1\) terms. The only difference between them is the \(n\)th term itself.

    \(n\)th term from the sums
    $$a_n=S_n-S_{n-1}$$

    Useful whenever a question gives you \(S_n\) as a formula in \(n\) and asks for a particular term. Note that \(a_1=S_1\), since there is no \(S_0\) to subtract.

    NCERT Example 11direct sum

    Find the sum of the first 22 terms of the AP 8, 3, \(-2\), …

    Solution

    Here \(a=8\), \(d=3-8=-5\) and \(n=22\).

    $$\begin{aligned} S_n&=\frac n2\,[\,2a+(n-1)d\,]\\ S_{22}&=\frac{22}{2}\,[\,2\times8+21\times(-5)\,]\\ &=11\,[\,16-105\,]\\ &=11\times(-89)=-979 \end{aligned}$$

    \(S_{22}=-979\)

    A negative sum is perfectly normal when \(d\) is negative — after a while the terms themselves turn negative and pull the total down.

    NCERT Example 12sum given, find d

    The sum of the first 14 terms of an AP is 1050 and its first term is 10. Find the 20th term.

    Solution

    Here \(S_{14}=1050\), \(n=14\) and \(a=10\); the common difference \(d\) is unknown.

    $$\begin{aligned} S_n&=\frac n2\,[\,2a+(n-1)d\,]\\ 1050&=\frac{14}{2}\,[\,20+13d\,]\\ 1050&=7\,[\,20+13d\,]\\ 1050&=140+91d\\ 91d&=910\\ d&=10 \end{aligned}$$

    Now use the \(n\)th term formula with \(a=10\) and \(d=10\):

    $$\begin{aligned} a_{20}&=a+(20-1)d\\ &=10+19\times10=200 \end{aligned}$$

    The 20th term is 200

    NCERT Example 13quadratic in n — two answers

    How many terms of the AP 24, 21, 18, … must be taken so that their sum is 78?

    Solution

    Here \(a=24\), \(d=21-24=-3\) and \(S_n=78\). We must find \(n\).

    $$\begin{aligned} S_n&=\frac n2\,[\,2a+(n-1)d\,]\\ 78&=\frac n2\,[\,48+(n-1)(-3)\,]\\ 78&=\frac n2\,[\,51-3n\,]\\ 156&=51n-3n^2 \end{aligned}$$

    Bring everything to one side and divide by 3:

    $$\begin{aligned} 3n^2-51n+156&=0\\ n^2-17n+52&=0\\ (n-4)(n-13)&=0\\ n=4\ \ \text{or}\ \ n&=13 \end{aligned}$$

    Both values are positive whole numbers, so both are acceptable.

    4 terms or 13 terms

    Why two answers? Here \(a\) is positive while \(d\) is negative, so the AP runs 24, 21, 18, 15, 12, 9, 6, 3, 0, \(-3\), \(-6\), \(-9\), \(-12\), … The terms from the 5th to the 13th are \(12,9,6,3,0,-3,-6,-9,-12\), and they add up to 0. Adding them changes nothing, which is why \(S_4=S_{13}=78\).

    When solving for \(n\) you will often get a quadratic. Reject any root that is negative, zero or a fraction — the number of terms must be a positive whole number. But if both roots are positive whole numbers, as here, both are correct answers and you must write both, with the reason.

    NCERT Example 14sum of the first n integers

    Find the sum of

    1. the first 1000 positive integers
    2. the first \(n\) positive integers
    Solution
    (i)

    Let \(S=1+2+3+\cdots+1000\). Here \(a=1\), the last term is \(l=1000\) and the number of terms is \(n=1000\), so the form \(S_n=\dfrac n2(a+l)\) is the quick one.

    $$\begin{aligned} S_{1000}&=\frac{1000}{2}\,(1+1000)\\ &=500\times1001=500500 \end{aligned}$$

    500500

    (ii)

    Let \(S_n=1+2+3+\cdots+n\). Here \(a=1\) and the last term is \(l=n\), and there are \(n\) terms.

    $$\begin{aligned} S_n&=\frac n2\,(1+n)\\ &=\frac{n(n+1)}{2} \end{aligned}$$

    \(S_n=\dfrac{n(n+1)}{2}\)

    Sum of the first \(n\) natural numbers
    $$1+2+3+\cdots+n=\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$$

    Worth memorising — it turns up constantly. Two useful cousins follow from it: the first \(n\) even numbers add to \(n(n+1)\), and the first \(n\) odd numbers add to \(n^2\).

    NCERT Example 15AP given by its nth term

    Find the sum of the first 24 terms of the list of numbers whose \(n\)th term is given by \(a_n=3+2n\).

    Solution

    First write out a few terms by putting \(n=1,2,3,\ldots\) into \(a_n=3+2n\):

    $$\begin{aligned} a_1&=3+2\times1=5\\ a_2&=3+2\times2=7\\ a_3&=3+2\times3=9 \end{aligned}$$

    So the list is 5, 7, 9, 11, … Check that it is an AP: \(7-5=2\), \(9-7=2\), \(11-9=2\). Yes, with \(d=2\).

    Now use the sum formula with \(a=5\), \(d=2\) and \(n=24\):

    $$\begin{aligned} S_{24}&=\frac{24}{2}\,[\,2\times5+(24-1)\times2\,]\\ &=12\,[\,10+46\,]\\ &=12\times56=672 \end{aligned}$$

    \(S_{24}=672\)

    Whenever \(a_n\) is a linear expression in \(n\), such as \(3+2n\), the list is automatically an AP whose common difference is the coefficient of \(n\) — here 2.

    NCERT Example 16word problem — term and sum

    A manufacturer of TV sets made 600 sets in the third year and 700 sets in the seventh year. Production goes up by the same fixed number of sets every year. Find

    1. the production in the 1st year
    2. the production in the 10th year
    3. the total production in the first 7 years
    Solution

    Production rises by a fixed number each year, so the numbers of sets made in the 1st, 2nd, 3rd, … years form an AP. Let \(a_n\) be the number of sets made in the \(n\)th year, with first term \(a\) and common difference \(d\).

    We are given \(a_3=600\) and \(a_7=700\), that is

    $$\begin{aligned} a+2d&=600 &&\cdots(1)\\ a+6d&=700 &&\cdots(2) \end{aligned}$$
    (i)

    Subtract (1) from (2):

    $$\begin{aligned} 4d&=100\\ d&=25 \end{aligned}$$

    Put \(d=25\) into (1): \(a+50=600\), so \(a=550\).

    550 sets were made in the 1st year

    (ii)
    $$\begin{aligned} a_{10}&=a+(10-1)d\\ &=550+9\times25\\ &=550+225=775 \end{aligned}$$

    775 sets were made in the 10th year

    (iii)
    $$\begin{aligned} S_7&=\frac72\,[\,2\times550+(7-1)\times25\,]\\ &=\frac72\,[\,1100+150\,]\\ &=\frac72\times1250=4375 \end{aligned}$$

    4375 sets were made in the first 7 years

    Five-mark questions from this chapter are nearly always of the Example 16 shape: two facts are given, you set up two equations, subtract to get \(d\), back-substitute for \(a\), and then answer a “which term” part and a “total” part. Write the formula before each substitution — one mark is reserved for it.

    Before you reach for \(S_n=\dfrac n2[2a+(n-1)d]\), ask whether you already know the last term. If you do, \(S_n=\dfrac n2(a+l)\) is shorter and safer — “half the count times first plus last”. For 12, 15, 18, … , 99 there are 30 terms, so the sum is \(15\times(12+99)=1665\) in one line.

    The sum of the first \(n\) terms of an AP is \(S_n=3n^2+2n\). What is its 5th term?

    Show answer

    Use \(a_n=S_n-S_{n-1}\). Here \(S_5=3(25)+2(5)=85\) and \(S_4=3(16)+2(4)=56\), so \(a_5=85-56=29\). (As a check, \(S_1=5\) gives \(a=5\), and \(S_2=16\) gives \(a_2=11\), so \(d=6\) and \(a_5=5+4\times6=29\). ✓)

    5.5 The chapter in one page

    Everything in this chapter grows out of one idea — the same number is added every time — and lands on two formulas. Here they are together, with the things students most often get wrong.

    1. An arithmetic progression (AP) is a list of numbers in which each term after the first is got by adding a fixed number \(d\), the common difference, to the term before it. Its general form is \(a,\ a+d,\ a+2d,\ a+3d,\ \ldots\)
    2. A list \(a_1,a_2,a_3,\ldots\) is an AP exactly when the differences \(a_2-a_1,\ a_3-a_2,\ a_4-a_3,\ \ldots\) are all equal — that is, when \(a_{k+1}-a_k\) has the same value for every \(k\).
    3. With first term \(a\) and common difference \(d\), the \(n\)th term (the general term) is \(a_n=a+(n-1)d\).
    4. The sum of the first \(n\) terms is \(S_n=\dfrac n2\bigl[2a+(n-1)d\bigr]\).
    5. If the AP is finite with last term \(l\) (its \(n\)th term), the sum of all its terms is \(S_n=\dfrac n2\,(a+l)\).
    The whole chapter
    $$\begin{aligned} d&=a_{k+1}-a_k\\ a_n&=a+(n-1)d\\ S_n&=\frac n2\bigl[2a+(n-1)d\bigr]=\frac n2(a+l)\\ a_n&=S_n-S_{n-1} \end{aligned}$$

    Four formulas, and the last one is only a way of getting back from sums to terms.

    Arithmetic mean

    A note to remember

    If \(a\), \(b\), \(c\) are three numbers in AP, then the middle one is the average of the outer two:

    $$b-a=c-b\quad\Rightarrow\quad 2b=a+c\quad\Rightarrow\quad b=\frac{a+c}{2}.$$

    This \(b\) is called the arithmetic mean of \(a\) and \(c\).

    This gives a neat trick for questions that say “three numbers are in AP; their sum is … and their product is …”. Choose the three numbers as \(a-d,\ a,\ a+d\). Their sum is then simply \(3a\), and the problem collapses in a line. For four numbers in AP take \(a-3d,\ a-d,\ a+d,\ a+3d\) — their common difference is \(2d\) and their sum is \(4a\).

    The six mistakes that cost marks

    • \(a+nd\)

      Writing \(a_n=a+nd\) instead of \(a+(n-1)d\). Every answer then comes out one \(d\) too large.

    • Backwards \(d\)

      Computing \(a_1-a_2\) instead of \(a_2-a_1\). For 10, 7, 4, … the common difference is \(-3\), not \(3\).

    • Losing a bracket

      \((n-1)(-3)=-3n+3\), not \(-3n-3\). Expand slowly when \(d\) is negative.

    • Off-by-one from the end

      The \(k\)th term from the end of an \(n\)-term AP is the \((n-k+1)\)th from the start — remember the \(+1\).

    • Fractional \(n\)

      Keeping a root like \(n=\tfrac{15}{2}\) or \(n=-4\). The number of terms is always a positive whole number, so reject such roots and say why.

    • \(S_n\) instead of \(a_n\)

      Answering “the total” when the question asked for “the 20th term”, or the other way round. Read the last line of the question twice.

    How the board splits the marks. 1 mark for writing the correct formula, 1 mark for correct substitution, 1 mark for the simplification and 1 mark for the final answer with its unit (₹, cm, sets, rows). Even if your arithmetic slips, the formula and substitution marks are yours — so never skip straight to a number.

    The 4th term of an AP is 0. Show that its 25th term is three times its 11th term.

    Show answer

    \(a_4=a+3d=0\), so \(a=-3d\). Then \(a_{11}=a+10d=-3d+10d=7d\) and \(a_{25}=a+24d=-3d+24d=21d\). Since \(21d=3\times7d\), the 25th term is three times the 11th. Hence proved.

    Find the sum \(5+8+11+\cdots+32\).

    Show answer

    \(a=5\), \(d=3\), \(l=32\). First find \(n\): \(32=5+(n-1)3\Rightarrow 27=3(n-1)\Rightarrow n=10\). Then \(S_{10}=\dfrac{10}{2}(5+32)=5\times37=185\).

    Exercise 5.1

    4 questions · recognising an AP, writing its terms, reading off \(a\) and \(d\)

    Every question in this exercise is answered by the same one-line test. Write the list of numbers, then subtract each term from the term that comes just after it:

    \(a_2-a_1,\quad a_3-a_2,\quad a_4-a_3,\ \dots\)

    If all these differences are the same number, the list is an AP and that number is the common difference \(d\). If even one of them is different, it is not an AP. Always subtract the earlier term from the later one — not the other way round, even when the numbers are going down.

    Q1

    Each situation below produces a list of numbers. Decide whether that list is an arithmetic progression, and say why.

    1. A taxi charges ₹ 15 for the first kilometre and ₹ 8 for every extra kilometre. List the fare after each kilometre.
    2. A vacuum pump removes \(\dfrac14\) of the air that is still in a cylinder at each stroke. List the amount of air left in the cylinder.
    3. Digging a well costs ₹ 150 for the first metre, and every metre after that costs ₹ 50 more than the metre before it. List the cost of digging each successive metre.
    4. ₹ 10000 is deposited in an account that pays compound interest at 8 % per year. List the amount in the account at the end of each year.
    Solution

    Method: build the list first, then check whether the difference between consecutive terms stays the same.

    (i)

    Fare for 1 km = ₹ 15. Each extra kilometre adds ₹ 8, so

    \(15,\ 15+8,\ 15+8+8,\ \dots\) that is, 15, 23, 31, 39, …

    $$\begin{aligned}a_2-a_1&=23-15=8\\ a_3-a_2&=31-23=8\\ a_4-a_3&=39-31=8\end{aligned}$$

    The difference is the same every time, so the list is an AP.

    Yes, it is an AP with \(a=15\) and \(d=8\).

    (ii)

    Let the cylinder hold \(V\) units of air to begin with. One stroke removes \(\dfrac14\) of what is there, so \(\dfrac34\) of it is left. The same thing happens at every stroke:

    $$V,\ \ \frac34V,\ \ \left(\frac34\right)^{2}V,\ \ \left(\frac34\right)^{3}V,\ \dots$$

    that is,

    $$V,\ \ \frac34V,\ \ \frac{9}{16}V,\ \ \frac{27}{64}V,\ \dots$$ $$\begin{aligned}a_2-a_1&=\frac34V-V=-\frac{V}{4}\\ a_3-a_2&=\frac9{16}V-\frac34V=-\frac{3V}{16}\end{aligned}$$

    Since \(-\dfrac{V}{4}\ne-\dfrac{3V}{16}\), the differences are not the same.

    No, it is not an AP.

    The pump removes a fixed fraction, not a fixed amount. Each term is the previous one multiplied by \(\tfrac34\), and multiplying by a fixed number gives a geometric progression, never an AP (unless \(d=0\)).

    (iii)

    Cost of the 1st metre = ₹ 150, of the 2nd metre = ₹ 200, of the 3rd metre = ₹ 250, and so on:

    150, 200, 250, 300, …

    $$\begin{aligned}a_2-a_1&=200-150=50\\ a_3-a_2&=250-200=50\\ a_4-a_3&=300-250=50\end{aligned}$$

    The same ₹ 50 is added each time.

    Yes, it is an AP with \(a=150\) and \(d=50\).

    (iv)

    Under compound interest the amount after each year is the previous amount multiplied by \(\left(1+\dfrac{8}{100}\right)\). Writing \(A_k\) for the amount at the end of the \(k\)th year,

    $$\begin{aligned}A_1&=10000\left(1+\tfrac{8}{100}\right)\\ A_2&=10000\left(1+\tfrac{8}{100}\right)^{2}\\ A_3&=10000\left(1+\tfrac{8}{100}\right)^{3}\end{aligned}$$

    In rupees these are 10800, 11664, 12597.12, …

    $$\begin{aligned}A_2-A_1&=11664-10800\\ &=864\\ A_3-A_2&=12597.12-11664\\ &=933.12\end{aligned}$$

    \(864\ne933.12\), so the differences are not the same.

    No, it is not an AP.

    Interest is charged on a bigger and bigger amount each year, so the yearly increase itself keeps growing. Simple interest would have given an AP; compound interest never does.

    (i) AP   (ii) not an AP   (iii) AP   (iv) not an AP

    Q2

    The first term \(a\) and the common difference \(d\) of an AP are given below. Write down the first four terms in each case.

    1. \(a=10,\ d=10\)
    2. \(a=-2,\ d=0\)
    3. \(a=4,\ d=-3\)
    4. \(a=-1,\ d=\dfrac12\)
    5. \(a=-1.25,\ d=-0.25\)
    Solution

    Method: the first term is \(a\) itself; after that keep adding \(d\).

    $$a_1=a,\qquad a_2=a+d$$ $$a_3=a+2d,\qquad a_4=a+3d$$
    (i)

    \(a=10,\ d=10\):  \(10,\ 10+10,\ 20+10,\ 30+10\)

    10, 20, 30, 40

    (ii)

    \(a=-2,\ d=0\): adding 0 changes nothing, so every term is \(-2\).

    \(-2,\ -2,\ -2,\ -2\)

    A constant list is a perfectly good AP — the definition allows \(d=0\).

    (iii)

    \(a=4,\ d=-3\):  \(4,\ 4-3,\ 1-3,\ -2-3\)

    \(4,\ 1,\ -2,\ -5\)

    (iv)

    \(a=-1,\ d=\dfrac12\):

    $$\begin{aligned}a_2&=-1+\tfrac12=-\tfrac12\\ a_3&=-\tfrac12+\tfrac12=0\\ a_4&=0+\tfrac12=\tfrac12\end{aligned}$$

    \(-1,\ -\dfrac12,\ 0,\ \dfrac12\)

    (v)

    \(a=-1.25,\ d=-0.25\): each term is 0.25 less than the one before it.

    $$-1.25,\quad -1.50,\quad -1.75,\quad -2.00$$

    \(-1.25,\ -1.50,\ -1.75,\ -2.00\)

    Because \(d\) is negative the terms go down, and \(-2.00\) is smaller than \(-1.75\).

    Q3

    For each AP below, write down the first term \(a\) and the common difference \(d\).

    1. \(3,\ 1,\ -1,\ -3,\ \dots\)
    2. \(-5,\ -1,\ 3,\ 7,\ \dots\)
    3. \(\dfrac13,\ \dfrac53,\ \dfrac93,\ \dfrac{13}3,\ \dots\)
    4. \(0.6,\ 1.7,\ 2.8,\ 3.9,\ \dots\)
    Solution

    Method: \(a\) is simply the first number written. For \(d\), subtract the first term from the second: \(d=a_2-a_1\). One subtraction is enough once the list is known to be an AP, but a second one is a free check.

    (i)

    \(a=3\) and \(d=a_2-a_1=1-3=-2\).

    Check: \(a_3-a_2=-1-1=-2\) ✓  and  \(a_4-a_3=-3-(-1)=-2\) ✓

    \(a=3,\ d=-2\)

    (ii)

    \(a=-5\) and \(d=-1-(-5)=-1+5=4\).

    Check: \(3-(-1)=4\) ✓  and  \(7-3=4\) ✓

    \(a=-5,\ d=4\)

    (iii)

    \(a=\dfrac13\) and \(d=\dfrac53-\dfrac13=\dfrac{5-1}{3}=\dfrac43\).

    Check: \(\dfrac93-\dfrac53=\dfrac43\) ✓  and  \(\dfrac{13}3-\dfrac93=\dfrac43\) ✓

    \(a=\dfrac13,\ d=\dfrac43\)

    Do not cancel \(\dfrac93\) to 3 while you are working — keeping the same denominator 3 for every term makes the subtraction a one-step job.

    (iv)

    \(a=0.6\) and \(d=1.7-0.6=1.1\).

    Check: \(2.8-1.7=1.1\) ✓  and  \(3.9-2.8=1.1\) ✓

    \(a=0.6,\ d=1.1\)

    (i) \(a=3,\ d=-2\)   (ii) \(a=-5,\ d=4\)   (iii) \(a=\dfrac13,\ d=\dfrac43\)   (iv) \(a=0.6,\ d=1.1\)

    Q4

    Which of the lists below are APs? For every list that is an AP, write its common difference \(d\) and the next three terms.

    1. \(2,\ 4,\ 8,\ 16,\ \dots\)
    2. \(2,\ \dfrac52,\ 3,\ \dfrac72,\ \dots\)
    3. \(-1.2,\ -3.2,\ -5.2,\ -7.2,\ \dots\)
    4. \(-10,\ -6,\ -2,\ 2,\ \dots\)
    5. \(3,\ 3+\sqrt2,\ 3+2\sqrt2,\ 3+3\sqrt2,\ \dots\)
    6. \(0.2,\ 0.22,\ 0.222,\ 0.2222,\ \dots\)
    7. \(0,\ -4,\ -8,\ -12,\ \dots\)
    8. \(-\dfrac12,\ -\dfrac12,\ -\dfrac12,\ -\dfrac12,\ \dots\)
    9. \(1,\ 3,\ 9,\ 27,\ \dots\)
    10. \(a,\ 2a,\ 3a,\ 4a,\ \dots\)
    11. \(a,\ a^2,\ a^3,\ a^4,\ \dots\)
    12. \(\sqrt2,\ \sqrt8,\ \sqrt{18},\ \sqrt{32},\ \dots\)
    13. \(\sqrt3,\ \sqrt6,\ \sqrt9,\ \sqrt{12},\ \dots\)
    14. \(1^2,\ 3^2,\ 5^2,\ 7^2,\ \dots\)
    15. \(1^2,\ 5^2,\ 7^2,\ 73,\ \dots\)
    Solution

    Method: compute \(a_2-a_1\), \(a_3-a_2\) and \(a_4-a_3\). All three equal \(\Rightarrow\) AP with that value as \(d\); then the next three terms are found by adding \(d\) to the last given term, three times over.

    (i)

    \(a_2-a_1=4-2=2\),  \(a_3-a_2=8-4=4\).

    Already \(2\ne4\), so there is nothing more to check.

    Not an AP

    (ii)
    $$\begin{aligned}a_2-a_1&=\tfrac52-2=\tfrac12\\ a_3-a_2&=3-\tfrac52=\tfrac12\\ a_4-a_3&=\tfrac72-3=\tfrac12\end{aligned}$$

    All equal, so it is an AP with \(d=\dfrac12\). Next three terms:

    \(\dfrac72+\dfrac12=4,\qquad 4+\dfrac12=\dfrac92,\qquad \dfrac92+\dfrac12=5\)

    AP, \(d=\dfrac12\); next terms \(4,\ \dfrac92,\ 5\)

    Exercise 5.2

    20 questions · the \(n\)th term \(a_n=a+(n-1)d\) and everything you can find with it

    The \(n\)th term of an AP
    $$a_n=a+(n-1)d$$

    \(a\) = first term, \(d\) = common difference, \(n\) = position of the term, \(a_n\) = the term sitting at that position. Four quantities — if you know any three of them, the equation gives you the fourth. That single sentence solves all twenty questions in this exercise.

    The one mistake that costs the most marks here: writing \(a_{30}=a+30d\) instead of \(a+29d\). Count the jumps, not the terms.

    Q1

    In the table below, \(a\) is the first term of an AP, \(d\) its common difference and \(a_n\) its \(n\)th term. Fill in the blank in each row.

    \(a\)\(d\)\(n\)\(a_n\)
    (i)738…
    (ii)\(-18\)…100
    (iii)…\(-3\)18\(-5\)
    (iv)\(-18.9\)2.5…3.6
    (v)3.50105…
    Solution

    Every row uses the one formula \(a_n=a+(n-1)d\). Put in the three numbers you are given and solve for the missing one.

    (i)

    Given \(a=7,\ d=3,\ n=8\); find \(a_8\).

    $$\begin{aligned}a_8&=7+(8-1)\times3\\ &=7+7\times3\\ &=7+21=28\end{aligned}$$

    Check by writing the AP out: 7, 10, 13, 16, 19, 22, 25, 28 — the 8th term is 28 ✓

    \(a_n=28\)

    (ii)

    Given \(a=-18,\ n=10,\ a_{10}=0\); find \(d\).

    $$\begin{aligned}0&=-18+(10-1)d\\ 0&=-18+9d\\ 9d&=18\\ d&=2\end{aligned}$$

    Check: \(-18+9\times2=-18+18=0\) ✓

    \(d=2\)

    (iii)

    Given \(d=-3,\ n=18,\ a_{18}=-5\); find \(a\).

    $$\begin{aligned}-5&=a+(18-1)(-3)\\ -5&=a-51\\ a&=-5+51=46\end{aligned}$$

    Check: \(46+17\times(-3)=46-51=-5\) ✓

    \(a=46\)

    (iv)

    Given \(a=-18.9,\ d=2.5,\ a_n=3.6\); find \(n\).

    $$\begin{aligned}-18.9+(n-1)(2.5)&=3.6\\ (n-1)(2.5)&=22.5\\ n-1&=9\\ n&=10\end{aligned}$$

    because \(3.6+18.9=22.5\) and \(22.5\div2.5=9\).

    Check: \(-18.9+9\times2.5=-18.9+22.5=3.6\) ✓

    \(n=10\)

    (v)

    Given \(a=3.5,\ d=0,\ n=105\); find \(a_{105}\).

    $$\begin{aligned}a_{105}&=3.5+(105-1)\times0\\ &=3.5+0=3.5\end{aligned}$$

    \(a_n=3.5\)

    With \(d=0\) every term of the AP is 3.5, so the position \(n\) makes no difference at all.

    Pick the correct option and justify your choice.

    1. The 30th term of the AP \(10,\ 7,\ 4,\ \dots\) is   (A) 97   (B) 77   (C) \(-77\)   (D) \(-87\)
    2. The 11th term of the AP \(-3,\ -\dfrac12,\ 2,\ \dots\) is   (A) 28   (B) 22   (C) \(-38\)   (D) \(-48\dfrac12\)

    (i)

    For the AP \(10,\ 7,\ 4,\ \dots\):  \(a=10\) and \(d=7-10=-3\).

    $$\begin{aligned}a_{30}&=a+(30-1)d\\ &=10+29\times(-3)\\ &=10-87=-77\end{aligned}$$

    (C) \(-77\)

    The wrong options are the usual slips: (A) 97 comes from taking \(d=+3\); (D) \(-87\) comes from writing only \(29d\) and forgetting to add \(a\).

    (ii)

    For the AP \(-3,\ -\dfrac12,\ 2,\ \dots\):  \(a=-3\) and

    $$d=-\frac12-(-3)=-\frac12+3=\frac52$$

    Check \(d\): \(2-\left(-\dfrac12\right)=\dfrac52\) ✓

    (B) 22

    Q3

    Each list below is an AP with some of its terms hidden in boxes. Find the terms in the boxes.

    1. \(2,\ \boxed{\phantom{000}},\ 26\)
    2. \(\boxed{\phantom{000}},\ 13,\ \boxed{\phantom{000}},\ 3\)
    3. \(5,\ \boxed{\phantom{000}},\ \boxed{\phantom{000}},\ 9\tfrac12\)
    4. \(-4,\ \boxed{\phantom{000}},\ \boxed{\phantom{000}},\ \boxed{\phantom{000}},\ \boxed{\phantom{000}},\ 6\)
    5. \(\boxed{\phantom{000}},\ 38,\ \boxed{\phantom{000}},\ \boxed{\phantom{000}},\ \boxed{\phantom{000}},\ -22\)
    Solution

    Method: take the two terms whose values you are given. Count how many jumps of \(d\) lie between them; their difference is that many \(d\)'s. That gives \(d\), and then every box can be filled by adding \(d\) step by step.

    (i)

    Three terms: \(a_1=2\) and \(a_3=26\), with one box in between. From the 1st to the 3rd term is 2 jumps.

    $$\begin{aligned}a_3&=a+2d\\ 26&=2+2d\\ 2d&=24\\ d&=12\end{aligned}$$

    So the box is \(a_2=2+12=14\).

    Check: 2, 14, 26 — the jumps are 12 and 12 ✓

    The missing term is 14.

    Quick way for a single middle box: the middle term of three terms in AP is the average of the outer two, \(\dfrac{2+26}{2}=14\).

    (ii)

    Four terms with \(a_2=13\) and \(a_4=3\).

    $$\begin{aligned}a+d&=13 &&\text{…(1)}\\ a+3d&=3 &&\text{…(2)}\end{aligned}$$

    Subtract (1) from (2):

    $$\begin{aligned}2d&=3-13=-10\\ d&=-5\end{aligned}$$

    From (1): \(a=13-d=13-(-5)=18\). Then \(a_3=13+(-5)=8\).

    Check: 18, 13, 8, 3 — each jump is \(-5\) ✓

    The boxes are 18 and 8.

    (iii)

    Four terms with \(a=5\) and \(a_4=9\tfrac12=\dfrac{19}{2}\). From the 1st to the 4th term is 3 jumps.

    $$\begin{aligned}a+3d&=\frac{19}{2}\\ 5+3d&=\frac{19}{2}\\ 3d&=\frac{19}{2}-5=\frac{9}{2}\\ d&=\frac{9}{2}\times\frac13=\frac32\end{aligned}$$

    So \(a_2=5+\dfrac32=\dfrac{13}{2}=6\tfrac12\)  and  \(a_3=\dfrac{13}{2}+\dfrac32=\dfrac{16}{2}=8\).

    Check: \(5,\ 6\tfrac12,\ 8,\ 9\tfrac12\) — each jump is \(1\tfrac12\) ✓

    The boxes are \(6\tfrac12\) and 8.

    (iv)

    Six terms with \(a=-4\) and \(a_6=6\). From the 1st to the 6th term is 5 jumps.

    $$\begin{aligned}a+5d&=6\\ -4+5d&=6\\ 5d&=10\\ d&=2\end{aligned}$$

    Adding 2 each time from \(-4\):  \(-2,\ 0,\ 2,\ 4\).

    Check: \(-4,\ -2,\ 0,\ 2,\ 4,\ 6\) ✓

    The boxes are \(-2,\ 0,\ 2,\ 4\).

    (v)

    Six terms with \(a_2=38\) and \(a_6=-22\). From the 2nd to the 6th term is \(6-2=4\) jumps.

    $$\begin{aligned}4d&=a_6-a_2\\ 4d&=-22-38=-60\\ d&=-15\end{aligned}$$

    Going one jump backwards from \(a_2\):  \(a=38-(-15)=53\).

    Going forwards from 38:  \(a_3=23,\ a_4=8,\ a_5=-7\).

    Check: \(53,\ 38,\ 23,\ 8,\ -7,\ -22\) — each jump is \(-15\) ✓

    The boxes are \(53,\ 23,\ 8,\ -7\).

    (i) 14   (ii) 18, 8   (iii) \(6\tfrac12\), 8   (iv) \(-2,\ 0,\ 2,\ 4\)   (v) \(53,\ 23,\ 8,\ -7\)

    Q4

    Which term of the AP \(3,\ 8,\ 13,\ 18,\ \dots\) is 78?

    Solution

    Given: the AP \(3,\ 8,\ 13,\ 18,\ \dots\) and a term whose value is 78. To find: the position \(n\) of that term.

    Here \(a=3\) and \(d=8-3=5\). Let the 78 be the \(n\)th term, so \(a_n=78\).

    $$\begin{aligned}a+(n-1)d&=a_n\\ 3+(n-1)5&=78\\ (n-1)5&=75\\ n-1&=15\\ n&=16\end{aligned}$$

    Check: \(a_{16}=3+15\times5=3+75=78\) ✓

    78 is the 16th term of the AP.

    Find how many terms each of these APs has.

    1. \(7,\ 13,\ 19,\ \dots,\ 205\)
    2. \(18,\ 15\tfrac12,\ 13,\ \dots,\ -47\)
    Solution

    Idea: both APs are finite, so the number written at the end is the last term \(l=a_n\). Solving \(a+(n-1)d=l\) for \(n\) counts the terms.

    (i)

    \(a=7,\quad d=13-7=6,\quad a_n=205\).

    $$\begin{aligned}7+(n-1)6&=205\\ (n-1)6&=198\\ n-1&=33\\ n&=34\end{aligned}$$

    Check: \(7+33\times6=7+198=205\) ✓

    The AP has 34 terms.

    (ii)

    \(a=18\) and \(d=15\tfrac12-18=\dfrac{31}{2}-18=\dfrac{31-36}{2}=-\dfrac52\), with \(a_n=-47\).

    because \(-65\div\left(-\tfrac52\right)=-65\times\left(-\tfrac25\right)=26\).

    Check: \(18+26\times\left(-\dfrac52\right)=18-65=-47\) ✓

    The AP has 27 terms.

    (i) 34 terms   (ii) 27 terms

    Q6

    Is \(-150\) a term of the AP \(11,\ 8,\ 5,\ 2,\ \dots\)? Check.

    Solution

    Method: assume \(-150\) is a term, say the \(n\)th one, and solve for \(n\). A term can only sit at a whole-number position, so if \(n\) does not come out as a positive whole number, the assumption was wrong.

    Here \(a=11\) and \(d=8-11=-3\).

    $$\begin{aligned}11+(n-1)(-3)&=-150\\ (n-1)(-3)&=-161\\ n-1&=\frac{-161}{-3}=\frac{161}{3}\\ n&=1+\frac{161}{3}=\frac{164}{3}\end{aligned}$$

    So \(n=\dfrac{164}{3}=54\tfrac23\).

    \(n\) is not a whole number, so no term of this AP can equal \(-150\).

    No — \(-150\) is not a term of this AP.

    A second way to see it. The terms are \(11-3(n-1)=14-3n\). Divide any term by 3 and the remainder is always 2 (11, 8, 5, 2, \(-1\), \(-4\), … ). But \(-150=3\times(-50)\) leaves remainder 0, so it can never appear in this list.

    Q7

    In an AP the 11th term is 38 and the 16th term is 73. Find its 31st term.

    Solution

    Given: \(a_{11}=38\) and \(a_{16}=73\). To find: \(a_{31}\).

    Write both facts with the formula \(a_n=a+(n-1)d\):

    $$\begin{aligned}a+10d&=38 &&\text{…(1)}\\ a+15d&=73 &&\text{…(2)}\end{aligned}$$

    Subtract (1) from (2) — this wipes out \(a\):

    $$\begin{aligned}5d&=73-38=35\\ d&=7\end{aligned}$$

    Put \(d=7\) back into (1):  \(a+70=38\), so \(a=-32\).

    $$\begin{aligned}a_{31}&=a+30d\\ &=-32+30\times7\\ &=-32+210=178\end{aligned}$$

    Check: \(a_{16}=-32+15\times7=-32+105=73\) ✓

    The 31st term is 178.

    Faster route. From the 11th term to the 16th term is \(16-11=5\) jumps, so \(5d=73-38=35\) and \(d=7\) straight away. Then from the 16th to the 31st is 15 jumps: \(a_{31}=73+15\times7=73+105=178\) — without ever finding \(a\).

    An AP has 50 terms. Its 3rd term is 12 and its last term is 106. Find the 29th term.

    Given: \(n=50\) terms in all, \(a_3=12\), last term \(a_{50}=106\). To find: \(a_{29}\).

    $$\begin{aligned}a+2d&=12 &&\text{…(1)}\\ a+49d&=106 &&\text{…(2)}\end{aligned}$$

    $$\begin{aligned}47d&=106-12=94\\ d&=2\end{aligned}$$

    From (1):  \(a+4=12\), so \(a=8\).

    $$\begin{aligned}a_{29}&=a+28d\\ &=8+28\times2\\ &=8+56=64\end{aligned}$$

    Check: \(a_{50}=8+49\times2=8+98=106\) ✓  and  \(a_3=8+2\times2=12\) ✓

    The 29th term is 64.

    Q9

    The 3rd term of an AP is 4 and its 9th term is \(-8\). Which term of this AP is 0?

    Solution

    Given: \(a_3=4\) and \(a_9=-8\). To find: the value of \(n\) for which \(a_n=0\).

    $$\begin{aligned}a+2d&=4 &&\text{…(1)}\\ a+8d&=-8 &&\text{…(2)}\end{aligned}$$

    Subtract (1) from (2):

    $$\begin{aligned}6d&=-8-4=-12\\ d&=-2\end{aligned}$$

    From (1):  \(a-4=4\), so \(a=8\).

    Now set the \(n\)th term equal to 0:

    $$\begin{aligned}8+(n-1)(-2)&=0\\ (n-1)(-2)&=-8\\ n-1&=4\\ n&=5\end{aligned}$$

    Check by listing: 8, 6, 4, 2, 0, \(-2\), … — the 3rd term is 4 ✓, the 9th is \(8+8(-2)=-8\) ✓, and the 5th is 0 ✓

    Q10

    The 17th term of an AP is 7 more than its 10th term. Find the common difference.

    Solution

    Given: \(a_{17}-a_{10}=7\). To find: \(d\).

    $$\begin{aligned}a_{17}&=a+16d\\ a_{10}&=a+9d\end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned}a_{17}-a_{10}&=(a+16d)-(a+9d)\\ &=7d\end{aligned}$$

    So \(7d=7\), giving \(d=1\).

    The common difference is \(d=1\).

    Notice that \(a\) cancelled by itself — the gap between the 10th and the 17th term is just \(17-10=7\) jumps of \(d\), whatever the first term happens to be. The answer would be the same for every AP with \(d=1\).

    Q11

    Which term of the AP \(3,\ 15,\ 27,\ 39,\ \dots\) is 132 more than its 54th term?

    Solution

    Given: the AP \(3,\ 15,\ 27,\ 39,\ \dots\). To find: the position \(n\) for which \(a_n=a_{54}+132\).

    Here \(a=3\) and \(d=15-3=12\).

    Step 1 — the 54th term.

    $$\begin{aligned}a_{54}&=3+(54-1)\times12\\ &=3+53\times12\\ &=3+636=639\end{aligned}$$

    Step 2 — the term we want. It must equal \(639+132=771\).

    $$\begin{aligned}3+(n-1)12&=771\\ (n-1)12&=768\\ n-1&=64\\ n&=65\end{aligned}$$

    Check: \(a_{65}=3+64\times12=3+768=771\), and \(771-639=132\) ✓

    The 65th term is 132 more than the 54th term.

    Shortcut. From the 54th term to the \(n\)th term is \((n-54)\) jumps, so \((n-54)\times12=132\), giving \(n-54=11\) and \(n=65\) in one line.

    Q12

    Two APs have the same common difference. Their 100th terms differ by 100. By how much do their 1000th terms differ?

    Solution

    Given: two APs with the same \(d\), and \(a_{100}-b_{100}=100\). To find: \(a_{1000}-b_{1000}\).

    Let the first AP have first term \(a\) and the second have first term \(b\); both have common difference \(d\).

    $$\begin{aligned}a_{100}-b_{100}&=\left[a+99d\right]-\left[b+99d\right]\\ &=a-b\end{aligned}$$

    So \(a-b=100\). Now do exactly the same at the 1000th place:

    $$\begin{aligned}a_{1000}-b_{1000}&=\left[a+999d\right]-\left[b+999d\right]\\ &=a-b\\ &=100\end{aligned}$$

    Their 1000th terms also differ by 100.

    Because the two APs climb at the same rate, the gap between them never changes. The difference between corresponding terms is \(a-b\) at every position — the 100, the 1000 and the \(n\) in the question are there only to test whether you notice this.

    Q13

    How many three-digit numbers are divisible by 7?

    Solution

    Idea: the multiples of 7 form an AP with \(d=7\). Find the first and the last one that is a three-digit number, then count the terms.

    First three-digit multiple of 7. \(100\div7=14\) remainder 2, so 100 is not a multiple. The next multiple is \(15\times7=105\).

    Last three-digit multiple of 7. \(999\div7=142\) remainder 5, so the largest one is \(142\times7=994\).

    So the numbers we are counting are

    $$105,\ 112,\ 119,\ \dots,\ 994$$

    an AP with \(a=105,\ d=7,\ a_n=994\).

    $$\begin{aligned}105+(n-1)7&=994\\ (n-1)7&=889\\ n-1&=127\\ n&=128\end{aligned}$$

    Check: \(105+127\times7=105+889=994\) ✓

    There are 128 three-digit numbers divisible by 7.

    A quicker count of the same thing: multiples of 7 up to 999 number \(142\), and those up to 99 number \(14\);  \(142-14=128\).

    Q14

    Q16

    Find the AP whose 3rd term is 16 and whose 7th term is 12 more than its 5th term.

    Solution

    Given: \(a_3=16\) and \(a_7-a_5=12\). To find: the AP, that is, \(a\) and \(d\).

    Step 1 — use the second fact first, because \(a\) cancels out of it:

    Step 2 — now use the first fact.

    $$\begin{aligned}a+2d&=16\\ a+12&=16\\ a&=4\end{aligned}$$

    With \(a=4\) and \(d=6\), the AP is

    $$4,\ 10,\ 16,\ 22,\ 28,\ \dots$$

    Check: the 3rd term is 16 ✓; \(a_7=4+36=40\) and \(a_5=4+24=28\), and \(40-28=12\) ✓

    The AP is \(4,\ 10,\ 16,\ 22,\ \dots\)

    Q17

    Find the 20th term counted from the last term of the AP \(3,\ 8,\ 13,\ \dots,\ 253\).

    Solution

    Given: the finite AP \(3,\ 8,\ 13,\ \dots,\ 253\). To find: the 20th term when you start counting from the last term.

    Method 1 — turn the AP around. Reading from the right, the first term is \(253\) and each step goes down by 5, so the common difference becomes \(-5\).

    $$\begin{aligned}&\text{20th term from the end}\\ &\quad=253+(20-1)(-5)\\ &\quad=253-95=158\end{aligned}$$

    Method 2 — count from the front instead. First find how many terms there are. With \(a=3,\ d=5,\ a_n=253\):

    $$\begin{aligned}3+(n-1)5&=253\\ (n-1)5&=250\\ n-1&=50\\ n&=51\end{aligned}$$

    The 1st term from the end is the 51st from the start, the 2nd from the end is the 50th, and in general the \(k\)th from the end is the \((51-k+1)\)th from the start. For \(k=20\) that is the 32nd term:

    $$a_{32}=3+31\times5=3+155=158$$

    Both methods give the same number ✓

    The 20th term from the last term is 158.

    In general, the \(n\)th term from the end of a finite AP is \(l-(n-1)d\), where \(l\) is the last term. Method 1 is this formula.

    Q18

    In an AP, the 4th and 8th terms add up to 24, and the 6th and 10th terms add up to 44. Find the first three terms of the AP.

    Solution

    Given: \(a_4+a_8=24\) and \(a_6+a_{10}=44\). To find: \(a_1,\ a_2,\ a_3\).

    Write every term with \(a\) and \(d\):

    $$\begin{aligned}a_4+a_8&=(a+3d)+(a+7d)\\ &=2a+10d\\ a_6+a_{10}&=(a+5d)+(a+9d)\\ &=2a+14d\end{aligned}$$

    So the two conditions become

    $$\begin{aligned}2a+10d&=24\\ 2a+14d&=44\end{aligned}$$

    Halving each equation:

    $$\begin{aligned}a+5d&=12 &&\text{…(1)}\\ a+7d&=22 &&\text{…(2)}\end{aligned}$$

    Subtract (1) from (2):

    $$\begin{aligned}2d&=22-12=10\\ d&=5\end{aligned}$$

    From (1):  \(a+25=12\), so \(a=-13\).

    First three terms:  \(a=-13\),  \(a+d=-8\),  \(a+2d=-3\).

    Check: \(a_4=-13+15=2\) and \(a_8=-13+35=22\), and \(2+22=24\) ✓   \(a_6=-13+25=12\) and \(a_{10}=-13+45=32\), and \(12+32=44\) ✓

    The first three terms are \(-13,\ -8,\ -3\).

    Q19

    Subba Rao began working in 1995 on an annual salary of ₹ 5000, and his salary rose by ₹ 200 every year. In which year did his salary reach ₹ 7000?

    Solution

    Given: salary in the 1st year (1995) = ₹ 5000, rising by ₹ 200 a year. To find: the year in which the salary is ₹ 7000.

    The yearly salaries in rupees are

    $$5000,\ 5200,\ 5400,\ 5600,\ \dots$$

    an AP with \(a=5000\) and \(d=200\). Let the salary be ₹ 7000 in the \(n\)th year of service.

    $$\begin{aligned}5000+(n-1)200&=7000\\ (n-1)200&=2000\\ n-1&=10\\ n&=11\end{aligned}$$

    So it is his 11th year of work. The 1st year was 1995, so the 11th year is

    $$1995+(11-1)=1995+10=2005$$

    Check: in 2005 he has had 10 increments, so his salary is \(5000+10\times200=7000\) ✓

    His salary reached ₹ 7000 in his 11th year of service, that is, in the year 2005.

    This is the same \(n-1\) trap as in the formula itself: the 11th year is ten years after 1995, not eleven.

    Q20

    Ramkali saved ₹ 5 in the first week of a year, and after that she increased her weekly saving by ₹ 1.75 every week. Her saving in the \(n\)th week was ₹ 20.75. Find \(n\).

    Solution

    Given: first week's saving \(a=5\), weekly increase \(d=1.75\), saving in the \(n\)th week \(a_n=20.75\) — all in rupees. To find: \(n\).

    The weekly savings in rupees are \(5,\ 6.75,\ 8.50,\ \dots\) — an AP.

    $$\begin{aligned}a+(n-1)d&=a_n\\ 5+(n-1)(1.75)&=20.75\\ (n-1)(1.75)&=15.75\\ n-1&=\frac{15.75}{1.75}=9\\ n&=10\end{aligned}$$

    Check: \(5+9\times1.75=5+15.75=20.75\) ✓

    \(n=10\) — she saved ₹ 20.75 in the 10th week.

    Exercise 5.3

    20 questions · the sum of the first n terms of an AP

    Every question in this exercise is one of two formulas in disguise. Write down what you are given, decide which of the two fits, substitute, and simplify. The four letters \(a,\ d,\ n,\ S_n\) are tied together by one equation — know any three and the fourth comes out.

    Common mistake: writing \(S_n=\dfrac n2[2a+nd]\). The bracket carries \((n-1)d\), not \(nd\) — the same \((n-1)\) as in \(a_n=a+(n-1)d\). Check it on a tiny case: for \(n=1\) the formula must give \(S_1=a\).

    Q1

    Find the sum of each of these APs, up to the number of terms stated:

    1. 2, 7, 12, … to 10 terms
    2. −37, −33, −29, … to 12 terms
    3. 0.6, 1.7, 2.8, … to 100 terms
    4. \(\tfrac1{15},\ \tfrac1{12},\ \tfrac1{10},\ \ldots\) to 11 terms

    Formula used throughout: \(S_n=\dfrac n2\bigl[2a+(n-1)d\bigr]\), where \(a\) is the first term and \(d\) the common difference.

    (i)

    Here \(a=2\), \(d=7-2=5\), \(n=10\).

    $$\begin{aligned}S_{10}&=\frac{10}{2}\bigl[2(2)+(10-1)(5)\bigr]\\ &=5\,[\,4+45\,]\\ &=5\times49=245\end{aligned}$$

    \(S_{10}=245\)

    (ii)

    Here \(a=-37\), \(d=-33-(-37)=4\), \(n=12\).

    $$\begin{aligned}S_{12}&=\frac{12}{2}\bigl[2(-37)+(12-1)(4)\bigr]\\ &=6\,[-74+44\,]\\ &=6\times(-30)=-180\end{aligned}$$

    \(S_{12}=-180\)

    (iii)

    Here \(a=0.6\), \(d=1.7-0.6=1.1\), \(n=100\), so \(n-1=99\).

    $$\begin{aligned}S_{100}&=\frac{100}{2}\bigl[2(0.6)+99(1.1)\bigr]\\ &=50\,[\,1.2+108.9\,]\\ &=50\times110.1\\ &=5505\end{aligned}$$

    \(S_{100}=5505\)

    (iv)

    Here \(a=\dfrac1{15}\), and

    $$d=\frac1{12}-\frac1{15}=\frac{5-4}{60}=\frac1{60}.$$

    Check with the next pair: \(\dfrac1{10}-\dfrac1{12}=\dfrac{6-5}{60}=\dfrac1{60}\). The difference is the same, so the list really is an AP.

    With \(n=11\) we have \(2a=\tfrac2{15}\) and \(n-1=10\):

    $$\begin{aligned}S_{11}&=\frac{11}{2}\left[\frac2{15}+10\left(\frac1{60}\right)\right]\\ &=\frac{11}{2}\left[\frac2{15}+\frac16\right]\\ &=\frac{11}{2}\left[\frac{4}{30}+\frac{5}{30}\right]\\ &=\frac{11}{2}\times\frac{9}{30}=\frac{11}{2}\times\frac{3}{10}\\ &=\frac{33}{20}\end{aligned}$$

    \(S_{11}=\dfrac{33}{20}\)

    Q2

    Find each of these sums:

    1. \(7+10\tfrac12+14+\cdots+84\)
    2. \(34+32+30+\cdots+10\)
    3. \((-5)+(-8)+(-11)+\cdots+(-230)\)
    Solution

    In all three the last term is given but the number of terms is not. So first find \(n\) from \(a_n=a+(n-1)d=l\), then use \(S_n=\dfrac n2\,(a+l)\).

    (i)

    \(a=7\), \(\;d=10\tfrac12-7=\dfrac{21}{2}-7=\dfrac72\), \(\;l=84\).

    $$\begin{aligned}a+(n-1)d&=84\\ 7+(n-1)\tfrac72&=84\\ (n-1)\tfrac72&=77\\ n-1&=77\times\tfrac27=22\\ n&=23\end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned}S_{23}&=\frac{23}{2}\,(7+84)\\ &=\frac{23}{2}\times91\\ &=\frac{2093}{2}=1046\tfrac12\end{aligned}$$

    Sum \(=1046\dfrac12\)

    (ii)

    \(a=34\), \(\;d=32-34=-2\), \(\;l=10\).

    $$\begin{aligned}34+(n-1)(-2)&=10\\ -2(n-1)&=-24\\ n-1&=12\\ n&=13\end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned}S_{13}&=\frac{13}{2}\,(34+10)\\ &=\frac{13}{2}\times44\\ &=13\times22=286\end{aligned}$$

    Sum \(=286\)

    (iii)

    \(a=-5\), \(\;d=-8-(-5)=-3\), \(\;l=-230\).

    $$\begin{aligned}-5+(n-1)(-3)&=-230\\ -3(n-1)&=-225\\ n-1&=75\\ n&=76\end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned}S_{76}&=\frac{76}{2}\,(-5-230)\\ &=38\times(-235)\\ &=-8930\end{aligned}$$

    Sum \(=-8930\)

    Notice the pattern: a negative \(d\) with a negative last term still gives \(n\) positive — the two minus signs cancel when you divide.

    Q4

    How many terms of the AP 9, 17, 25, … must be added up to make a total of 636?

    Solution

    Given: \(a=9\), \(d=17-9=8\), \(S_n=636\).  To find: \(n\).

    $$\begin{aligned}\frac n2\bigl[2a+(n-1)d\bigr]&=636\\ \frac n2\bigl[18+8(n-1)\bigr]&=636\\ \frac n2\,(8n+10)&=636\\ n\,(4n+5)&=636\\ 4n^2+5n-636&=0\end{aligned}$$

    12 terms must be taken.

    Q9

    In an AP the first 7 terms add up to 49 and the first 17 terms add up to 289. Find the sum of its first \(n\) terms.

    Solution

    Given: \(S_7=49\) and \(S_{17}=289\).  To find: \(S_n\).

    Write both facts with \(S_n=\dfrac n2\bigl[2a+(n-1)d\bigr]\).

    $$\begin{aligned}\frac72\bigl[2a+6d\bigr]&=49\\ 7\,(a+3d)&=49\\ a+3d&=7\qquad\cdots(1)\end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned}\frac{17}{2}\bigl[2a+16d\bigr]&=289\\ 17\,(a+8d)&=289\\ a+8d&=17\qquad\cdots(2)\end{aligned}$$

    Subtracting (1) from (2):

    $$\begin{aligned}5d&=10\quad\Rightarrow\quad d=2\\ a&=7-3(2)=1\end{aligned}$$

    So the AP is 1, 3, 5, 7, … and

    $$\begin{aligned}S_n&=\frac n2\bigl[2(1)+(n-1)2\bigr]\\ &=\frac n2\,[\,2+2n-2\,]\\ &=\frac n2\times2n=n^2\end{aligned}$$

    \(S_n=n^2\)

    This is the familiar fact that the first \(n\) odd numbers add up to \(n^2\): \(1+3=4\), \(1+3+5=9\), and so on.

    Q10

    Show that the numbers \(a_1,\ a_2,\ \ldots,\ a_n,\ \ldots\) form an AP when the \(n\)th term is given by the rule below. In each case also find the sum of the first 15 terms.

    1. \(a_n=3+4n\)
    2. \(a_n=9-5n\)
    Solution

    Test for an AP: work out \(a_{n+1}-a_n\). If the answer is a constant — the same number whatever \(n\) is — the list is an AP, and that constant is \(d\).

    (i)

    \(a_n=3+4n\), so \(a_{n+1}=3+4(n+1)=7+4n\).

    $$\begin{aligned}a_{n+1}-a_n&=(7+4n)-(3+4n)\\ &=4\end{aligned}$$

    This is 4 for every \(n\), so the list is an AP with \(d=4\). Hence proved.

    First term: \(a_1=3+4(1)=7\). (The list is 7, 11, 15, 19, ….) With \(n=15\), \(n-1=14\):

    $$\begin{aligned}S_{15}&=\frac{15}{2}\bigl[2(7)+14(4)\bigr]\\ &=\frac{15}{2}\,[\,14+56\,]\\ &=\frac{15}{2}\times70\\ &=15\times35=525\end{aligned}$$

    AP with \(d=4\); \(S_{15}=525\)

    (ii)

    \(a_n=9-5n\), so \(a_{n+1}=9-5(n+1)=4-5n\).

    $$\begin{aligned}a_{n+1}-a_n&=(4-5n)-(9-5n)\\ &=-5\end{aligned}$$

    This is \(-5\) for every \(n\), so the list is an AP with \(d=-5\). Hence proved.

    First term: \(a_1=9-5(1)=4\). (The list is 4, −1, −6, −11, ….) With \(n=15\), \(n-1=14\):

    $$\begin{aligned}S_{15}&=\frac{15}{2}\bigl[2(4)+14(-5)\bigr]\\ &=\frac{15}{2}\,[\,8-70\,]\\ &=\frac{15}{2}\times(-62)\\ &=15\times(-31)=-465\end{aligned}$$

    AP with \(d=-5\); \(S_{15}=-465\)

    Checking only that \(a_2-a_1=a_3-a_2\) is not a proof — it tests two gaps out of infinitely many. Working out \(a_{n+1}-a_n\) in letters settles every gap at once, and that is what earns the marks.

    Q11

    For a certain AP the sum of the first \(n\) terms is \(4n-n^2\). Find:

    1. the first term, that is \(S_1\)
    2. the sum of the first two terms
    3. the second term
    4. the 3rd term
    5. the 10th term
    6. the \(n\)th term
    Solution

    Given: \(S_n=4n-n^2\).  Key idea: a single term is the difference of two sums, \(a_n=S_n-S_{n-1}\).

    (a) First term. \(S_1\) is the sum of just one term, so it is the first term:

    $$a=S_1=4(1)-1^2=4-1=3$$

    (b) Sum of the first two terms.

    $$S_2=4(2)-2^2=8-4=4$$

    (c) Second term.

    $$a_2=S_2-S_1=4-3=1$$

    (d) Third term. First \(S_3=4(3)-3^2=12-9=3\), so

    $$a_3=S_3-S_2=3-4=-1$$

    (e) Tenth term.

    $$\begin{aligned}S_{10}&=4(10)-10^2=40-100=-60\\ S_{9}&=4(9)-9^2=36-81=-45\\ a_{10}&=S_{10}-S_9=-60-(-45)=-15\end{aligned}$$

    (f) The \(n\)th term. Replace \(n\) by \(n-1\) in the given rule, then subtract:

    $$\begin{aligned}S_{n-1}&=4(n-1)-(n-1)^2\\ &=4n-4-(n^2-2n+1)\\ &=-n^2+6n-5\end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned}a_n&=S_n-S_{n-1}\\ &=(4n-n^2)-(-n^2+6n-5)\\ &=4n-n^2+n^2-6n+5\\ &=5-2n\end{aligned}$$

    \(a=S_1=3\); \(S_2=4\); \(a_2=1\); \(a_3=-1\); \(a_{10}=-15\); \(a_n=5-2n\)

    Check: \(a_n=5-2n\) gives \(a_1=3\), \(a_2=1\), \(a_3=-1\), \(a_{10}=-15\) — all matching. The AP is 3, 1, −1, −3, … with \(d=-2\).

    Q12

    Find the sum of the first 40 positive integers that are divisible by 6.

    Solution

    The positive integers divisible by 6 are 6, 12, 18, 24, … Each one is 6 more than the one before, so they form an AP.

    Given: \(a=6\), \(d=6\), \(n=40\).  To find: \(S_{40}\).

    $$\begin{aligned}S_{40}&=\frac{40}{2}\bigl[2(6)+(40-1)(6)\bigr]\\ &=20\,[\,12+234\,]\\ &=20\times246=4920\end{aligned}$$

    The sum is 4920.

    Shortcut: the 40th such number is \(6\times40=240\), so \(S_{40}=\tfrac{40}{2}(6+240)=20\times246=4920\) — the same answer.

    Q13

    Find the sum of the first 15 multiples of 8.

    Solution

    The multiples of 8 are 8, 16, 24, …, and the 15th of them is \(8\times15=120\).

    Given: \(a=8\), \(d=8\), \(n=15\), last term \(l=120\).

    $$\begin{aligned}S_{15}&=\frac{15}{2}\,(a+l)\\ &=\frac{15}{2}\,(8+120)\\ &=\frac{15}{2}\times128=15\times64=960\end{aligned}$$

    The sum is 960.

    Q14

    Find the sum of the odd numbers lying between 0 and 50.

    Solution

    The odd numbers between 0 and 50 are 1, 3, 5, …, 49 — an AP with \(a=1\), \(d=2\) and last term \(l=49\).

    First find how many there are:

    $$\begin{aligned}a+(n-1)d&=49\\ 1+(n-1)2&=49\\ 2\,(n-1)&=48\\ n-1&=24\quad\Rightarrow\quad n=25\end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned}S_{25}&=\frac{25}{2}\,(1+49)\\ &=\frac{25}{2}\times50=25\times25=625\end{aligned}$$

    The sum is 625.

    This agrees with Question 9: the first \(n\) odd numbers always add up to \(n^2\), and here \(25^2=625\).

    Q15

    A construction contract fixes a penalty for finishing late: ₹ 200 for the first day of delay, ₹ 250 for the second day, ₹ 300 for the third, and so on, each day's penalty being ₹ 50 more than the previous day's. How much must the contractor pay if the work is delayed by 30 days?

    Solution

    The daily penalties are 200, 250, 300, … rupees — an AP, because each day's penalty is ₹ 50 more than the day before.

    Given: \(a=200\), \(d=50\), \(n=30\).  To find: the total penalty \(S_{30}\).

    $$\begin{aligned}S_{30}&=\frac{30}{2}\bigl[2(200)+(30-1)(50)\bigr]\\ &=15\,[\,400+1450\,]\\ &=15\times1850=27750\end{aligned}$$

    The contractor has to pay ₹ 27750 as penalty.

    The penalty on the 30th day alone is \(a_{30}=200+29(50)=\) ₹ 1650 — a good reason not to be late.

    Q16

    A school sets aside ₹ 700 for seven cash prizes for overall academic performance. Each prize is ₹ 20 less than the prize just above it. Find the value of every prize.

    Solution

    Let the largest prize be ₹ \(a\). Going down the list, each prize is ₹ 20 less, so the seven prizes form an AP with \(d=-20\).

    Given: \(n=7\), \(d=-20\), \(S_7=700\).  To find: all seven prizes.

    $$\begin{aligned}\frac72\bigl[2a+6(-20)\bigr]&=700\\ \frac72\,(2a-120)&=700\\ 7\,(a-60)&=700\\ a-60&=100\\ a&=160\end{aligned}$$

    Now subtract 20 each time:

    $$160,\ 140,\ 120,\ 100,\ 80,\ 60,\ 40$$

    Check: \(160+140+120+100+80+60+40=700\) ✓

    The prizes are ₹ 160, ₹ 140, ₹ 120, ₹ 100, ₹ 80, ₹ 60 and ₹ 40.

    Q17

    To cut down air pollution, the students of a school decided to plant trees in and around the school. Each section would plant as many trees as the number of the class it belongs to: a section of Class I plants 1 tree, a section of Class II plants 2 trees, and so on up to Class XII. There are three sections in every class. How many trees will the students plant altogether?

    Solution

    One section of Class \(k\) plants \(k\) trees. Taking one section from each class, the numbers of trees are

    $$1,\ 2,\ 3,\ \ldots,\ 12$$

    which is an AP with \(a=1\), \(d=1\), \(n=12\) and last term \(l=12\).

    $$\begin{aligned}S_{12}&=\frac{12}{2}\,(1+12)\\ &=6\times13=78\end{aligned}$$

    So one section of each class plants 78 trees. There are three sections in every class, so

    $$\text{total trees}=3\times78=234$$

    The students will plant 234 trees.

    You may also add the class totals directly: 3, 6, 9, …, 36 is an AP with \(a=3,\ d=3,\ n=12\), and \(S_{12}=\tfrac{12}{2}(3+36)=6\times39=234\) — the same number.

    Q18

    A spiral is drawn from semicircles placed one after another. Their centres are taken alternately at A and at B, starting with centre A, and their radii are 0.5 cm, 1.0 cm, 1.5 cm, 2.0 cm, … as in the figure. What is the total length of such a spiral made of thirteen semicircles in a row? \(\left(\text{Take }\pi=\dfrac{22}{7}\right)\)

    Solution

    Key fact: a semicircle of radius \(r\) is half of a circle of circumference \(2\pi r\), so its length is \(\pi r\).

    The thirteen radii are

    $$r_1=0.5,\ \ r_2=1.0,\ \ r_3=1.5,\ \ \ldots,\ \ r_{13}$$

    an AP with \(a=0.5\) and \(d=0.5\), so

    $$\begin{aligned}r_{13}&=0.5+(13-1)(0.5)\\ &=0.5+6=6.5\ \text{cm}\end{aligned}$$

    The total length of the spiral is

    $$\begin{aligned}L&=l_1+l_2+\cdots+l_{13}\\ &=\pi r_1+\pi r_2+\cdots+\pi r_{13}\\ &=\pi\,(r_1+r_2+\cdots+r_{13})\end{aligned}$$

    The radii add up (as an AP with 13 terms) to

    $$\begin{aligned}r_1+\cdots+r_{13}&=\frac{13}{2}\,(0.5+6.5)\\ &=\frac{13}{2}\times7=\frac{91}{2}\ \text{cm}\end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned}L&=\frac{22}{7}\times\frac{91}{2}\\ &=\frac{22\times13}{2}=143\ \text{cm}\end{aligned}$$

    The spiral is 143 cm long.

    Taking \(\pi\) out as a common factor is what makes this easy — add the radii first, multiply by \(\pi\) once at the end.

    Q19

    200 logs are stacked so that the bottom row has 20 logs, the row above it 19, the next 18, and so on, each row holding one log less than the row below. In how many rows are the 200 logs stacked, and how many logs lie in the top row?

    Solution

    Counting from the bottom row upwards, the numbers of logs are 20, 19, 18, … — an AP with \(a=20\) and \(d=-1\). If there are \(n\) rows, all the logs together give \(S_n=200\).

    $$\begin{aligned}\frac n2\bigl[2(20)+(n-1)(-1)\bigr]&=200\\ \frac n2\,[\,40-n+1\,]&=200\\ n\,(41-n)&=400\\ 41n-n^2&=400\\ n^2-41n+400&=0\end{aligned}$$

    Two numbers with product 400 and sum 41 are 16 and 25:

    $$\begin{aligned}n^2-16n-25n+400&=0\\ n(n-16)-25(n-16)&=0\\ (n-16)(n-25)&=0\end{aligned}$$ $$n=16\qquad\text{or}\qquad n=25$$

    Both roots are positive whole numbers, so this time you cannot reject one just by looking at it. Test each against the situation — a row can never hold a negative number of logs.

    If \(n=25\):

    $$\begin{aligned}a_{25}&=20+(25-1)(-1)\\ &=20-24=-4\end{aligned}$$

    The 25th row would hold \(-4\) logs, which is impossible. So \(n=25\) is rejected.

    If \(n=16\):

    $$\begin{aligned}a_{16}&=20+(16-1)(-1)\\ &=20-15=5\end{aligned}$$

    Five logs in the top row — perfectly possible.

    Check: \(S_{16}=\dfrac{16}{2}(20+5)=8\times25=200\) ✓

    The logs are stacked in 16 rows, with 5 logs in the top row.

    Why did 25 turn up at all? Rows 21 to 25 would hold 0, −1, −2, −3, −4 logs; those negatives cancel the extra rows, so the algebra is happy even though the stack is not.

    Q20

    In a potato race a bucket is kept at the starting point. The first potato lies 5 m from the bucket and the remaining potatoes are placed 3 m apart from one another along a straight line; there are ten potatoes in all. A competitor starts at the bucket, runs to the nearest potato, brings it back and drops it in the bucket, then runs out for the next potato and brings it back, and so on until every potato is in the bucket. What total distance does the competitor run?

    Solution

    The potatoes stand at distances

    $$5,\ 8,\ 11,\ \ldots\ \text{metres}$$

    from the bucket. For each potato the runner goes out and comes back, so the distance run for a potato at \(x\) metres is \(2x\) metres.

    The distances run are therefore

    $$2(5),\ \ 2(8),\ \ 2(11),\ \ \ldots$$

    that is,

    $$10,\ \ 16,\ \ 22,\ \ \ldots$$

    which is an AP with

    $$a=10,\quad d=16-10=6,\quad n=10$$ $$\begin{aligned}S_{10}&=\frac{10}{2}\bigl[2(10)+(10-1)(6)\bigr]\\ &=5\,[\,20+54\,]\\ &=5\times74=370\end{aligned}$$

    The competitor runs 370 m in all.

    Check the last trip: the tenth potato is \(5+9(3)=32\) m away, so that trip is 64 m — and indeed \(a_{10}=10+9(6)=64\) ✓

    Q1

    Which term of the AP 121, 117, 113, … is the first one that is negative?

    Solution

    Given: \(a=121\), \(d=117-121=-4\).  To find: the smallest \(n\) for which \(a_n\lt0\).

    First write the \(n\)th term in a simple form:

    $$\begin{aligned}a_n&=a+(n-1)d\\ &=121+(n-1)(-4)\\ &=121-4n+4\\ &=125-4n\end{aligned}$$

    We want this to be negative:

    $$\begin{aligned}125-4n&\lt0\\ 125&\lt4n\\ n&\gt\frac{125}{4}=31.25\end{aligned}$$

    \(n\) must be a whole number, and the smallest whole number greater than 31.25 is 32.

    Check both sides of the boundary:

    $$\begin{aligned}a_{31}&=125-4(31)=125-124=1\\ a_{32}&=125-4(32)=125-128=-3\end{aligned}$$

    So the 31st term is still positive and the 32nd is the first negative one.

    The 32nd term is the first negative term; it equals −3.

    Do not round 31.25 to 31. The inequality is \(n\gt31.25\), so you must go up to the next whole number.

    Q2

    In an AP the third and the seventh terms add up to 6 and multiply to 8. Find the sum of the first sixteen terms of that AP.

    Solution

    Given: \(a_3+a_7=6\) and \(a_3\,a_7=8\).  To find: \(S_{16}\).

    Write the two terms with \(a\) and \(d\):

    $$a_3=a+2d,\qquad a_7=a+6d$$

    Using the sum:

    $$\begin{aligned}(a+2d)+(a+6d)&=6\\ 2a+8d&=6\\ a+4d&=3\qquad\cdots(1)\end{aligned}$$

    A neat way to see (1): \(a+4d\) is the 5th term, and the 3rd and 7th terms sit symmetrically about it, so their average is \(a_5=3\).

    From (1), \(a=3-4d\), which makes the two terms very tidy:

    $$\begin{aligned}a_3&=a+2d=3-2d\\ a_7&=a+6d=3+2d\end{aligned}$$

    Using the product:

    $$\begin{aligned}(3-2d)(3+2d)&=8\\ 9-4d^2&=8\\ 4d^2&=1\\ d^2&=\frac14\quad\Rightarrow\quad d=\pm\frac12\end{aligned}$$

    Both signs are allowed here — nothing in the question forces the AP to increase. So there are two APs that fit, and the question has two answers. (Compare Question 19 of Exercise 5.3, where one root had to be thrown away because a row cannot hold a negative number of logs.)

    Case 1

    \(d=\dfrac12\), so \(a=3-4\left(\dfrac12\right)=1\). The AP is \(1,\ \tfrac32,\ 2,\ \tfrac52,\ \ldots\)

    $$\begin{aligned}S_{16}&=\frac{16}{2}\left[2(1)+15\left(\frac12\right)\right]\\ &=8\left[2+\frac{15}{2}\right]\\ &=8\times\frac{19}{2}=76\end{aligned}$$

    Check: \(a_3=2,\ a_7=4\); sum 6 ✓ product 8 ✓

    Case 2

    \(d=-\dfrac12\), so \(a=3-4\left(-\dfrac12\right)=5\). The AP is \(5,\ \tfrac92,\ 4,\ \tfrac72,\ \ldots\)

    $$\begin{aligned}S_{16}&=\frac{16}{2}\left[2(5)+15\left(-\frac12\right)\right]\\ &=8\left[10-\frac{15}{2}\right]\\ &=8\times\frac52=20\end{aligned}$$

    Check: \(a_3=4,\ a_7=2\); sum 6 ✓ product 8 ✓

    \(S_{16}=76\) (when \(d=\tfrac12\)) or \(S_{16}=20\) (when \(d=-\tfrac12\)). Both are correct.

    Q3

    The rungs of a ladder are 25 cm apart. Their lengths shrink evenly from 45 cm at the bottom rung to 25 cm at the top rung, and the top and bottom rungs are \(2\tfrac12\) m apart. What length of wood is needed for all the rungs?

    Solution

    Step 1 — how many rungs are there?

    Distance from the bottom rung to the top rung \(=2\tfrac12\ \text{m}=\dfrac52\times100=250\ \text{cm}\).

    Consecutive rungs are 25 cm apart, so the number of gaps is

    $$\frac{250}{25}=10$$ $$\text{number of rungs}=10+1=11$$

    Rungs are like fence posts: 10 gaps need 11 posts. Forgetting the “\(+1\)” is the commonest slip in this question.

    Step 2 — the lengths form an AP.

    The lengths shrink evenly from 45 cm to 25 cm over 11 rungs, so they form an AP with

    $$a=45,\qquad l=a_{11}=25,\qquad n=11$$

    Its common difference is \(d=\dfrac{25-45}{10}=-2\) cm, so the lengths are 45, 43, 41, …, 25 cm.

    Step 3 — add them up.

    $$\begin{aligned}S_{11}&=\frac{11}{2}\,(a+l)\\ &=\frac{11}{2}\,(45+25)\\ &=\frac{11}{2}\times70=11\times35=385\end{aligned}$$

    385 cm (that is, 3.85 m) of wood is needed for the rungs.

    Q4

    The houses along one side of a road are numbered 1, 2, 3, …, 49 in order. Show that some house number \(x\) has this property: the numbers of all the houses before it add up to exactly the same total as the numbers of all the houses after it. Find that value of \(x\).

    Solution

    The house numbers 1, 2, 3, …, 49 form an AP with \(a=1\) and \(d=1\), so the first \(k\) of them add up to

    $$S_k=\frac k2\,(1+k)=\frac{k(k+1)}{2}$$

    Houses before house \(x\) are 1, 2, …, \(x-1\), so their total is

    $$S_{x-1}=\frac{(x-1)x}{2}$$

    Houses after house \(x\) are \(x+1,\ x+2,\ \ldots,\ 49\). Their total is everything minus the first \(x\) houses:

    $$\begin{aligned}S_{49}-S_x&=\frac{49\times50}{2}-\frac{x(x+1)}{2}\\ &=1225-\frac{x(x+1)}{2}\end{aligned}$$

    Set the two totals equal:

    $$\begin{aligned}\frac{(x-1)x}{2}&=1225-\frac{x(x+1)}{2}\\ x^2-x&=2450-x^2-x\\ 2x^2&=2450\\ x^2&=1225\\ x&=\pm35\end{aligned}$$

    A house number cannot be negative, so \(x=-35\) is rejected and \(x=35\).

    Since 35 lies between 1 and 49, such a house really does exist — which is what we had to show. Hence proved.

    Check:

    $$\begin{aligned}1+2+\cdots+34&=\frac{34\times35}{2}=595\\ 36+37+\cdots+49&=1225-\frac{35\times36}{2}\\ &=1225-630=595\end{aligned}$$

    The two totals agree. ✓

    \(x=35\)

    Q5

    A small terrace at a football ground is built of solid concrete in 15 steps, each step 50 m long. Every step rises \(\dfrac14\) m and has a tread of \(\dfrac12\) m. Work out the total volume of concrete needed to build the terrace.

    Solution

    Each step is a cuboid of concrete: volume = height × tread × length, with tread \(=\dfrac12\) m and length \(=50\) m throughout. Only the height changes.

    Step 1. Its height is \(\dfrac14\) m, so

    $$V_1=\frac14\times\frac12\times50=\frac{50}{8}=\frac{25}{4}\ \text{m}^3$$

    Step 2. To stand \(\dfrac14\) m higher, this block must be \(2\times\dfrac14=\dfrac12\) m tall:

    $$V_2=\frac12\times\frac12\times50=\frac{50}{4}=\frac{25}{2}\ \text{m}^3$$

    Step 3. Height \(3\times\dfrac14=\dfrac34\) m:

    $$V_3=\frac34\times\frac12\times50=\frac{75}{4}\ \text{m}^3$$

    So the volumes are

    $$\frac{25}{4},\quad \frac{50}{4},\quad \frac{75}{4},\quad\ldots\ \text{m}^3$$

    an AP with

    $$a=\frac{25}{4},\qquad d=\frac{25}{4},\qquad n=15$$ $$\begin{aligned}S_{15}&=\frac{15}{2}\left[2\left(\frac{25}{4}\right)+(15-1)\left(\frac{25}{4}\right)\right]\\ &=\frac{15}{2}\times\frac{25}{4}\,[\,2+14\,]\\ &=\frac{15}{2}\times\frac{25}{4}\times16\\ &=15\times25\times2=750\end{aligned}$$

    750 m³ of concrete is needed.

    Shortcut: every volume is \(\dfrac{25}{4}\) times a whole number, so \(S_{15}=\dfrac{25}{4}\,(1+2+\cdots+15)=\dfrac{25}{4}\times120=750\) m³.

    Penalty for 15 days of delay = ₹ 6750

    (ii)

    Let the delay be \(n\) days, so \(S_n=12600\).

    $$\begin{aligned}\tfrac n2\bigl[200+(n-1)50\bigr]&=12600\\ \tfrac n2\,[150+50n]&=12600\\ 25n(n+3)&=12600\\ n^2+3n-504&=0\\ (n+24)(n-21)&=0\end{aligned}$$

    So \(n=21\) or \(n=-24\). A number of days cannot be negative, so \(n=-24\) is rejected.

    The work was delayed by 21 days.

    Check: \(S_{21}=\frac{21}{2}[200+20\times50]=\frac{21}{2}\times1200=12\,600\) ✓

    2 marks for part (i) · 2 marks for forming and solving the quadratic · 1 mark for rejecting the negative root and stating the answer.

    The sum of three numbers in AP is 27 and their product is 504. Find the three numbers. ORThe sum of three numbers in AP is 27 and the sum of their squares is 293. Find the three numbers.

    Answer & solution

    Choosing the numbers cleverly. Take the three numbers as \(a-d,\ a,\ a+d\). Then the \(d\)s cancel in the sum, which makes the working short.

    Step 1 (both versions).

    \((a-d)+a+(a+d)=27\Rightarrow3a=27\Rightarrow a=9\).

    Step 2. Using the product 504:

    $$\begin{aligned}(9-d)(9)(9+d)&=504\\9\,(81-d^2)&=504\\81-d^2&=56\\d^2&=25\end{aligned}$$

    So \(d=5\) or \(d=-5\).

    If \(d=5\): the numbers are \(4,\ 9,\ 14\). If \(d=-5\): they are \(14,\ 9,\ 4\) — the same three numbers in the reverse order.

    4, 9 and 14

    OR — using the sum of squares 293:

    $$\begin{aligned}(9-d)^2+9^2+(9+d)^2&=293\\2\bigl(81+d^2\bigr)+81&=293\\243+2d^2&=293\\d^2&=25\end{aligned}$$

    Here \((9-d)^2+(9+d)^2=2(81+d^2)\) — the \(18d\) terms cancel.

    Again \(d=\pm5\), so the numbers are 4, 9 and 14.

    Check: \(4+9+14=27\); \(4\times9\times14=504\); \(16+81+196=293\). ✓

    1 for taking \(a-d,\ a,\ a+d\) · 1 for \(a=9\) · 2 for the equation in \(d\) and solving it · 1 for the numbers.

    Find the sum of all the integers between 100 and 500 that are divisible by 8. Hence find the sum of all the integers between 100 and 500 that are not divisible by 8. ORFind the sum of all the odd integers between 200 and 400.

    Step 1. The multiples of 8. The first multiple of 8 after 100 is \(104=8\times13\) and the last one before 500 is \(496=8\times62\). They form an AP:

    104, 112, 120, …, 496 with \(a=104,\ d=8,\ l=496\).

    Step 2. How many?

    \(104+(n-1)8=496\Rightarrow(n-1)8=392\Rightarrow n-1=49\Rightarrow n=50\).

    Step 3. Their sum.

    \(S_{50}=\dfrac{50}{2}(104+496)=25\times600=15\,000\).

    Step 4. The integers that are not multiples of 8. All the integers between 100 and 500 are 101, 102, …, 499 — an AP with \(a=101,\ l=499\) and \(n=499-101+1=399\).

    $$\begin{aligned}\text{Total}&=\tfrac{399}{2}(101+499)\\&=\tfrac{399}{2}\times600=399\times300\\&=119\,700\end{aligned}$$

    Required sum \(=119\,700-15\,000=104\,700\).

    15 000 and 104 700

    OR — odd integers between 200 and 400. They are 201, 203, 205, …, 399, an AP with \(a=201,\ d=2,\ l=399\).

    Ten flags are to be fixed in a straight line, 4 m apart from one another. All ten flags are kept at the place where the first flag is to be fixed.     3. Find the total distance she covers to fix all ten flags.

    The \(k\)th flag stands \(4(k-1)\) m from the start, and she walks there and back, so the distance for the \(k\)th flag is \(8(k-1)\) m. The first flag needs no walk at all.

    (i)

    2nd flag: \(2\times4=8\) m. 3rd flag: \(2\times8=16\) m. 4th flag: \(2\times12=24\) m.

    (ii)

    Let the \(m\)th of these trips be 48 m: \(\ 8+(m-1)8=48\Rightarrow(m-1)8=40\Rightarrow m=6\).

    This is the 6th trip, and the trips start at the 2nd flag, so it is for the 7th flag. (Directly: \(8(k-1)=48\Rightarrow k=7\).)

    (iii)

    There are 9 trips in all, for the 2nd to the 10th flag: 8, 16, 24, …, 72 metres.

    Total distance = 360 m

    Long answer5 marks

    In an AP, the sum of the first \(m\) terms is \(n\) and the sum of the first \(n\) terms is \(m\), where \(m\ne n\). Prove that the sum of its first \((m+n)\) terms is \(-(m+n)\).

    Answer & solution

    Let the first term be \(a\) and the common difference be \(d\).

    Given:

    \(S_m=\dfrac m2\bigl[2a+(m-1)d\bigr]=n\), so

    $$m\bigl[2a+(m-1)d\bigr]=2n\quad\cdots(1)$$

    \(S_n=\dfrac n2\bigl[2a+(n-1)d\bigr]=m\), so

    $$n\bigl[2a+(n-1)d\bigr]=2m\quad\cdots(2)$$

    Subtract (2) from (1):

    $$\begin{aligned}2a(m-n)&+d\bigl[m(m-1)-n(n-1)\bigr]\\&=2n-2m\end{aligned}$$

    Simplify the bracket:

    $$\begin{aligned}&m^2-m-n^2+n\\&=(m^2-n^2)-(m-n)\\&=(m-n)(m+n)-(m-n)\\&=(m-n)(m+n-1)\end{aligned}$$

    So the equation becomes

    $$\begin{aligned}&(m-n)\bigl[2a+(m+n-1)d\bigr]\\&=-2(m-n)\end{aligned}$$

    Since \(m\ne n\), we may divide both sides by \(m-n\):

    $$2a+(m+n-1)d=-2\quad\cdots(3)$$

    Now the required sum:

    $$\begin{aligned}S_{m+n}&=\frac{m+n}{2}\bigl[2a+(m+n-1)d\bigr]\\&=\frac{m+n}{2}\times(-2)\qquad\text{[by (3)]}\\&=-(m+n)\end{aligned}$$

    Hence proved.

    1 for both given equations · 2 for subtracting and factorising · 1 for result (3) · 1 for the final sum.

    Arithmetic Progressions

    What makes it an AP

    • Each term = previous term \(+\ d\)
    • Test: \(a_2-a_1=a_3-a_2=\cdots\)
    • \(d\) may be positive, negative or zero
    • General form \(a,\ a+d,\ a+2d,\ \ldots\)

    The \(n\)th term

    • \(a_n=a+(n-1)d\) — four letters, know any three
    • Last term \(l=a+(n-1)d\)
    • \(n\)th term from the end \(=l-(n-1)d\)
    • \(n=\dfrac{l-a}{d}+1\) — never forget the \(+1\)

    The sum of \(n\) terms

    • \(S_n=\dfrac n2\bigl[2a+(n-1)d\bigr]\)
    • \(S_n=\dfrac n2(a+l)\) when the last term is known
    • \(a_n=S_n-S_{n-1}\), and \(a_1=S_1\)

    Finding \(a\) and \(d\)

    • Two facts → two equations → subtract
    • Three numbers in AP: take \(a-d,\ a,\ a+d\)
    • \(b\) is the arithmetic mean: \(b=\dfrac{a+c}{2}\)

    Sums worth memorising

    • \(1+2+\cdots+n=\dfrac{n(n+1)}{2}\)
    • First \(n\) odd numbers \(=n^2\)
    • First \(n\) even numbers \(=n(n+1)\)
    • Multiples of \(k\): take \(k\) out first

    Word problems

    • Money each month/week/day → savings, salary, instalments, penalty
    • Rows of seats, logs, plants, rungs of a ladder
    • “How many terms give this sum?” → quadratic in \(n\)
    • \(n\) must be a positive integer — reject the rest

    Formulas and facts to remember

    • Common difference\(d=a_{k+1}-a_k\) — always “later term minus earlier term”.
    • \(n\)th term\(a_n=a+(n-1)d\)
    • Number of terms\(n=\dfrac{l-a}{d}+1\), where \(l\) is the last term.
    • \(n\)th term from the end\(l-(n-1)d\)
    • Sum of \(n\) terms\(S_n=\dfrac n2\bigl[2a+(n-1)d\bigr]\)
    • Sum when \(l\) is known\(S_n=\dfrac n2(a+l)\)
    • Term from the sums\(a_n=S_n-S_{n-1}\), and \(a_1=S_1\).
    • Arithmetic meanIf \(a,b,c\) are in AP then \(b=\dfrac{a+c}{2}\).
    • First \(n\) natural numbers\(1+2+\cdots+n=\dfrac{n(n+1)}{2}\)
    • First \(n\) odd numbers\(1+3+5+\cdots=n^2\)

    Mistakes that cost marks

    • Finding \(d\) as \(a_1-a_2\).Subtract the earlier term from the later one: \(d=a_2-a_1\). In 11, 8, 5, … the answer is \(-3\), not \(3\).
    • Writing \(a_n=a+nd\).It is \(a+(n-1)d\). From the first term you take \(n-1\) steps, not \(n\).
    • Answering \(n-1\) when the question asks for \(n\).After \((n-1)d=\ldots\) there is one more line: add 1. Gaps and terms are different things.
    • Taking the \(n\)th term from the end as \(l-nd\).The last term is the 1st from the end, so it is \(l-(n-1)d\).
    • For \(a_n=pn+q\), saying \(d=q\).\(d\) is \(p\), the coefficient of \(n\); \(q+p\) is the first term. Same for \(S_n\): work out \(a_n=S_n-S_{n-1}\) first.
    • Keeping an impossible root of a quadratic in \(n\).\(n\) must be a positive integer. Reject negatives and fractions — but if both roots are positive integers, both are answers.
    • Using the sum formula beyond where the AP holds.If a pattern stops (a ceiling on the walking time, a last row of seats), split the total into the AP part plus the rest.
    • Calling 1, 4, 9, 16, … an AP because “it has a pattern”.Only equal differences count. Here they are 3, 5, 7 — not an AP.

    Last-minute checklist

    Which formula when

    The question says…Use
    “find the 30th term”, “which term is …”\(a_n=a+(n-1)d\)
    “how many terms”, “how many rows”\(a_n=a+(n-1)d\) with \(a_n=l\)
    “find the sum”, “in all”, “altogether”\(S_n=\dfrac n2\bigl[2a+(n-1)d\bigr]\)
    the last term is given as well\(S_n=\dfrac n2(a+l)\) — much shorter
    \(S_n\) is given as a formula in \(n\)\(a_n=S_n-S_{n-1}\)
    “how many terms add up to …”\(S_n=\) given, then a quadratic in \(n\)
    “three numbers in AP”take them as \(a-d,\ a,\ a+d\)

    Why the sum formula works

    If you ever forget the formula in the exam, rebuild it in three lines: write \(S\), write \(S\) backwards, add. You get \(2S=n(a+l)\), so \(S_n=\frac n2(a+l)\); and putting \(l=a+(n-1)d\) gives the other form.
