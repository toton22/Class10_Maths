    2.1 Polynomials, their values and their zeroes

    In Class IX you learnt to add, multiply and factorise polynomials. This chapter asks one sharper question: for which values of \(x\) does a polynomial become zero? Those special values are called the zeroes. You can see them on a graph, and for a quadratic polynomial they are tied to the coefficients by two small formulas.

    • See the zeroes

      The zeroes are the places where the graph of \(y=p(x)\) meets the \(x\)-axis. Count them by looking.

    • Find the zeroes

      Factorise the quadratic polynomial by splitting the middle term, then make each factor zero.

    • Link them to the coefficients

      Sum of zeroes \(=-\frac ba\), product of zeroes \(=\frac ca\). Use the link forwards (to verify) and backwards (to build a polynomial).

    A polynomial in one variable \(x\) is an expression made of terms like \(a\,x^n\), where every coefficient \(a\) is a real number and every power \(n\) is a whole number (0, 1, 2, 3, …). It is written \(p(x)\), read “\(p\) of \(x\)”.

    The degree of \(p(x)\) is the highest power of \(x\) in it. A non-zero constant such as 7 is a polynomial of degree 0.

    Is it a polynomial? What is its degree?

    Check one thing only: every power of the variable must be a whole number. The coefficients can be any real numbers — fractions, negatives, even surds.

    ExpressionPolynomial?Reason

    \(2y^2-3y+4\)Yes, degree 2the variable is \(y\); its highest power is 2
    \(5x^3-4x^2+x-\sqrt2\)Yes, degree 3\(\sqrt2\) is only a number (the constant term); the root is not on \(x\)

    Three families you must recognise

    • 1

      Linear polynomial

      Degree 1. General form \(ax+b\), \(a\ne0\).

      Examples: \(2x-3\), \(\sqrt3x+5\), \(y+\sqrt2\), \(\dfrac23u+1\).

    • 2

      Quadratic polynomial

      Degree 2. General form \(ax^2+bx+c\), \(a\ne0\).

      Examples: \(2x^2+3x-\dfrac25\), \(y^2-2\), \(2-x^2+\sqrt3x\).

    • 3

      Cubic polynomial

      Degree 3. General form \(ax^3+bx^2+cx+d\), \(a\ne0\).

      Examples: \(2-x^3\), \(x^3\), \(3x^3-2x^2+x-1\).

    In every general form \(a,\,b,\,c,\,d\) are real numbers. The word quadratic comes from “quadrate”, which means “square” — the highest power is a square.

    Decide the family from the highest power, not from the first term you see. \(2x+5-x^2\) is quadratic, not linear. \(x^3+1\) is cubic even though it has only two terms.

    The condition \(a\ne0\) matters. If \(a=0\) in \(ax^2+bx+c\), the \(x^2\) term vanishes and the polynomial is no longer quadratic.

    Value of a polynomial

    Definition

    If \(p(x)\) is a polynomial in \(x\) and \(k\) is any real number, the number you get by replacing \(x\) by \(k\) in \(p(x)\) is called the value of \(p(x)\) at \(x=k\). It is written \(p(k)\).

    Take \(p(x)=x^2-3x-4\). Then

    $$\begin{aligned} p(2)&=2^2-3(2)-4=4-6-4=-6\\ p(0)&=0^2-3(0)-4=-4\\ p(-1)&=(-1)^2-3(-1)-4\\ &=1+3-4=0\\ p(4)&=4^2-3(4)-4=16-12-4=0 \end{aligned}$$

    At \(x=-1\) and at \(x=4\) the value is zero. Such numbers get a special name.

    Zero of a polynomial

    Definition

    A real number \(k\) is called a zero of the polynomial \(p(x)\) if \(p(k)=0\).

    So \(-1\) and \(4\) are zeroes of \(x^2-3x-4\), while 2 and 0 are not.

    When you substitute a negative number, always put it in brackets: \((-1)^2=1\), but \(-1^2=-1\). Most “silly mistakes” in this chapter are sign mistakes made in this one step.

    To test whether a number is a zero, you do not need to factorise anything. Just substitute and see whether you get 0.

    The zero of a linear polynomial

    If \(k\) is a zero of \(p(x)=2x+3\), then \(p(k)=2k+3=0\), which gives \(k=-\dfrac32\).

    In general, if \(k\) is a zero of \(p(x)=ax+b\), then \(ak+b=0\), so \(k=-\dfrac ba\).

    Zero of a linear polynomial
    $$\begin{aligned} \text{zero of } ax+b&=-\frac ba\\ &=\frac{-(\text{constant term})}{\text{coefficient of } x} \end{aligned}$$

    A linear polynomial has exactly one zero, and the zero is decided completely by the coefficients \(a\) and \(b\).

    This raises the question that drives the rest of the chapter: are the zeroes of a quadratic polynomial also tied to its coefficients? They are — Section 2.3 shows how. First, Section 2.2 shows what a zero looks like on a graph.

    Do not mix up two phrases. A zero of a polynomial is a value of \(x\) that makes the polynomial 0. The zero polynomial is the polynomial \(p(x)=0\) itself. Also, polynomials have zeroes; equations have roots (Chapter 4). The zeroes of \(p(x)\) are the roots of the equation \(p(x)=0\).

    (a) What is the degree of \(7-2x^3+x\)? Which family does it belong to? (b) Is \(x^2+\sqrt{x}\) a polynomial? (c) Which of the numbers \(-1,\ 1,\ 2\) are zeroes of \(p(x)=x^2-x-2\)?

    Show answer

    (a) The highest power is 3, so the degree is 3 — a cubic polynomial. (b) No: \(\sqrt x=x^{1/2}\) and \(\frac12\) is not a whole number. (c) \(p(-1)=1+1-2=0\), \(p(1)=1-1-2=-2\), \(p(2)=4-2-2=0\). So \(-1\) and \(2\) are zeroes; 1 is not.

    2.2 Geometrical meaning of the zeroes of a polynomial

    A zero is a number. It is also a place: the spot where the graph of \(y=p(x)\) meets the \(x\)-axis. Once you see this, counting the zeroes of a polynomial becomes a one-look job.

    Every point on the \(x\)-axis has \(y\)-coordinate 0. The graph of \(y=p(x)\) passes through the point \((k,\,0)\) exactly when \(p(k)=0\), that is, exactly when \(k\) is a zero of \(p(x)\).

    Key idea

    The zeroes of a polynomial \(p(x)\) are precisely the \(x\)-coordinates of the points where the graph of \(y=p(x)\) meets the \(x\)-axis.

    Linear polynomial: a straight line

    From Class IX, the graph of \(y=ax+b\) is a straight line. Two points are enough to draw it. For \(y=2x+3\):

    \(x\)\(-2\)\(2\)
    \(y=2x+3\)\(-1\)\(7\)

    In general, the graph of \(y=ax+b\), \(a\ne0\), is a straight line that meets the \(x\)-axis at exactly one point, namely \(\left(-\dfrac ba,\,0\right)\). So a linear polynomial has exactly one zero: the \(x\)-coordinate of the point where its graph crosses the \(x\)-axis.

    Quadratic polynomial: a parabola

    Take \(p(x)=x^2-3x-4\) again. Work out \(y=x^2-3x-4\) for a few values of \(x\):

    \(x\)\(-2\)\(-1\)\(0\)\(1\)\(2\)\(3\)\(4\)\(5\)
    \(y=x^2-3x-4\)\(6\)\(0\)\(-4\)\(-6\)\(-6\)\(-4\)\(0\)\(6\)

    Plot these eight points on graph paper and join them with a smooth curve.

    This U-shaped curve is called a parabola. The graph of every quadratic polynomial is a parabola, and the zeroes of \(ax^2+bx+c\) are precisely the \(x\)-coordinates of the points where the parabola \(y=ax^2+bx+c\) meets the \(x\)-axis.

    The book says clearly that plotting the graph of a quadratic or a cubic polynomial is not expected from you and is not evaluated. What is asked is reading a given graph: how many zeroes, which numbers they are, whether \(a\) is positive or negative. So learn the shapes; do not waste time practising plotting.

    Which way does the parabola open?

    \(a\gt0\): opens upwards

    The coefficient of \(x^2\) is positive. The curve looks like a cup, ∪, and has a lowest point.

    Example: \(y=x^2-3x-4\), where \(a=1\).

    \(a\lt0\): opens downwards

    The coefficient of \(x^2\) is negative. The curve looks like a cap, ∩, and has a highest point.

    Example: \(y=-x^2+3x+4\), where \(a=-1\).

    Positive \(a\), happy face ∪. Negative \(a\), sad face ∩. Look only at the sign of the coefficient of \(x^2\); the other coefficients do not change which way the parabola opens.

    How many zeroes can a quadratic polynomial have? Three cases

    A parabola that opens up or down can sit in only three ways with respect to the \(x\)-axis.

    Case (i): the parabola cuts the \(x\)-axis at two distinct points A and A′. The \(x\)-coordinates of A and A′ are the two zeroes of the quadratic polynomial. Example: \(x^2-3x-4\), with zeroes \(-1\) and \(4\).

    Case (ii): the parabola touches the \(x\)-axis at exactly one point A. Think of the two points A and A′ of Case (i) sliding towards each other until they become one point. The \(x\)-coordinate of A is the only zero; we also say the polynomial has two equal (coincident) zeroes. Example: \(x^2-2x+1=(x-1)^2\), whose only zero is 1.

    Case (iii): the parabola does not meet the \(x\)-axis at all. It lies completely above the \(x\)-axis or completely below it. There is no point of the form \((k,0)\) on the graph, so the polynomial has no zero. Example: \(x^2+1\), whose value is never less than 1.

    Cuts at two points

    Two distinct zeroes.

    Touches at one point

    One zero (two equal zeroes).

    Never meets the axis

    No zero.

    So a quadratic polynomial can have two distinct zeroes, or two equal zeroes (that is, one zero), or no zero. In short: a polynomial of degree 2 has at most two zeroes.

    Cubic polynomial: up to three zeroes

    Now take the cubic polynomial \(x^3-4x\). A few values of \(y=x^3-4x\):

    \(x\)\(-2\)\(-1\)\(0\)\(1\)\(2\)
    \(y=x^3-4x\)\(0\)\(3\)\(0\)\(-3\)\(0\)

    Does every cubic have three zeroes? No. Look at two more graphs.

    So a cubic polynomial can have one, two or three zeroes, but never more: a polynomial of degree 3 has at most three zeroes.

    Key result

    In general, the graph of \(y=p(x)\), where \(p(x)\) is a polynomial of degree \(n\), meets the \(x\)-axis at at most \(n\) points. Therefore a polynomial of degree \(n\) has at most \(n\) zeroes.

    The six graphs are re-drawn for this page; each meets the \(x\)-axis exactly as often as the matching graph in the book.

    Solution

    The number of zeroes of \(p(x)\) is the number of points at which the graph of \(y=p(x)\) meets the \(x\)-axis. In the figures below these points are marked in red.

    (i)

    The graph meets the \(x\)-axis at one point only. The bends on the right stay above the axis, so they add nothing. Number of zeroes = 1.

    (ii)

    (iii)

    (iv)

    (v)

    (i) 1   (ii) 2   (iii) 3   (iv) 1   (v) 1   (vi) 4

    Read it backwards too. The number of zeroes can never be more than the degree. So if a graph meets the \(x\)-axis at 4 points, as in graph (vi), the polynomial must have degree at least 4. A graph that meets the \(x\)-axis 3 times cannot belong to a quadratic polynomial.

    How to count zeroes from a graph

    1. Find the \(x\)-axis (the horizontal axis). Ignore everything else for a moment.
    2. Move your pencil along the \(x\)-axis from left to right. Count every point where the curve meets it — whether the curve cuts through the axis or only touches it.
    3. That count is the number of zeroes. If the zeroes themselves are asked, read the \(x\)-coordinates of those points.

    Do not count the point where the graph crosses the \(y\)-axis, and do not count the “hills and valleys” (turning points) of the curve. Only meetings with the \(x\)-axis matter.

    “No zero” and “the zero is 0” are different answers. A graph that never meets the \(x\)-axis has no zero. A graph that passes through the origin has the number 0 as a zero.

    This idea is usually tested by a 1-mark MCQ (“the graph of \(y=p(x)\) is shown; the number of zeroes of \(p(x)\) is …”) or inside a case study built on a parabolic shape — an arch, a fountain, the path of a ball. Typical parts: the number of zeroes, the zeroes themselves, the sign of \(a\), and the polynomial, which you write as \(k(x-\alpha)(x-\beta)\).

    (a) The graph of \(y=p(x)\) cuts the \(x\)-axis at \(x=-3\) and \(x=2\), and touches it at \(x=5\). How many zeroes does \(p(x)\) have? (b) Can the graph of a quadratic polynomial meet the \(x\)-axis at three points? (c) The parabola \(y=ax^2+bx+c\) opens downwards and its highest point is below the \(x\)-axis. What can you say about \(a\) and about the zeroes?

    Show answer

    (a) Three zeroes: \(-3,\ 2\) and \(5\). (b) No. A polynomial of degree 2 has at most 2 zeroes, so its graph meets the \(x\)-axis at most twice. (c) \(a\lt0\), and the parabola lies completely below the \(x\)-axis, so the polynomial has no zero.

    2.3 Relationship between zeroes and coefficients of a polynomial

    The zero of \(ax+b\) is \(-\dfrac ba\) — it is decided by the coefficients. The same is true for a quadratic polynomial: the sum and the product of its two zeroes can be read straight from \(a,\ b\) and \(c\), without finding the zeroes at all. This is the most asked idea of the chapter.

    To factorise \(ax^2+bx+c\), find two numbers whose sum is \(b\) and whose product is \(a\times c\). Break the middle term \(bx\) into these two parts, then take common factors in pairs.

    1. Multiply \(a\) and \(c\)For \(6x^2+x-12\): \(a\times c=6\times(-12)=-72\), and \(b=1\).
    2. Find the pairTwo numbers with product \(-72\) and sum \(1\): they are \(9\) and \(-8\).
    3. Split the middle term\(6x^2+9x-8x-12\)
    4. Group in pairs\(3x(2x+3)-4(2x+3)\) \(=(3x-4)(2x+3)\)
    5. Read the zeroes\(3x-4=0\) or \(2x+3=0\), so \(x=\dfrac43\) or \(x=-\dfrac32\).

    Look for the pattern

    First polynomial. Take \(p(x)=2x^2-8x+6\). We need two numbers with sum \(-8\) and product \(2\times6=12\); they are \(-6\) and \(-2\).

    $$\begin{aligned} &2x^2-8x+6\\ &=2x^2-6x-2x+6\\ &=2x(x-3)-2(x-3)\\ &=(2x-2)(x-3)\\ &=2(x-1)(x-3) \end{aligned}$$

    So \(p(x)=0\) when \(x-1=0\) or \(x-3=0\). The zeroes are \(1\) and \(3\).

    Second polynomial. Take \(p(x)=3x^2+5x-2\). Sum \(5\), product \(3\times(-2)=-6\); the numbers are \(6\) and \(-1\).

    $$\begin{aligned} &3x^2+5x-2\\ &=3x^2+6x-x-2\\ &=3x(x+2)-1(x+2)\\ &=(3x-1)(x+2) \end{aligned}$$

    The zeroes are \(\dfrac13\) and \(-2\). Now compare the zeroes with the coefficients:

    PolynomialZeroesSum of zeroes\(-\dfrac ba\)Product of zeroes\(\dfrac ca\)
    \(2x^2-8x+6\)\(1,\ 3\)\(4\)\(-\dfrac{(-8)}{2}=4\)\(3\)\(\dfrac62=3\)
    \(3x^2+5x-2\)\(\dfrac13,\ -2\)\(-\dfrac53\)\(-\dfrac53\)\(-\dfrac23\)\(\dfrac{-2}{3}\)

    Both times the sum of the zeroes equals \(-\dfrac ba\) and the product equals \(\dfrac ca\). This is no accident.

    Key resultzeroes and coefficients of a quadratic polynomial

    If \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) are the zeroes of the quadratic polynomial \(p(x)=ax^2+bx+c,\ a\ne0\), then

    $$\alpha+\beta=-\frac ba,\qquad \alpha\beta=\frac ca.$$
    Derivation (the book gives it — understand every line)

    Since \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) are the zeroes of \(p(x)\), \((x-\alpha)\) and \((x-\beta)\) are factors of \(p(x)\). A quadratic has no room for any other factor except a constant. So, for some constant \(k\),

    $$\begin{aligned} &ax^2+bx+c\\ &=k(x-\alpha)(x-\beta)\\ &=k\left[x^2-(\alpha+\beta)x+\alpha\beta\right]\\ &=kx^2-k(\alpha+\beta)x+k\alpha\beta. \end{aligned}$$

    Two polynomials that are equal for every \(x\) have the same coefficients. Comparing the coefficients of \(x^2\), of \(x\), and the constant terms:

    $$a=k,\quad b=-k(\alpha+\beta),\quad c=k\alpha\beta.$$

    Dividing the second and the third result by the first,

    $$\alpha+\beta=-\frac ba,\qquad \alpha\beta=\frac ca.$$ Derived.

    \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) are the Greek letters alpha and beta. Later we need a third letter, \(\gamma\) (gamma).

    Sum and product of zeroes — learn it in words too
    $$\begin{aligned} \text{sum of zeroes}&=\alpha+\beta=-\frac ba\\ &=\frac{-(\text{coefficient of }x)}{\text{coefficient of }x^2} \end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned} \text{product of zeroes}&=\alpha\beta=\frac ca\\ &=\frac{\text{constant term}}{\text{coefficient of }x^2} \end{aligned}$$

    First write the polynomial in the standard order \(ax^2+bx+c\). Take \(a,\ b,\ c\) with their signs.

    Sum → Sign changes: \(-\dfrac ba\). Product → Plain: \(\dfrac ca\). And the letters run in order: \(b\) over \(a\), then \(c\) over \(a\).

    NCERT Example 2find zeroes and verify

    Find the zeroes of the quadratic polynomial \(x^2+7x+10\), and verify the relationship between the zeroes and the coefficients.

    Solution

    Two numbers with sum 7 and product 10 are 2 and 5.

    $$\begin{aligned} &x^2+7x+10\\ &=x^2+2x+5x+10\\ &=x(x+2)+5(x+2)\\ &=(x+2)(x+5) \end{aligned}$$

    The value of \(x^2+7x+10\) is zero when \(x+2=0\) or \(x+5=0\), that is, when \(x=-2\) or \(x=-5\).

    Therefore the zeroes of \(x^2+7x+10\) are \(-2\) and \(-5\).

    Verification. Here \(a=1,\ b=7,\ c=10\).

    Sum of zeroes \(=-2+(-5)=-7\), and \(\dfrac{-(\text{coefficient of }x)}{\text{coefficient of }x^2}=\dfrac{-(7)}{1}=-7\). The two are equal. ✓

    Product of zeroes \(=(-2)\times(-5)=10\), and \(\dfrac{\text{constant term}}{\text{coefficient of }x^2}=\dfrac{10}{1}=10\). The two are equal. ✓

    The zeroes are \(-2\) and \(-5\); the relationship is verified.

    NCERT Example 3no middle term

    Find the zeroes of the polynomial \(x^2-3\) and verify the relationship between the zeroes and the coefficients.

    Solution

    There is no middle term to split. Use the identity \(a^2-b^2=(a-b)(a+b)\) with \(3=(\sqrt3)^2\):

    $$\begin{aligned} &x^2-3\\ &=x^2-\left(\sqrt3\right)^2\\ &=\left(x-\sqrt3\right)\left(x+\sqrt3\right) \end{aligned}$$

    The value of \(x^2-3\) is zero when \(x=\sqrt3\) or \(x=-\sqrt3\).

    Therefore the zeroes of \(x^2-3\) are \(\sqrt3\) and \(-\sqrt3\).

    Verification. Write \(x^2-3=x^2+0x-3\), so \(a=1,\ b=0,\ c=-3\).

    Sum of zeroes \(=\sqrt3+\left(-\sqrt3\right)=0\), and \(\dfrac{-(\text{coefficient of }x)}{\text{coefficient of }x^2}=\dfrac{-(0)}{1}=0\). The two are equal. ✓

    Product of zeroes \(=\left(\sqrt3\right)\left(-\sqrt3\right)=-3\), and \(\dfrac{\text{constant term}}{\text{coefficient of }x^2}=\dfrac{-3}{1}=-3\). The two are equal. ✓

    The zeroes are \(\sqrt3\) and \(-\sqrt3\); the relationship is verified.

    “Find the zeroes and verify the relationship” is a standard 2- or 3-mark question. Marks are given for three separate things: the factorisation and zeroes, the sum check and the product check. In each check, work out the two sides separately — the zeroes on one side, the coefficients on the other — and show that they agree. Writing only “\(\alpha+\beta=-\frac ba\), verified” earns nothing.

    A missing term means a zero coefficient. In \(x^2-3\), \(b=0\). In \(4u^2+8u\), \(c=0\). Do not pick up the wrong number.

    Put the polynomial in standard order first. \(6x^2-3-7x\) is \(6x^2-7x-3\): here \(b=-7\) and \(c=-3\), not the other way round.

    Always divide by \(a\), the coefficient of \(x^2\), even when it is not 1. For \(2x^2-8x+6\) the sum of the zeroes is \(\frac82=4\), not 8.

    Going backwards: building a quadratic polynomial from its zeroes

    In the derivation we found \(ax^2+bx+c=k\left[x^2-(\alpha+\beta)x+\alpha\beta\right]\). Read from right to left, this tells us how to write a quadratic polynomial when the sum and the product of its zeroes are known.

    Quadratic polynomial with given zeroes
    $$\begin{aligned} p(x)=k\big[\,x^2&-(\text{sum of zeroes})\,x\\ &+(\text{product of zeroes})\,\big] \end{aligned}$$

    That is, \(p(x)=k\left[x^2-(\alpha+\beta)x+\alpha\beta\right]\). Here \(k\) is any non-zero real number, so there are infinitely many such polynomials; they all have the same zeroes. Choose \(k\) to clear the fractions. Mind the minus before the sum.

    NCERT Example 4sum and product given

    Find a quadratic polynomial, the sum and product of whose zeroes are \(-3\) and \(2\), respectively.

    Solution

    Let the quadratic polynomial be \(ax^2+bx+c\), and let its zeroes be \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\). We are given

    $$\begin{aligned} \alpha+\beta&=-3=-\frac ba\\ \alpha\beta&=2=\frac ca \end{aligned}$$

    If \(a=1\), then \(b=3\) and \(c=2\).

    So one quadratic polynomial which fits the given conditions is \(x^2+3x+2\).

    Same answer from the formula: \(x^2-(\alpha+\beta)x+\alpha\beta=x^2-(-3)x+2=x^2+3x+2\).

    \(x^2+3x+2\)

    Any other quadratic polynomial that fits these conditions is of the form \(k(x^2+3x+2)\), where \(k\) is a non-zero real number. Check: \(x^2+3x+2=(x+1)(x+2)\) has zeroes \(-1\) and \(-2\); their sum is \(-3\) and their product is \(2\). ✓

    Clearing fractions. Suppose the sum of the zeroes is \(-\dfrac12\) and the product is \(-\dfrac13\). Then \(p(x)=k\left[x^2-\left(-\dfrac12\right)x+\left(-\dfrac13\right)\right]=k\left[x^2+\dfrac12x-\dfrac13\right]\). Take \(k=6\), the LCM of the denominators, to get the clean answer \(6x^2+3x-2\).

    A polynomial is an expression. Do not write “\(=0\)” after it — \(x^2+3x+2=0\) is an equation, and that is not what the question asks for.

    Board extension: expressions in \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) without finding the zeroes

    Board papers often ask for the value of an expression such as \(\alpha^2+\beta^2\) or \(\dfrac1\alpha+\dfrac1\beta\). You are not expected to find \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) (they may be ugly surds). Rewrite the expression using only \(\alpha+\beta\) and \(\alpha\beta\), then substitute \(-\dfrac ba\) and \(\dfrac ca\).

    \(\alpha^2+\beta^2\)\((\alpha+\beta)^2-2\alpha\beta\)
    \(\dfrac1\alpha+\dfrac1\beta\)\(\dfrac{\alpha+\beta}{\alpha\beta}\)
    \(\alpha^2\beta+\alpha\beta^2\)\(\alpha\beta(\alpha+\beta)\)
    \((\alpha-\beta)^2\)\((\alpha+\beta)^2-4\alpha\beta\)
    \(\dfrac\alpha\beta+\dfrac\beta\alpha\)\(\dfrac{(\alpha+\beta)^2-2\alpha\beta}{\alpha\beta}\)
    \(\alpha^3+\beta^3\)\((\alpha+\beta)^3-3\alpha\beta(\alpha+\beta)\)

    Let \(\alpha,\ \beta\) be the zeroes of \(2x^2-6x+3\). Then \(\alpha+\beta=-\dfrac{(-6)}{2}=3\) and \(\alpha\beta=\dfrac32\).

    \(\alpha^2+\beta^2=(\alpha+\beta)^2-2\alpha\beta=9-3=6\), and \(\dfrac1\alpha+\dfrac1\beta=\dfrac{\alpha+\beta}{\alpha\beta}=3\div\dfrac32=2\).

    A polynomial whose zeroes are \(\dfrac1\alpha\) and \(\dfrac1\beta\): new sum \(=2\), new product \(=\dfrac{1}{\alpha\beta}=\dfrac23\). So \(p(x)=k\left[x^2-2x+\dfrac23\right]\), and \(k=3\) gives \(3x^2-6x+2\).

    Cubic polynomials: the same idea, one step further

    Take \(p(x)=2x^3-5x^2-14x+8\). You can check that \(p(4)=0\), \(p(-2)=0\) and \(p\!\left(\frac12\right)=0\). A cubic polynomial has at most three zeroes, so \(4,\ -2\) and \(\dfrac12\) are all the zeroes of \(p(x)\). Here \(a=2,\ b=-5,\ c=-14,\ d=8\). Now observe:

    $$\begin{aligned} &\text{sum of the zeroes}=4+(-2)+\frac12=\frac52\\ &\quad=\frac{-(-5)}{2}=\frac{-(\text{coefficient of }x^2)}{\text{coefficient of }x^3} \end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned} &\text{product of the zeroes}=4\times(-2)\times\frac12=-4\\ &\quad=\frac{-8}{2}=\frac{-(\text{constant term})}{\text{coefficient of }x^3} \end{aligned}$$

    With three zeroes there is one more combination — the sum of the products of the zeroes taken two at a time:

    $$\begin{aligned} &\{4\times(-2)\}+\left\{(-2)\times\frac12\right\}+\left\{\frac12\times4\right\}\\ &\quad=-8-1+2=-7\\ &\quad=\frac{-14}{2}=\frac{\text{coefficient of }x}{\text{coefficient of }x^3} \end{aligned}$$
    Zeroes and coefficients of a cubic polynomial

    If \(\alpha,\ \beta,\ \gamma\) are the zeroes of \(ax^3+bx^2+cx+d\), \(a\ne0\), then

    $$\begin{aligned} \alpha+\beta+\gamma&=-\frac ba\\ \alpha\beta+\beta\gamma+\gamma\alpha&=\frac ca\\ \alpha\beta\gamma&=-\frac da \end{aligned}$$

    The signs alternate: minus, plus, minus. The quadratic formulas follow the same pattern: \(-\dfrac ba\), then \(+\dfrac ca\).

    NCERT Example 5cubic · not from the examination point of view

    Verify that \(3,\ -1,\ -\dfrac13\) are the zeroes of the cubic polynomial \(p(x)=3x^3-5x^2-11x-3\), and then verify the relationship between the zeroes and the coefficients.

    Solution

    Comparing \(3x^3-5x^2-11x-3\) with \(ax^3+bx^2+cx+d\): \(a=3,\ b=-5,\ c=-11,\ d=-3\).

    $$\begin{aligned} p(3)&=3(3)^3-5(3)^2-11(3)-3\\ &=81-45-33-3=0 \end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned} p(-1)&=3(-1)^3-5(-1)^2\\ &\qquad-11(-1)-3\\ &=-3-5+11-3=0 \end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned} p\!\left(-\tfrac13\right)&=3\left(-\tfrac13\right)^3-5\left(-\tfrac13\right)^2\\ &\qquad-11\left(-\tfrac13\right)-3\\ &=-\tfrac19-\tfrac59+\tfrac{11}{3}-3\\ &=-\tfrac23+\tfrac23=0 \end{aligned}$$

    Therefore \(3,\ -1\) and \(-\dfrac13\) are the zeroes of \(3x^3-5x^2-11x-3\). Take \(\alpha=3,\ \beta=-1,\ \gamma=-\dfrac13\).

    $$\begin{aligned} &\alpha+\beta+\gamma=3+(-1)+\left(-\tfrac13\right)\\ &\quad=\tfrac53=\frac{-(-5)}{3}=-\frac ba \end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned} &\alpha\beta+\beta\gamma+\gamma\alpha\\ &\quad=3(-1)+(-1)\left(-\tfrac13\right)\\ &\qquad+\left(-\tfrac13\right)(3)\\ &\quad=-3+\tfrac13-1=-\tfrac{11}{3}=\frac ca \end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned} &\alpha\beta\gamma=3\times(-1)\times\left(-\tfrac13\right)=1\\ &\quad=\frac{-(-3)}{3}=-\frac da \end{aligned}$$

    All three relations hold, so the relationship is verified.

    1 question · reading the number of zeroes from the graph of \(y=p(x)\)

    Q1

    The graphs of \(y=p(x)\) are shown below for six different polynomials \(p(x)\). In each case, find the number of zeroes of \(p(x)\).

    The six graphs are re-drawn for this page. Each one meets the \(x\)-axis in exactly the same way, and the same number of times, as the matching graph in the book.

    Solution

    Idea used: the zeroes of \(p(x)\) are the \(x\)-coordinates of the points where the graph of \(y=p(x)\) meets the \(x\)-axis. So the number of zeroes = the number of points at which the graph meets the \(x\)-axis (cutting or touching). These points are marked in red below.

    (i)

    The graph is a straight line parallel to the \(x\)-axis. It does not meet the \(x\)-axis at any point.

    ∴ \(p(x)\) has no zeroes.

    (ii)

    The two bends of the curve lie below the \(x\)-axis and never reach it. The graph meets the \(x\)-axis at one point only.

    ∴ Number of zeroes = 1.

    (iii)

    The graph cuts the \(x\)-axis at three points.

    ∴ Number of zeroes = 3.

    (iv)

    The graph is a parabola lying to the left of the \(y\)-axis. It cuts the \(x\)-axis at two points.

    ∴ Number of zeroes = 2. (Both zeroes are negative numbers, because both points are on the negative side of the \(x\)-axis.)

    (v)

    The graph cuts the \(x\)-axis at four points.

    ∴ Number of zeroes = 4.

    (vi)

    The graph cuts the \(x\)-axis at one point and touches it at two more points. Altogether it meets the \(x\)-axis at three points.

    ∴ Number of zeroes = 3.

    (i) No zeroes   (ii) 1   (iii) 3   (iv) 2   (v) 4   (vi) 3

    Points where the graph crosses the \(y\)-axis, and the hills and valleys of the curve, are not counted. Also note what the answers tell you about the degree: a polynomial of degree \(n\) has at most \(n\) zeroes, so the polynomial in (v) must have degree 4 or more.

    Exercise 2.2

    2 questions · zeroes of a quadratic polynomial and their relationship with the coefficients

    Q1

    Find the zeroes of the following quadratic polynomials and verify the relationship between the zeroes and the coefficients.

    1. \(x^2-2x-8\)
    2. \(4s^2-4s+1\)
    3. \(6x^2-3-7x\)
    4. \(4u^2+8u\)
    5. \(t^2-15\)
    6. \(3x^2-x-4\)
    Solution

    Method: factorise by splitting the middle term (find two numbers whose sum is \(b\) and whose product is \(a\times c\)), and make each factor zero. Then verify, for \(ax^2+bx+c\), that sum of zeroes \(=-\dfrac ba\) and product of zeroes \(=\dfrac ca\). In words, \(-\dfrac ba\) is “minus the coefficient of \(x\), divided by the coefficient of \(x^2\)”, and \(\dfrac ca\) is “the constant term divided by the coefficient of \(x^2\)”. In each check, work out the two sides separately and see that they agree.

    (i)

    \(x^2-2x-8\). We need two numbers with sum \(-2\) and product \(-8\): they are \(-4\) and \(2\).

    $$\begin{aligned} &x^2-2x-8\\ &=x^2-4x+2x-8\\ &=x(x-4)+2(x-4)\\ &=(x-4)(x+2) \end{aligned}$$

    The value of the polynomial is zero when \(x-4=0\) or \(x+2=0\), that is, when \(x=4\) or \(x=-2\).

    ∴ The zeroes of \(x^2-2x-8\) are \(-2\) and \(4\).

    Verification. Here \(a=1,\ b=-2,\ c=-8\).

    Sum of zeroes \(=-2+4=2\), and \(\dfrac{-(\text{coefficient of }x)}{\text{coefficient of }x^2}=\dfrac{-(-2)}{1}=2\). ✓

    Product of zeroes \(=(-2)\times4=-8\), and \(\dfrac{\text{constant term}}{\text{coefficient of }x^2}=\dfrac{-8}{1}=-8\). ✓

    (ii)

    \(4s^2-4s+1\). Sum \(-4\), product \(4\times1=4\): the numbers are \(-2\) and \(-2\).

    $$\begin{aligned} &4s^2-4s+1\\ &=4s^2-2s-2s+1\\ &=2s(2s-1)-1(2s-1)\\ &=(2s-1)(2s-1)=(2s-1)^2 \end{aligned}$$

    The value is zero when \(2s-1=0\), that is, when \(s=\dfrac12\). Both factors give the same value.

    ∴ The zeroes of \(4s^2-4s+1\) are \(\dfrac12\) and \(\dfrac12\) (two equal zeroes).

    Verification. Here \(a=4,\ b=-4,\ c=1\).

    Sum of zeroes \(=\dfrac12+\dfrac12=1\), and \(-\dfrac ba=\dfrac{-(-4)}{4}=1\). ✓

    Product of zeroes \(=\dfrac12\times\dfrac12=\dfrac14\), and \(\dfrac ca=\dfrac14\). ✓

    (iii)

    First write the polynomial in standard order: \(6x^2-3-7x=6x^2-7x-3\). Sum \(-7\), product \(6\times(-3)=-18\): the numbers are \(-9\) and \(2\).

    $$\begin{aligned} &6x^2-7x-3\\ &=6x^2-9x+2x-3\\ &=3x(2x-3)+1(2x-3)\\ &=(3x+1)(2x-3) \end{aligned}$$

    The value is zero when \(3x+1=0\) or \(2x-3=0\), that is, when \(x=-\dfrac13\) or \(x=\dfrac32\).

    ∴ The zeroes of \(6x^2-7x-3\) are \(-\dfrac13\) and \(\dfrac32\).

    Verification. Here \(a=6,\ b=-7,\ c=-3\).

    Sum of zeroes \(=-\dfrac13+\dfrac32=\dfrac{-2+9}{6}=\dfrac76\), and \(-\dfrac ba=\dfrac{-(-7)}{6}=\dfrac76\). ✓

    Product of zeroes \(=\left(-\dfrac13\right)\times\dfrac32=-\dfrac12\), and \(\dfrac ca=\dfrac{-3}{6}=-\dfrac12\). ✓

    (iv)

    \(4u^2+8u\) has no constant term, so take the common factor out:

    $$4u^2+8u=4u(u+2)$$

    The value is zero when \(4u=0\) or \(u+2=0\), that is, when \(u=0\) or \(u=-2\).

    ∴ The zeroes of \(4u^2+8u\) are \(-2\) and \(0\).

    Verification. Write \(4u^2+8u=4u^2+8u+0\), so \(a=4,\ b=8,\ c=0\).

    Sum of zeroes \(=-2+0=-2\), and \(-\dfrac ba=\dfrac{-8}{4}=-2\). ✓

    Product of zeroes \(=(-2)\times0=0\), and \(\dfrac ca=\dfrac04=0\). ✓

    (v)

    \(t^2-15\) has no middle term. Use \(a^2-b^2=(a-b)(a+b)\) with \(15=\left(\sqrt{15}\right)^2\):

    $$\begin{aligned} &t^2-15\\ &=t^2-\left(\sqrt{15}\right)^2\\ &=\left(t-\sqrt{15}\right)\left(t+\sqrt{15}\right) \end{aligned}$$

    The value is zero when \(t=\sqrt{15}\) or \(t=-\sqrt{15}\).

    ∴ The zeroes of \(t^2-15\) are \(-\sqrt{15}\) and \(\sqrt{15}\).

    Verification. Write \(t^2-15=t^2+0t-15\), so \(a=1,\ b=0,\ c=-15\).

    Sum of zeroes \(=-\sqrt{15}+\sqrt{15}=0\), and \(-\dfrac ba=\dfrac{-0}{1}=0\). ✓

    Product of zeroes \(=\left(-\sqrt{15}\right)\left(\sqrt{15}\right)=-15\), and \(\dfrac ca=\dfrac{-15}{1}=-15\). ✓

    (vi)

    \(3x^2-x-4\). Sum \(-1\), product \(3\times(-4)=-12\): the numbers are \(-4\) and \(3\).

    $$\begin{aligned} &3x^2-x-4\\ &=3x^2-4x+3x-4\\ &=x(3x-4)+1(3x-4)\\ &=(3x-4)(x+1) \end{aligned}$$

    The value is zero when \(3x-4=0\) or \(x+1=0\), that is, when \(x=\dfrac43\) or \(x=-1\).

    ∴ The zeroes of \(3x^2-x-4\) are \(-1\) and \(\dfrac43\).

    Verification. Here \(a=3,\ b=-1,\ c=-4\).

    Sum of zeroes \(=-1+\dfrac43=\dfrac13\), and \(-\dfrac ba=\dfrac{-(-1)}{3}=\dfrac13\). ✓

    Product of zeroes \(=(-1)\times\dfrac43=-\dfrac43\), and \(\dfrac ca=\dfrac{-4}{3}=-\dfrac43\). ✓

    (i) \(-2,\ 4\)   (ii) \(\dfrac12,\ \dfrac12\)   (iii) \(-\dfrac13,\ \dfrac32\)   (iv) \(-2,\ 0\)   (v) \(-\sqrt{15},\ \sqrt{15}\)   (vi) \(-1,\ \dfrac43\)

    The graph of each polynomial agrees with these answers. Two of them are shown here.

    Q2

    Find a quadratic polynomial each with the given numbers as the sum and product of its zeroes respectively.

    1. \(\dfrac14,\ -1\)
    2. \(\sqrt2,\ \dfrac13\)
    3. \(0,\ \sqrt5\)
    4. \(1,\ 1\)
    5. \(-\dfrac14,\ \dfrac14\)
    6. \(4,\ 1\)
    Solution

    Formula: if \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) are the zeroes, a quadratic polynomial is \(p(x)=k\left[x^2-(\alpha+\beta)x+\alpha\beta\right]\), where \(k\) is any non-zero real number. In each part the first number is \(\alpha+\beta\) and the second is \(\alpha\beta\). We choose \(k\) so that no fractions are left.

    (i)

    \(\alpha+\beta=\dfrac14,\quad \alpha\beta=-1\).

    $$\begin{aligned} p(x)&=k\left[x^2-\frac14x+(-1)\right]\\ &=k\left[x^2-\frac x4-1\right] \end{aligned}$$

    Taking \(k=4\): \(p(x)=4x^2-x-4\).

    Check: \(-\dfrac ba=\dfrac{-(-1)}{4}=\dfrac14\) and \(\dfrac ca=\dfrac{-4}{4}=-1\). ✓

    (ii)

    \(\alpha+\beta=\sqrt2,\quad \alpha\beta=\dfrac13\).

    $$p(x)=k\left[x^2-\sqrt2\,x+\frac13\right]$$

    Taking \(k=3\): \(p(x)=3x^2-3\sqrt2\,x+1\).

    Check: \(-\dfrac ba=\dfrac{3\sqrt2}{3}=\sqrt2\) and \(\dfrac ca=\dfrac13\). ✓

    (iii)

    \(\alpha+\beta=0,\quad \alpha\beta=\sqrt5\).

    $$\begin{aligned} p(x)&=k\left[x^2-0\cdot x+\sqrt5\right]\\ &=k\left[x^2+\sqrt5\right] \end{aligned}$$

    Taking \(k=1\): \(p(x)=x^2+\sqrt5\).

    Check: \(-\dfrac ba=\dfrac{-0}{1}=0\) and \(\dfrac ca=\dfrac{\sqrt5}{1}=\sqrt5\). ✓

    (iv)

    \(\alpha+\beta=1,\quad \alpha\beta=1\).

    $$\begin{aligned} p(x)&=k\left[x^2-1\cdot x+1\right]\\ &=k\left[x^2-x+1\right] \end{aligned}$$

    Taking \(k=1\): \(p(x)=x^2-x+1\).

    Check: \(-\dfrac ba=\dfrac{-(-1)}{1}=1\) and \(\dfrac ca=\dfrac11=1\). ✓

    (v)

    \(\alpha+\beta=-\dfrac14,\quad \alpha\beta=\dfrac14\).

    $$\begin{aligned} p(x)&=k\left[x^2-\left(-\frac14\right)x+\frac14\right]\\ &=k\left[x^2+\frac x4+\frac14\right] \end{aligned}$$

    Taking \(k=4\): \(p(x)=4x^2+x+1\).

    Check: \(-\dfrac ba=-\dfrac14\) and \(\dfrac ca=\dfrac14\). ✓

    (vi)

    \(\alpha+\beta=4,\quad \alpha\beta=1\).

    $$p(x)=k\left[x^2-4x+1\right]$$

    Taking \(k=1\): \(p(x)=x^2-4x+1\).

    Check: \(-\dfrac ba=\dfrac{-(-4)}{1}=4\) and \(\dfrac ca=\dfrac11=1\). ✓

    (i) \({4x^2-x-4}\)   (ii) \({3x^2-3\sqrt2\,x+1}\)   (iii) \({x^2+\sqrt5}\)   (iv) \({x^2-x+1}\)   (v) \({4x^2+x+1}\)   (vi) \({x^2-4x+1}\)

    Each answer is a polynomial, not the polynomial: multiplying by any non-zero constant \(k\) keeps the sum and the product of the zeroes the same, so \(8x^2-2x-8\) is also correct for (i). The forms above are the simplest ones and are the ones printed in the book’s answers. Remember the minus sign before the sum, and do not write “\(=0\)” after the polynomial.

    You are not asked to find the zeroes here. In parts (iii), (iv) and (v) you could not: no two real numbers have these sums and products. For example, in (iv), \((\alpha-\beta)^2=(\alpha+\beta)^2-4\alpha\beta=1-4=-3\), and the square of a real number cannot be negative. The graphs of these three polynomials do not meet the \(x\)-axis. The polynomial asked for is still built in exactly the same way.

    3. Verify the relationship between the zeroes and the coefficients of \(P(x)\). 2ORThe profit is greatest for the value of \(x\) that lies midway between the two zeroes. Find this value of \(x\) and the greatest profit.
    Answer & solution
    (i)

    The graph cuts the \(x\)-axis at \(x=2\) and \(x=12\), so the zeroes are 2 and 12. At these points \(P(x)=0\): the workshop makes neither profit nor loss when it sells 200 bags or 1200 bags. Between them it makes a profit.

    (ii)

    \(P(0)=-24\). If no bag is sold, the workshop loses ₹ 24,000 in the month (rent, wages and other fixed costs).

    (iii)

    \(a=-1,\ b=14,\ c=-24\).

    Sum of zeroes \(=2+12=14\), and \(-\dfrac ba=-\dfrac{14}{-1}=14\). ✓

    Product of zeroes \(=2\times12=24\), and \(\dfrac ca=\dfrac{-24}{-1}=24\). ✓

    OR: Midway between the zeroes: \(x=\dfrac{2+12}{2}=7\). \(P(7)=-49+98-24=25\). The greatest profit is ₹ 25,000, earned when 700 bags are sold.

    Case study4 marks

    The park fountain. In a park fountain two jets of water leave the same nozzle at ground level. Take the nozzle as the origin and the ground as the \(x\)-axis (1 unit = 1 metre). Jet A follows the graph of \(f(x)=-x^2+4x\) and jet B follows the graph of \(g(x)=-\dfrac12x^2+3x\).

    1. Find the zeroes of \(f(x)\). 1
    2. How far from the nozzle does jet B fall back on the ground? 1
    3. Each jet is highest midway between its two zeroes. Which jet rises higher, and by how much? 2ORA third jet from the same nozzle has to land 8 m away and pass through the point \((2,3)\). Find the quadratic polynomial \(h(x)\) whose graph it follows.
    Answer & solution
    (i)

    \(f(x)=-x^2+4x=-x(x-4)\). The zeroes are 0 and 4.

    (ii)

    \(g(x)=-\dfrac12x^2+3x=-\dfrac12x(x-6)\). Its zeroes are 0 (the nozzle) and 6. Jet B falls back on the ground 6 m from the nozzle.

    (iii)

    Jet A is highest at \(x=\dfrac{0+4}{2}=2\): \(f(2)=-4+8=4\) m.

    Jet B is highest at \(x=\dfrac{0+6}{2}=3\): \(g(3)=-\dfrac92+9=4.5\) m.

    Jet B rises higher, by \(4.5-4=0.5\) m.

    OR: The third jet starts at the nozzle and lands 8 m away, so its zeroes are 0 and 8: \(h(x)=kx(x-8)\). It passes through \((2,3)\):

    $$\begin{aligned}h(2)&=k(2)(2-8)=-12k\\-12k&=3\ \Rightarrow\ k=-\frac14\\h(x)&=-\frac14x(x-8)\\&=-\frac14x^2+2x\end{aligned}$$

    Polynomials

    The basics

    • Degree = highest power of \(x\): linear 1, quadratic 2, cubic 3
    • Value at \(x=k\): replace \(x\) by \(k\) to get \(p(k)\)
    • \(k\) is a zero of \(p(x)\) if \(p(k)=0\)
    • Zero of \(ax+b\) is \(-\frac ba\)

    Zeroes on a graph

    • Zero = \(x\)-coordinate of a point where the graph meets the \(x\)-axis
    • Straight line: exactly 1 zero. Parabola: 2, 1 or 0 zeroes
    • \(a\gt0\): parabola opens upwards; \(a\lt0\): downwards
    • Degree \(n\) → at most \(n\) zeroes

    Zeroes and coefficients

    • For \(ax^2+bx+c\): \(\ \alpha+\beta=-\frac ba\)
    • \(\alpha\beta=\frac ca\)
    • Works for surd coefficients too
    • “Verify” = work out both sides and show they match

    Building a polynomial

    • \(x^2-(\text{sum})x+\text{product}\)
    • Multiply every term to clear fractions
    • \(k(\ldots)\) with \(k\ne0\): infinitely many answers
    • Zeroes \(p\pm\sqrt q\): sum \(2p\), product \(p^2-q\)

    Expressions in \(\alpha,\ \beta\)

    • \(\alpha^2+\beta^2=(\alpha+\beta)^2-2\alpha\beta\)
    • \(\frac1\alpha+\frac1\beta=\frac{\alpha+\beta}{\alpha\beta}\)
    • \((\alpha-\beta)^2=(\alpha+\beta)^2-4\alpha\beta\)
    • \(\alpha^3+\beta^3=(\alpha+\beta)^3-3\alpha\beta(\alpha+\beta)\)

    Finding \(k\)

    • A zero is given → put it in: \(p(\text{zero})=0\)
    • Reciprocal zeroes → product = 1 → \(c=a\)
    • Equal and opposite zeroes → sum = 0 → \(b=0\)
    • One zero double the other → take \(\alpha\) and \(2\alpha\)

    Formulas and facts to remember

    • Zero of a polynomial\(k\) is a zero of \(p(x)\) if \(p(k)=0\). On the graph of \(y=p(x)\) it is the \(x\)-coordinate of a point where the graph meets the \(x\)-axis.
    • Linear polynomial\(ax+b\) has exactly one zero, \(-\dfrac ba\). Its graph is a straight line.
    • How many zeroes?Quadratic: at most 2. Cubic: at most 3. Degree \(n\): at most \(n\).
    • Sum of zeroes (quadratic)For \(ax^2+bx+c\): \(\ \alpha+\beta=-\dfrac ba\), that is, \(\dfrac{-(\text{coefficient of }x)}{\text{coefficient of }x^2}\)
    • Product of zeroes (quadratic)\(\alpha\beta=\dfrac ca\), that is, \(\dfrac{\text{constant term}}{\text{coefficient of }x^2}\)
    • Polynomial from its zeroes\(k\,\bigl[x^2-(\alpha+\beta)x+\alpha\beta\bigr]\), where \(k\) is any non-zero real number
    • Shape of the graph\(y=ax^2+bx+c\) is a parabola: it opens upwards if \(a\gt0\) and downwards if \(a\lt0\). It cuts the \(y\)-axis at \((0,\,c)\).
    • Cubic polynomial — for reference only; the board asks the quadratic caseFor \(ax^3+bx^2+cx+d\): \(\ \alpha+\beta+\gamma=-\dfrac ba\), \(\ \alpha\beta+\beta\gamma+\gamma\alpha=\dfrac ca\), \(\ \alpha\beta\gamma=-\dfrac da\)

    Mistakes that cost marks

    • Writing \(\alpha+\beta=\dfrac ba\).The sum is \(-\dfrac ba\); only the product \(\dfrac ca\) has no minus sign.
    • Reading \(b=7\) and \(c=6\) from \(3x^2-7x-6\).Coefficients carry their signs: \(b=-7,\ c=-6\). Write \(a, b, c\) down first.
    • Forming the polynomial as \(x^2+(\text{sum})x+\text{product}\).It is \(x^2-(\text{sum})x+\text{product}\).
    • Clearing fractions in some terms only: \(x^2+\frac52x-3\to2x^2+5x-3\).Multiply every term: \(2x^2+5x-6\).
    • Counting the point on the \(y\)-axis, or the turning points, as zeroes.Count only the points where the graph meets the \(x\)-axis. A point where it just touches counts once.
    • Cancelling \(x\) in \(3x^2-12x\) and losing the zero 0.Take \(x\) common: \(3x(x-4)\) gives the zeroes 0 and 4.
    • In “verify the relationship”, stopping after finding the zeroes.Show sum \(=-\frac ba\) and product \(=\frac ca\) with actual numbers — that part carries marks.
    • Saying a quadratic polynomial always has two zeroes.It has at most two: its graph may cut the \(x\)-axis twice, touch it once or miss it.

    What the board usually asks

    Polynomials is part of Unit II, Algebra (20 marks for four chapters). This chapter usually brings about 4 to 6 marks. A typical paper has:

    • 1–2 MCQs: number of zeroes from a graph (Q5, Q6), sum and product of zeroes (Q12), find \(k\) (Q11, Q16, Q18), polynomial from given zeroes (Q21).
    • Sometimes an assertion–reason item on “at most two zeroes” — see Q28.
    • One 2- or 3-mark question: find the zeroes and verify the relationship (Q45, surd type Q46), an expression in \(\alpha, \beta\) (Q43, Q51), or a polynomial with changed zeroes (Q48).
    • Sometimes a case study on a parabola — a bridge arch, the path of a ball, a profit curve — see Q64–Q68.

    The division algorithm for polynomials is no longer in the syllabus, so no question asks you to divide one polynomial by another.
