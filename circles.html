Chapter 10 Circles

A line can miss a circle, cut it, or just touch it. That last case — the tangent — hides two short theorems that the board asks again and again, and both of them can be seen in the wheel of a moving bicycle.

    10.1 Introduction

    A circle is an old friend from Class IX: all the points of a plane that lie at one fixed distance from one fixed point. This chapter asks a new question about it — what happens when a straight line and a circle are drawn in the same plane?

    Everything you need from Class IX, in one line each.

    Circle: the set of all points in a plane at a constant distance (the radius) from a fixed point (the centre).

    Chord: a segment joining two points of the circle. The longest chord is the diameter, and it passes through the centre.

    Arc, segment, sector: a piece of the circle, the region between a chord and its arc, and the region between two radii and their arc.

    Two facts we will use: the perpendicular drawn from the centre to a chord bisects the chord; and equal chords of a circle are equidistant from the centre.

    A line and a circle: only three things can happen

    Draw a circle on paper. Now take a ruler and lay a line \(PQ\) across the page, in any position you like. Slide it slowly towards the circle and watch how many points the line and the circle share.

    Definitions

    Let a line and a circle lie in the same plane.

    (i) If they have no common point, the line is called a non-intersecting line with respect to the circle.

    (ii) If they have two common points, the line is called a secant of the circle.

    (iii) If they have exactly one common point, the line is called a tangent to the circle. That single shared point is the point of contact, and the tangent is said to touch the circle there.

    • Non-intersecting

      Distance of the line from the centre is more than the radius. Common points: 0.

    • Secant

      Distance of the line from the centre is less than the radius. Common points: 2 — and the part of the line inside the circle is a chord.

    • Tangent

      Distance of the line from the centre is exactly the radius. Common points: 1, the point of contact.

    So the whole story is told by one number: the distance \(d\) of the line from the centre, compared with the radius \(r\).

    $$d \gt r\ \Rightarrow\ \text{no common point},\qquad d \lt r\ \Rightarrow\ \text{secant},\qquad d = r\ \Rightarrow\ \text{tangent}.$$

    There is no fourth case, because \(d\) is either bigger than \(r\), smaller than \(r\), or equal to \(r\).

    Tangents are not only a classroom idea. The rope on both sides of a pulley leaves the wheel along two tangents. A bicycle wheel rolls along the road, touching it at one point at a time — the road is a tangent to the wheel. The word tangent itself comes from the Latin tangere, “to touch”.

    A circle has radius 8 cm. A straight line is drawn 8 cm away from the centre. How many points does it share with the circle, and what is such a line called?

    Show answer

    Here \(d = r = 8\) cm, so the line touches the circle at exactly one point. It is a tangent, and the shared point is the point of contact.

    10.2 Tangent to a circle

    This section does two things: it convinces you that a tangent really exists at every point of a circle, and it proves the one property of a tangent that almost every question in this chapter uses.

    Definition

    A tangent to a circle is a line that intersects the circle at only one point. That point is the point of contact.

    Activity 1 — turn a line about a point of the circle

    Bend a wire into a circle and fix a second, straight wire \(AB\) so that it touches the circular wire at a point \(P\) and can turn freely about \(P\). Now rotate \(AB\) slowly and look at where else it meets the circle.

    As the line turns, the second point creeps closer and closer to \(P\), and for one particular position it coincides with \(P\). Turn the line a little further and a second point appears again, on the other side. So:

    At a given point of a circle there is one and only one tangent.

    Activity 2 — slide a secant across the circle

    Draw a circle and a secant \(PQ\). Now draw line after line parallel to \(PQ\), on both sides of it, and measure the chord that each one cuts off.

    This activity gives two results at once.

    1. A tangent is the special case of a secant in which the two end points of the chord coincide.

    2. A circle can have at most two parallel tangents, and they are at the two ends of the diameter perpendicular to that direction.

    The wheel on the road

    Look at the wheel of a moving bicycle. The rim is a circle, the spokes are radii, and the road is a tangent to that circle. Notice where the spoke through the point of contact points: straight down, at right angles to the road. That is not an accident — it is the next theorem.

    Theorem 10.1The tangent is perpendicular to the radius

    The tangent at any point of a circle is perpendicular to the radius through the point of contact.

    Proof (asked in the board exam — learn it)

    Given: A circle with centre \(O\) and a tangent \(XY\) to the circle, touching it at the point \(P\).

    To prove: \(OP\perp XY\).

    Construction: Take any point \(Q\) on \(XY\) other than \(P\), and join \(OQ\).

    Proof: The point \(Q\) lies outside the circle. [If \(Q\) were inside the circle, the line \(XY\) would cross the circle at two points and would be a secant, not a tangent]

    Since \(Q\) is outside the circle, its distance from the centre is greater than the radius:

    $$OQ \gt OP \qquad \text{[}OP\text{ is the radius]}$$

    The point \(Q\) was any point of \(XY\) other than \(P\). So the same is true for every such point, and therefore

    \(OP\) is the shortest of all the segments joining \(O\) to the points of \(XY\).

    But of all the segments drawn from a point to a line, the perpendicular is the shortest.

    Hence \(OP\) is the perpendicular from \(O\) to \(XY\), that is, \(OP\perp XY\). Proved.

    Two remarks the book adds after the theorem.

    1. At any point of a circle there is one and only one tangent. (If two different lines through \(P\) were both tangents, both would be perpendicular to \(OP\) at \(P\) — impossible, since only one perpendicular can be drawn to a line at a given point on it.)

    2. The line containing the radius through the point of contact is called the normal to the circle at that point.

    In nine questions out of ten the very first line of your solution should be: join the centre to the point of contact. That single segment gives you a right angle, and a right angle gives you Pythagoras, trigonometry and the angle sum of a triangle — everything you need.

    The radius is perpendicular to the tangent only at the point of contact. In the figure above \(OQ\) is not perpendicular to \(XY\), and \(OQ\) is not a radius either. Do not mark a right angle anywhere except at the point where the line touches.

    “Prove that the tangent at any point of a circle is perpendicular to the radius through the point of contact” is a straight 2–3 mark question. Write all four headings — Given, To prove, Construction, Proof — and draw the figure with \(Q\) marked. The single mark that students lose most often is for the sentence explaining why \(Q\) lies outside the circle.

    \(O\) is the centre of a circle and \(AB\) is a tangent touching it at \(T\). If \(OT = 7\) cm, what is the distance of \(O\) from the line \(AB\)?

    Show answer

    By Theorem 10.1, \(OT\perp AB\), so \(OT\) is the perpendicular distance from \(O\) to \(AB\). The distance is 7 cm — the radius itself.

    10.3 Number of tangents from a point on a circle

    Section 10.2 fixed the point on the circle. Now let the point be anywhere in the plane — inside the circle, on it, or outside it — and count how many tangents can be drawn through it. The answer, and the theorem that follows it, are the most-asked pieces of this chapter.

    Activity 3 — move the point

    Draw a circle, mark a point \(P\), and try to draw a tangent to the circle through \(P\). Do it three times, with \(P\) in three different places.

    Case 1 — \(P\) inside

    No tangent passes through \(P\). Every line drawn through an inside point crosses the circle at two points, so it is always a secant.

    Number of tangents: 0

    Case 2 — \(P\) on the circle

    Exactly one tangent passes through \(P\) — the line through \(P\) perpendicular to the radius \(OP\).

    Number of tangents: 1

    Case 3 — \(P\) outside

    Exactly two tangents can be drawn from \(P\). They touch the circle at two different points.

    Number of tangents: 2

    Definition — length of a tangent

    Let \(P\) be a point outside a circle and let a tangent from \(P\) touch the circle at \(T\). The length of the segment \(PT\), from the external point to the point of contact, is called the length of the tangent from \(P\) to the circle.

    It is a segment that has a length; the tangent itself is a line and goes on for ever.

    If \(P\) is outside a circle of centre \(O\) and radius \(r\), the two tangent lengths are equal and are found in one step from Theorem 10.1 with Pythagoras:

    $$PT=\sqrt{OP^{2}-r^{2}}$$

    because \(\triangle OTP\) is right-angled at \(T\). Remember this — it turns most numerical questions of this chapter into one line.

    Theorem 10.2Tangents from an external point are equal

    The lengths of tangents drawn from an external point to a circle are equal.

    Proof (a board favourite — learn it)

    Given: A circle with centre \(O\), a point \(P\) lying outside the circle, and two tangents \(PQ\) and \(PR\) drawn from \(P\), touching the circle at \(Q\) and \(R\).

    To prove: \(PQ = PR\).

    Construction: Join \(OP\), \(OQ\) and \(OR\).

    Proof: \(OQ\) is the radius through the point of contact \(Q\) of the tangent \(PQ\), and \(OR\) is the radius through the point of contact \(R\) of the tangent \(PR\). So, by Theorem 10.1,

    $$\angle OQP = 90^\circ \quad\text{and}\quad \angle ORP = 90^\circ.$$

    Therefore \(\triangle OQP\) and \(\triangle ORP\) are right triangles. In these two triangles,

    $$\begin{aligned} OQ &= OR &&\text{[radii of the same circle]}\\ OP &= OP &&\text{[common hypotenuse]} \end{aligned}$$

    \(\therefore\ \triangle OQP\cong\triangle ORP\) [RHS congruence rule]

    \(\therefore\ PQ = PR\) [CPCT — corresponding parts of congruent triangles] Proved.

    If the question does not insist on congruence, you can finish in two lines. In the right triangles \(OQP\) and \(ORP\),

    $$PQ^{2}=OP^{2}-OQ^{2}=OP^{2}-OR^{2}=PR^{2}$$

    since \(OQ = OR\). Lengths are positive, so \(PQ = PR\).

    One more fact falls out of the same congruence. Because \(\triangle OQP\cong\triangle ORP\), we also get \(\angle OPQ = \angle OPR\). So \(OP\) bisects \(\angle QPR\) — in other words, the centre of the circle lies on the bisector of the angle between the two tangents.

    The kite \(OAPB\) — four facts to carry into the exam

    Whenever two tangents are drawn from an outside point \(P\), touching the circle at \(A\) and \(B\), the four points \(O,\,A,\,P,\,B\) make a kite. Almost every board question about this chapter is one of the four facts of that kite.

    • Right angles

      \(\angle OAP=\angle OBP=90^\circ\) — a radius meets its tangent at the point of contact at \(90^\circ\). [Theorem 10.1]

    • Equal tangents

      \(PA = PB\), so \(\triangle APB\) is always isosceles. [Theorem 10.2]

    • \(OP\) bisects both angles

      \(\angle APO=\angle BPO\) and \(\angle AOP=\angle BOP\), and \(OP\perp AB\).

    • Supplementary angles

      \(\angle APB+\angle AOB=180^\circ\), because the four angles of quadrilateral \(OAPB\) add to \(360^\circ\) and two of them are \(90^\circ\).
    NCERT Example 1concentric circles

    Two circles have the same centre. A chord of the bigger circle touches the smaller circle. Prove that the point of contact is the midpoint of that chord.

    Solution

    Given: Two concentric circles \(C_1\) (the bigger one) and \(C_2\) (the smaller one) with common centre \(O\), and a chord \(AB\) of \(C_1\) which touches \(C_2\) at \(P\). To prove: \(AP = BP\).

    Construction: Join \(OP\).

    \(AB\) is a tangent to the circle \(C_2\) at the point \(P\), and \(OP\) is the radius of \(C_2\) through \(P\).

    \(\therefore\ OP\perp AB\) [Theorem 10.1: the tangent is perpendicular to the radius at the point of contact]

    Now look at the bigger circle \(C_1\). Here \(AB\) is a chord, \(O\) is the centre, and \(OP\perp AB\).

    \(\therefore\ OP\) bisects \(AB\) [the perpendicular drawn from the centre of a circle to a chord bisects the chord]

    \(\therefore\ AP = BP\)

    Hence proved. The chord is bisected at the point of contact.

    NCERT Example 2angle between tangents

    From a point \(T\) outside a circle with centre \(O\), two tangents \(TP\) and \(TQ\) are drawn. Prove that \(\angle PTQ = 2\,\angle OPQ\).

    Solution

    Given: A circle with centre \(O\), an external point \(T\), and tangents \(TP\) and \(TQ\) with points of contact \(P\) and \(Q\). To prove: \(\angle PTQ = 2\,\angle OPQ\).

    Let \(\angle PTQ = \theta\).

    \(TP = TQ\) [Theorem 10.2: tangents from an external point are equal]

    So \(\triangle TPQ\) is isosceles, and the angles opposite the equal sides are equal:

    $$\angle TPQ = \angle TQP$$

    The three angles of \(\triangle TPQ\) add up to \(180^\circ\), so

    $$\begin{aligned} \angle TPQ+\angle TQP+\theta &= 180^\circ\\ 2\,\angle TPQ &= 180^\circ-\theta\\ \angle TPQ &= 90^\circ-\tfrac12\theta \end{aligned}$$

    Also \(\angle OPT = 90^\circ\) [Theorem 10.1: \(OP\) is the radius through the point of contact \(P\)]

    Now \(\angle OPQ=\angle OPT-\angle TPQ\), so

    $$\begin{aligned} \angle OPQ &= 90^\circ-\left(90^\circ-\tfrac12\theta\right)\\ &= \tfrac12\theta=\tfrac12\,\angle PTQ \end{aligned}$$

    \(\therefore\ \angle PTQ = 2\,\angle OPQ\)

    Hence proved.

    NCERT Example 3length of a tangent

    In a circle of radius 5 cm, \(PQ\) is a chord of length 8 cm. The tangents drawn at \(P\) and at \(Q\) meet at the point \(T\). Find the length of \(TP\).

    Solution

    Given: \(OP = OQ = 5\) cm, \(PQ = 8\) cm, and \(TP,\ TQ\) are tangents. To find: \(TP\).

    Construction: Join \(OT\); let it cut the chord \(PQ\) at \(R\).

    \(TP = TQ\) [Theorem 10.2], so \(\triangle TPQ\) is isosceles, and \(TO\) is the bisector of \(\angle PTQ\) [the centre lies on the bisector of the angle between the tangents].

    In an isosceles triangle the bisector of the vertical angle is also the perpendicular bisector of the base, so

    $$OT\perp PQ \qquad\text{and}\qquad PR=RQ=\tfrac12\times 8=4\ \text{cm}$$

    Step 1 — find \(OR\). In right \(\triangle OPR\), right-angled at \(R\):

    $$\begin{aligned} OR&=\sqrt{OP^{2}-PR^{2}}\\ &=\sqrt{5^{2}-4^{2}}=\sqrt{25-16}\\ &=\sqrt9=3\ \text{cm}\end{aligned}$$

    Step 2 — find \(TP\) by similar triangles. In \(\triangle TRP\) and \(\triangle PRO\):

    $$\angle TRP=\angle PRO=90^\circ$$

    Also \(\angle TPR+\angle RPO=\angle TPO=90^\circ\) [Theorem 10.1] and \(\angle TPR+\angle PTR=90^\circ\) [angle sum of right \(\triangle TPR\)], so

    $$\angle RPO=\angle PTR$$

    \(\therefore\ \triangle TRP\sim\triangle PRO\) [AA similarity]

    Corresponding sides are in the same ratio:

    $$\frac{TP}{PO}=\frac{RP}{RO}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{TP}{5}=\frac43$$ $$TP=\frac{5\times4}{3}=\frac{20}{3}\ \text{cm}$$

    \(TP=\dfrac{20}{3}\ \text{cm}=6\dfrac23\ \text{cm}\)

    Second method (Pythagoras only). Let \(TP=x\) and \(TR=y\). From right \(\triangle PRT\): \(x^{2}=y^{2}+16\). From right \(\triangle OPT\) (right-angled at \(P\), with \(OT=y+3\)): \(x^{2}+5^{2}=(y+3)^{2}\). Subtracting the first from the second gives \(25=6y-7\), so \(y=\frac{16}{3}\); then \(x^{2}=\frac{256}{9}+16=\frac{400}{9}\) and \(x=\frac{20}{3}\) cm — the same answer.

    How this section is asked in the board paper:

    1. 1 mark. “The length of the tangent from a point 13 cm from the centre of a circle of radius 5 cm is …” — one use of \(PT=\sqrt{OP^{2}-r^{2}}\).
    2. 1 mark. “If \(\angle APB=80^\circ\), then \(\angle AOB=\) …” — use \(\angle APB+\angle AOB=180^\circ\).
    3. 2–3 marks. The proof of Theorem 10.2, with Given, To prove, Construction, Proof and a clean figure.
    4. 3–5 marks. A result built on the kite — a quadrilateral drawn around a circle, or a triangle with its incircle.

    Three mistakes that cost marks every year.

    1. Writing \(PT=\sqrt{OP^{2}+r^{2}}\). The hypotenuse of \(\triangle OTP\) is \(OP\), not \(PT\), so the radius is subtracted.

    2. Claiming \(\angle APB=\angle AOB\). They are supplementary, not equal — they are equal only when both are \(90^\circ\).

    3. Using RHS without first saying why the angles at \(Q\) and \(R\) are right angles. That reason is Theorem 10.1, and it carries a mark.

    For the kite \(OAPB\), remember “two rights, two equals, one bisector, one straight angle”: two right angles at \(A\) and \(B\), two equal tangents \(PA=PB\), \(OP\) bisecting the angle at \(P\), and the angles at \(P\) and \(O\) adding to a straight angle, \(180^\circ\).

    The two tangents drawn from a point \(P\) to a circle with centre \(O\) touch it at \(A\) and \(B\), and \(\angle APB=70^\circ\). Find \(\angle AOB\) and \(\angle OAB\).

    Show answer

    \(\angle AOB=180^\circ-70^\circ=110^\circ\). In \(\triangle OAB\), \(OA=OB\) (radii), so \(\angle OAB=\angle OBA=\frac12(180^\circ-110^\circ)=35^\circ\).

    A point \(P\) is 17 cm from the centre of a circle of radius 8 cm. How long is each tangent from \(P\)?

    Show answer

    \(PT=\sqrt{OP^{2}-r^{2}}=\sqrt{17^{2}-8^{2}}=\sqrt{289-64}=\sqrt{225}=15\) cm. Both tangents are 15 cm long, by Theorem 10.2.

    Exercise 10.1

    4 questions · tangent, secant, point of contact and the tangent–radius right angle

    Q1

    How many tangents can a circle have?

    Solution

    A circle is made up of points, and there is exactly one tangent at each of those points [at a point of a circle only one line can be drawn perpendicular to the radius through that point].

    A circle has endlessly many points on it. So it has endlessly many tangents — one for each point.

    A circle can have infinitely many tangents.

    Q2

    Fill in the blanks.

    1. A tangent to a circle meets it in ______ point(s).
    2. A line that meets a circle in two points is called a ______.
    3. A circle can have ______ parallel tangents at the most.
    4. The common point of a tangent and the circle is called the ______.
    Solution
    (i)

    A tangent is defined as a line that meets the circle at only one point.

    one

    (ii)

    When a line meets a circle at two points, the part of it inside the circle is a chord, and the whole line is called a secant.

    secant

    (iii)

    Take any direction. The tangents in that direction are perpendicular to one particular diameter, and there is only one such tangent at each end of that diameter. So a circle has two parallel tangents at the most, and they touch it at the two ends of a diameter.

    two

    (iv)

    The single point that a tangent shares with the circle is where the tangent touches it.

    point of contact

    (i) one   (ii) secant   (iii) two   (iv) point of contact

    Q3

    A tangent \(PQ\) touches a circle of radius 5 cm at the point \(P\), and meets a line drawn through the centre \(O\) at the point \(Q\), where \(OQ = 12\) cm. Then the length of \(PQ\) is:

    1. 12 cm
    2. 13 cm
    3. 8.5 cm
    4. \(\sqrt{119}\) cm
    Solution

    Given: \(OP = 5\) cm (radius), \(OQ = 12\) cm, and \(PQ\) is the tangent at \(P\). To find: \(PQ\).

    \(PQ\) is a tangent to the circle at \(P\), and \(OP\) is the radius through \(P\).

    \(\therefore\ OP\perp PQ\) [Theorem 10.1: the tangent at any point of a circle is perpendicular to the radius through the point of contact]

    So \(\triangle OPQ\) is right-angled at \(P\), with \(OQ\) as its hypotenuse. By the Pythagoras theorem,

    $$\begin{aligned} OQ^{2} &= OP^{2}+PQ^{2}\\ 12^{2} &= 5^{2}+PQ^{2}\\ PQ^{2} &= 144-25=119\\ PQ &= \sqrt{119}\ \text{cm} \end{aligned}$$

    (d) \(\sqrt{119}\) cm (about 10.9 cm)

    Why the other options are wrong: 12 cm is \(OQ\) itself, 13 cm comes from adding \(5^2\) instead of subtracting it \((\sqrt{144+25}=13)\), and 8.5 cm is just the average of 12 and 5 — none of them comes from a correct use of Pythagoras. The hypotenuse here is \(OQ\), so the radius must be subtracted. The book labels the choices (A)–(D) and the official answer is D — the same fourth option.

    Q4

    Draw a circle and two lines parallel to a given line, so that one of them is a tangent to the circle and the other is a secant.

    Solution

    To draw: a circle, and two lines parallel to a given line \(\ell\) — one touching the circle, the other cutting it.

    1. Draw the given line \(\ell\).
    2. Mark a point \(O\) above \(\ell\) and draw a circle with centre \(O\) and any convenient radius \(r\) (say 2.5 cm), keeping the whole circle clear of \(\ell\).
    3. From \(O\) drop a perpendicular to \(\ell\) and extend it backwards to meet the circle at \(X\) on the far side of \(O\).
    4. Through \(X\) draw a line parallel to \(\ell\). Its distance from \(O\) is exactly \(OX = r\), so it meets the circle at the single point \(X\) — this line is the required tangent. (By Theorem 10.1 it is perpendicular to the radius \(OX\), which is why it is parallel to \(\ell\).)
    5. Take any point \(Y\) on the segment \(OX\), so that \(OY \lt r\), and through \(Y\) draw another line parallel to \(\ell\). Its distance from \(O\) is less than the radius, so it meets the circle at two points \(A\) and \(B\) — this line is the required secant.

    The line through \(X\) is the tangent and the line through \(A\) and \(B\) is the secant; both are parallel to \(\ell\).

    The rule behind the construction is the one from Section 10.1: a line at distance \(d\) from the centre is a tangent when \(d = r\) and a secant when \(d \lt r\). Choosing the two distances is all the construction really does.

    Exercise 10.2

    13 questions · Q1–Q3 are multiple choice with justification, the rest are proofs and calculations on tangents

    Q1

    From a point Q outside a circle, the tangent drawn to the circle is 24 cm long, and Q is 25 cm away from the centre. Choose the radius of the circle and justify your choice.

    1. 7 cm
    2. 12 cm
    3. 15 cm
    4. 24.5 cm
    Solution

    Given: a circle with centre O; the tangent from Q touches it at P, with PQ = 24 cm and OQ = 25 cm. To find: the radius OP.

    Join OP and OQ.

    \(OP\perp PQ\) [Theorem 10.1 — the tangent at any point of a circle is perpendicular to the radius through the point of contact]

    So \(\triangle OPQ\) is right-angled at P, and OQ is its hypotenuse. [Pythagoras theorem]

    $$\begin{aligned} OQ^2&=OP^2+PQ^2\\ 25^2&=OP^2+24^2\\ 625&=OP^2+576\\ OP^2&=49\\ OP&=7 \end{aligned}$$

    A length cannot be negative, so we keep the positive root.

    (a) 7 cm

    Why the other options fail. If the radius were 12 cm, then \(OQ=\sqrt{12^2+24^2}=\sqrt{720}\approx26.8\) cm, not 25 cm. If it were 15 cm, \(OQ=\sqrt{801}\approx28.3\) cm. And 24.5 cm is just the average of 24 and 25 — with that radius the tangent would be only \(\sqrt{25^2-24.5^2}=\sqrt{24.75}\approx4.97\) cm, nowhere near 24 cm.

    The book labels the choices (A)–(D) and the official answer is A, the same first option. Remember the triple 7, 24, 25 — it turns up again and again in circle questions.

    Q2

    In the figure below (Fig. 10.11 of the book, re-drawn), TP and TQ are the two tangents drawn from T to a circle with centre O, and \(\angle POQ=110^\circ.\) Choose the value of \(\angle PTQ\) and justify your choice.

    1. \(60^\circ\)
    2. \(70^\circ\)
    3. \(80^\circ\)
    4. \(90^\circ\)
    Solution

    Given: TP and TQ are tangents to the circle with centre O, touching it at P and Q, and \(\angle POQ=110^\circ.\) To find: \(\angle PTQ.\)

    \(OP\perp TP\) and \(OQ\perp TQ\) [Theorem 10.1 — tangent \(\perp\) radius at the point of contact]

    \(\therefore\ \angle OPT=\angle OQT=90^\circ\)

    Now OPTQ is a quadrilateral, so its four angles add up to \(360^\circ.\) Those four angles are \(\angle OPT=90^\circ,\) \(\angle PTQ,\) \(\angle TQO=90^\circ\) and \(\angle QOP=110^\circ.\)

    $$\begin{aligned} 90^\circ+\angle PTQ+90^\circ+110^\circ&=360^\circ\\ \angle PTQ+290^\circ&=360^\circ\\ \angle PTQ&=70^\circ \end{aligned}$$

    (b) \(70^\circ\)

    The book's choices are (A)–(D) and the official answer is B — the same second option.

    Shortcut for the exam. Two right angles always eat up \(180^\circ\) of the quadrilateral, so the angle at the outside point and the angle at the centre are always supplementary:

    $$\begin{aligned} \angle PTQ+\angle POQ&=180^\circ\\ \angle PTQ&=180^\circ-110^\circ=70^\circ \end{aligned}$$

    That is exactly what Q10 of this exercise asks you to prove. Learn it — it answers most one-mark questions on tangents in one line.

    Q3

    Two tangents PA and PB are drawn from a point P to a circle with centre O, and they are inclined to each other at an angle of \(80^\circ.\) Choose the value of \(\angle POA\) and justify your choice.

    1. \(50^\circ\)
    2. \(60^\circ\)
    3. \(70^\circ\)
    4. \(80^\circ\)
    Solution

    Given: PA and PB are tangents from P to the circle with centre O, touching it at A and B, and \(\angle APB=80^\circ.\) To find: \(\angle POA.\)

    Step 1 — the angle at the centre.

    \(\angle OAP=\angle OBP=90^\circ\) [Theorem 10.1 — tangent \(\perp\) radius at the point of contact]

    In quadrilateral OAPB the angle sum is \(360^\circ.\) Its four angles are \(\angle OAP=90^\circ,\) \(\angle APB=80^\circ,\) \(\angle PBO=90^\circ\) and \(\angle BOA.\)

    $$\begin{aligned} 90^\circ+80^\circ+90^\circ+\angle AOB&=360^\circ\\ \angle AOB+260^\circ&=360^\circ\\ \angle AOB&=100^\circ \end{aligned}$$

    Step 2 — OP splits that angle into two equal halves.

    In \(\triangle OAP\) and \(\triangle OBP:\)

    \(OA=OB\) [radii of the same circle]

    \(OP=OP\) [common side]

    \(\angle OAP=\angle OBP=90^\circ\)

    \(\therefore\ \triangle OAP\cong\triangle OBP\) [RHS congruence rule]

    \(\therefore\ \angle AOP=\angle BOP\) [CPCT]

    $$\angle POA=\tfrac12\,\angle AOB=\tfrac12\times100^\circ=50^\circ$$

    (a) \(50^\circ\)

    The book's choices are (A)–(D) and the official answer is A — the same first option.

    A shorter route. The same congruence also gives \(\angle APO=\angle BPO=\tfrac12\times80^\circ=40^\circ.\) Then in right \(\triangle OAP,\)

    $$\begin{aligned} \angle POA&=180^\circ-\angle OAP-\angle APO\\ &=180^\circ-90^\circ-40^\circ=50^\circ \end{aligned}$$

    Note the trap: the question asks for \(\angle POA,\) the angle at the centre in one of the two small triangles — not \(\angle AOB=100^\circ,\) which is the whole angle at the centre. Option (d) \(80^\circ\) is there to catch students who simply copy the given angle.

    Q4

    Prove that the tangents drawn at the two ends of a diameter of a circle are parallel.

    Solution

    Given: a circle with centre O and diameter AB. PQ is the tangent to the circle at A, and RS is the tangent at B.  To prove: \(PQ\parallel RS.\)  Construction: join OA and OB — they lie along the diameter AB.

    Proof.

    PQ is a tangent at A and OA is the radius through A, so

    \(OA\perp PQ\) [Theorem 10.1 — tangent \(\perp\) radius at the point of contact]

    \(\therefore\ \angle OAQ=90^\circ\)

    O lies on the segment AB, so the ray AO is the same as the ray AB. Hence

    \(\angle BAQ=\angle OAQ=90^\circ\)  …(1)

    In the same way, RS is a tangent at B and OB is the radius through B, so

    \(OB\perp RS\ \Rightarrow\ \angle OBR=90^\circ,\) that is, \(\angle ABR=90^\circ\)  …(2)

    Now look at the two lines PQ and RS cut by the transversal AB. The points Q and R lie on opposite sides of AB, so \(\angle BAQ\) and \(\angle ABR\) are a pair of alternate interior angles.

    From (1) and (2), \(\angle BAQ=\angle ABR=90^\circ.\)

    A pair of alternate interior angles is equal, therefore

    \(PQ\parallel RS\) [if a transversal makes a pair of equal alternate interior angles, the two lines are parallel]

    Hence proved.

    Second way of finishing. \(\angle BAQ\) and \(\angle ABS\) are co-interior angles, and \(90^\circ+90^\circ=180^\circ.\) Co-interior angles adding to \(180^\circ\) also make the lines parallel.

    Why "diameter" matters. The proof uses the fact that A, O and B are in one straight line. For two tangents at the ends of an ordinary chord, the radii point in different directions and the tangents meet — they are not parallel.

    Q5

    A tangent touches a circle at a point. Prove that the line drawn perpendicular to the tangent at that point of contact passes through the centre of the circle.

    Solution

    Given: a circle with centre O; XY is a tangent touching it at P; a line \(\ell\) is drawn through P perpendicular to XY.  To prove: \(\ell\) passes through the centre O.  Construction: join OP.

    Proof. This is the converse of Theorem 10.1, and the neat way to prove it is to assume the opposite and reach an absurdity.

    XY is a tangent at P and OP is the radius through P, so

    \(OP\perp XY\) [Theorem 10.1]

    \(\therefore\ \angle OPY=90^\circ\)  …(1)

    Suppose, if possible, that \(\ell\) does not pass through O. Take any point \(O'\) on \(\ell,\) on the same side of XY as O. Since \(\ell\perp XY\) at P,

    \(\angle O'PY=90^\circ\)  …(2)

    From (1) and (2),

    \(\angle O'PY=\angle OPY\)

    Both rays PO and \(PO'\) start at P and lie on the same side of the line XY. Two such rays that make equal angles with the ray PY must be the very same ray.

    \(\therefore\) \(O'\) lies on the ray PO, so \(\ell\) is the line PO itself — and that line does pass through O. This contradicts our supposition.

    The contradiction came from assuming that \(\ell\) misses the centre. Hence the perpendicular drawn to the tangent at the point of contact passes through the centre.

    Hence proved.

    Put together with Theorem 10.1, this says: at the point of contact, the tangent, the radius and the "normal" all know about one another — the line through the point of contact perpendicular to the tangent is exactly the line joining it to the centre. It is the standard trick for locating the centre of a circle from two tangents.

    Q6

    The point A is 5 cm away from the centre of a circle, and the tangent drawn from A to the circle is 4 cm long. Find the radius of the circle.

    Solution

    Given: O is the centre, OA = 5 cm, and the tangent from A touches the circle at B with AB = 4 cm. To find: the radius OB.

    Join OB and OA.

    \(OB\perp AB\) [Theorem 10.1 — tangent \(\perp\) radius at the point of contact]

    So \(\triangle OBA\) is right-angled at B, with OA as the hypotenuse. By Pythagoras,

    $$\begin{aligned} OA^2&=OB^2+AB^2\\ 5^2&=OB^2+4^2\\ 25&=OB^2+16\\ OB^2&=9\\ OB&=3 \end{aligned}$$

    The radius of the circle is 3 cm.

    This matches the official answer. Once again a 3, 4, 5 right triangle: the radius, the tangent and the distance from the centre always sit like this, with the distance from the centre as the longest side.

    Q7

    Two circles with the same centre have radii 5 cm and 3 cm. A chord of the bigger circle touches the smaller circle. Find the length of that chord.

    Solution

    Given: two concentric circles with centre O, of radii 5 cm and 3 cm. AB is a chord of the bigger circle which touches the smaller circle at P.  To find: AB.  Construction: join OA and OP.

    Step 1 — the radius at the point of contact is perpendicular to AB.

    AB touches the smaller circle at P, and OP is the radius of the smaller circle through P, so

    \(OP\perp AB\) [Theorem 10.1]

    Step 2 — that perpendicular bisects the chord.

    For the bigger circle, AB is a chord and OP lies along the perpendicular dropped from the centre O to AB. Therefore

    \(AP=PB\) [the perpendicular from the centre of a circle to a chord bisects the chord]

    Step 3 — Pythagoras in \(\triangle OPA.\)

    \(\triangle OPA\) is right-angled at P, with OA = 5 cm (radius of the bigger circle) and OP = 3 cm (radius of the smaller circle).

    $$\begin{aligned} OA^2&=OP^2+AP^2\\ 5^2&=3^2+AP^2\\ 25&=9+AP^2\\ AP^2&=16\\ AP&=4 \end{aligned}$$

    \(AB=2\,AP=2\times4=8\)

    The chord is 8 cm long.

    This matches the official answer. Useful shape to remember: for radii \(R\) and \(r,\) such a chord is always \(2\sqrt{R^2-r^2}\) long. Here \(2\sqrt{25-9}=8\) cm.

    Q8

    A quadrilateral ABCD is drawn around a circle so that all four of its sides touch the circle (Fig. 10.12 of the book, re-drawn). Prove that

    $$AB+CD=AD+BC$$
    Solution

    Given: a quadrilateral ABCD circumscribing a circle. The circle touches AB at P, BC at Q, CD at R and DA at S.  To prove: \(AB+CD=AD+BC.\)

    The one idea used. Each vertex is a point outside the circle from which two tangents are drawn, so at every vertex a pair of equal tangent lengths appears. [Theorem 10.2 — the lengths of the tangents drawn from an external point to a circle are equal]

    Proof. From A, the tangents are AP and AS; from B, they are BP and BQ; from C, CQ and CR; from D, DR and DS. So

    $$\begin{aligned} AP&=AS &&\dots(1)\\ BP&=BQ &&\dots(2)\\ CR&=CQ &&\dots(3)\\ DR&=DS &&\dots(4) \end{aligned}$$

    Add (1), (2), (3) and (4):

    $$\begin{aligned} &AP+BP+CR+DR\\ &\quad=AS+BQ+CQ+DS \end{aligned}$$

    Now regroup each side so that the pieces of one side of the quadrilateral come together. P lies on AB and R lies on CD, so \(AP+BP=AB\) and \(CR+DR=CD.\) On the right, S lies on AD and Q lies on BC, so \(AS+DS=AD\) and \(BQ+CQ=BC.\) Therefore

    $$\begin{aligned} &(AP+BP)+(CR+DR)\\ &\quad=(AS+DS)+(BQ+CQ)\\ &AB+CD=AD+BC \end{aligned}$$

    Hence proved.

    Said in words: in any quadrilateral that circumscribes a circle, one pair of opposite sides adds up to exactly the same total as the other pair. The converse is true as well, and the board often uses this result to find one unknown side when the other three are given.

    Q9

    In the figure below (Fig. 10.13 of the book, re-drawn), XY and \(X'Y'\) are two parallel tangents to a circle with centre O. A third tangent AB touches the circle at C and meets XY at A and \(X'Y'\) at B. Prove that \(\angle AOB=90^\circ.\)

    Solution

    Given: \(XY\parallel X'Y',\) both tangents to the circle with centre O, touching it at P and Q. AB is another tangent, touching the circle at C, with A on XY and B on \(X'Y'.\)  To prove: \(\angle AOB=90^\circ.\)  Construction: join OP, OQ, OC, OA and OB.

    Step 1 — OA bisects \(\angle PAB.\)

    AP and AC are the two tangents drawn from the point A, so

    \(AP=AC\) [Theorem 10.2 — tangents from an external point are equal]

    In \(\triangle OPA\) and \(\triangle OCA:\)

    \(OP=OC\) [radii of the same circle]

    \(AP=AC\) [proved above]

    \(OA=OA\) [common side]

    \(\therefore\ \triangle OPA\cong\triangle OCA\) [SSS congruence rule]

    \(\therefore\ \angle OAP=\angle OAC\) [CPCT]

    C lies on the segment AB, so the ray AC is the same as the ray AB, and \(\angle OAC\) is \(\angle OAB.\) The two equal parts together make the whole angle \(\angle PAB,\) so

    \(\angle OAB=\tfrac12\,\angle PAB\)  …(1)

    Step 2 — OB bisects \(\angle QBA,\) for exactly the same reason.

    BQ and BC are tangents from B, so \(BQ=BC;\) also \(OQ=OC\) (radii) and OB is common. Hence \(\triangle OQB\cong\triangle OCB\) [SSS], and \(\angle OBQ=\angle OBC\) [CPCT].

    So \(\angle OBA=\tfrac12\,\angle QBA\)  …(2)

    Step 3 — the two whole angles at A and B add up to \(180^\circ.\)

    \(XY\parallel X'Y'\) and AB is a transversal cutting them, so \(\angle PAB\) and \(\angle QBA\) are co-interior angles:

    \(\angle PAB+\angle QBA=180^\circ\) [co-interior (allied) angles on the same side of a transversal]  …(3)

    Step 4 — finish in \(\triangle AOB.\)

    Adding (1) and (2) and using (3),

    $$\begin{aligned} &\angle OAB+\angle OBA\\ &\quad=\tfrac12\,\angle PAB+\tfrac12\,\angle QBA\\ &\quad=\tfrac12\,(\angle PAB+\angle QBA)\\ &\quad=\tfrac12\times180^\circ=90^\circ \end{aligned}$$

    In \(\triangle AOB,\) the three angles add up to \(180^\circ\) [angle sum property of a triangle], so

    $$\begin{aligned} \angle AOB&=180^\circ-(\angle OAB+\angle OBA)\\ &=180^\circ-90^\circ=90^\circ \end{aligned}$$

    Hence proved.

    Another way, using the diameter. By Theorem 10.1, \(OP\perp XY\) and \(OQ\perp X'Y'.\) As \(XY\parallel X'Y',\) the line OP is perpendicular to \(X'Y'\) too, and only one line through O can be perpendicular to \(X'Y'.\) So OP and OQ lie along the same line, i.e. POQ is a straight line and \(\angle POC+\angle COQ=180^\circ.\) The congruences above give \(\angle AOC=\tfrac12\angle POC\) and \(\angle BOC=\tfrac12\angle COQ,\) so \(\angle AOB=\tfrac12\times180^\circ=90^\circ.\)

    Q10

    Two tangents are drawn to a circle from a point outside it. Prove that the angle between these two tangents is supplementary to the angle that the line segment joining the two points of contact subtends at the centre.

    Solution

    Given: a circle with centre O and a point P outside it. PA and PB are the tangents from P, touching the circle at A and B.  To prove: \(\angle APB+\angle AOB=180^\circ.\)  Construction: join OA, OB and AB.

    Proof.

    PA is a tangent at A and OA is the radius through A, so

    \(OA\perp PA\ \Rightarrow\ \angle OAP=90^\circ\) [Theorem 10.1]  …(1)

    PB is a tangent at B and OB is the radius through B, so

    \(OB\perp PB\ \Rightarrow\ \angle OBP=90^\circ\) [Theorem 10.1]  …(2)

    Now OAPB is a quadrilateral, so the sum of its four angles \(\angle OAP,\ \angle APB,\ \angle PBO\) and \(\angle BOA\) is \(360^\circ.\) Putting in the two right angles from (1) and (2),

    $$\begin{aligned} &90^\circ+\angle APB+90^\circ+\angle AOB=360^\circ\\ &\angle APB+\angle AOB=360^\circ-180^\circ\\ &\angle APB+\angle AOB=180^\circ \end{aligned}$$

    The line segment AB joining the points of contact subtends exactly \(\angle AOB\) at the centre, so the angle between the tangents and the angle at the centre are supplementary.

    Hence proved.

    Keep this pair of facts about the "kite" OAPB together: \(\angle APB+\angle AOB=180^\circ,\) and OP bisects both \(\angle APB\) and \(\angle AOB.\) Q2 and Q3 of this exercise are one-line applications of them.

    Q11

    A parallelogram is drawn around a circle so that all four of its sides touch the circle. Prove that the parallelogram must be a rhombus.

    Solution

    Given: a parallelogram ABCD circumscribing a circle; the circle touches AB at P, BC at Q, CD at R and DA at S.  To prove: ABCD is a rhombus.

    Step 1 — the four pairs of equal tangents. [Theorem 10.2 — tangents from an external point are equal]

    $$\begin{aligned} AP&=AS &&\dots(1)\\ BP&=BQ &&\dots(2)\\ CR&=CQ &&\dots(3)\\ DR&=DS &&\dots(4) \end{aligned}$$

    Step 2 — add them and regroup, exactly as in Q8. Adding (1), (2), (3) and (4) gives \(AP+BP+CR+DR=AS+BQ+CQ+DS.\) Since P lies on AB, Q on BC, R on CD and S on DA, the pieces at each vertex join up into whole sides:

    $$\begin{aligned} &(AP+BP)+(CR+DR)\\ &\quad=(AS+DS)+(BQ+CQ)\\ &AB+CD=AD+BC\qquad\dots(5) \end{aligned}$$

    Step 3 — use the fact that ABCD is a parallelogram.

    \(AB=CD\) and \(AD=BC\) [opposite sides of a parallelogram are equal]

    Substituting in (5):

    $$\begin{aligned} AB+AB&=BC+BC\\ 2\,AB&=2\,BC\\ AB&=BC \end{aligned}$$

    Step 4 — conclude.

    So \(AB=BC,\) and with \(AB=CD\) and \(AD=BC\) this gives

    $$AB=BC=CD=DA$$

    All four sides of the parallelogram ABCD are equal, and a parallelogram whose sides are all equal is a rhombus.

    Hence proved.

    The same argument shows that a rectangle that circumscribes a circle has to be a square, and that the circle drawn inside any rhombus touches all four sides. Do not skip Step 3 in the exam: the equal-tangent result alone only gives \(AB+CD=AD+BC,\) which is true for every circumscribing quadrilateral; it is the parallelogram property that turns it into \(AB=BC.\)

    Q12

    A triangle ABC is drawn around a circle of radius 4 cm, so that all three sides touch the circle (Fig. 10.14 of the book, re-drawn). The point of contact D on the side BC divides it into BD = 8 cm and DC = 6 cm. Find the lengths of the sides AB and AC.

    Solution

    Given: the circle with centre O and radius 4 cm touches BC at D, CA at E and AB at F, with BD = 8 cm and DC = 6 cm.  To find: AB and AC.  Construction: join OA, OB, OC and the radii OD, OE, OF.

    Step 1 — name the unknown using equal tangents. [Theorem 10.2 — tangents from an external point are equal]

    From B: \(BF=BD=8\) cm.   From C: \(CE=CD=6\) cm.   From A: let \(AE=AF=x\) cm.

    So the three sides are

    $$\begin{aligned} a&=BC=BD+DC=8+6=14\\ b&=CA=CE+EA=6+x\\ c&=AB=AF+FB=x+8 \end{aligned}$$

    Step 2 — the semi-perimeter.

    $$\begin{aligned} s&=\frac{a+b+c}{2}\\ &=\frac{14+(6+x)+(x+8)}{2}\\ &=\frac{28+2x}{2}=14+x \end{aligned}$$

    From this, \(s-a=x,\quad s-b=8,\quad s-c=6.\)

    Step 3 — area of \(\triangle ABC,\) the first way: three small triangles.

    OD, OE and OF are radii drawn to points of contact, so each is perpendicular to its side [Theorem 10.1]. Joining O to A, B and C cuts \(\triangle ABC\) into \(\triangle OBC,\ \triangle OCA\) and \(\triangle OAB,\) each of height 4 cm:

    $$\begin{aligned} \text{ar}(\triangle ABC)&=\tfrac12\,a\cdot4+\tfrac12\,b\cdot4+\tfrac12\,c\cdot4\\ &=2\,(a+b+c)=4s\\ &=4\,(14+x) \end{aligned}$$

    Step 4 — area of \(\triangle ABC,\) the second way: Heron's formula.

    $$\begin{aligned} \text{ar}(\triangle ABC)&=\sqrt{s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)}\\ &=\sqrt{(14+x)\cdot x\cdot8\cdot6}\\ &=\sqrt{48x\,(14+x)} \end{aligned}$$

    Step 5 — equate the two areas and solve.

    $$\begin{aligned} \sqrt{48x\,(14+x)}&=4\,(14+x)\\ 48x\,(14+x)&=16\,(14+x)^2\\ 48x&=16\,(14+x)\\ 3x&=14+x\\ 2x&=14\\ x&=7 \end{aligned}$$

    Dividing by \((14+x)\) is allowed because \(x\gt 0,\) so \(14+x\ne0.\)

    Step 6 — write the sides.

    $$\begin{aligned} AB&=x+8=7+8=15\\ AC&=x+6=7+6=13 \end{aligned}$$

    AB = 15 cm and AC = 13 cm

    Both agree with the official answer. Check: the sides are 13, 14, 15, so \(s=21\) and \(\text{ar}=\sqrt{21\times7\times6\times8}=\sqrt{7056}=84\) cm². Then the radius of the circle is \(\dfrac{\text{ar}}{s}=\dfrac{84}{21}=4\) cm — exactly the radius given. ✓

    Remember the pattern: for a triangle whose incircle touches BC at D, the tangent lengths are \(BD=s-b,\ CD=s-c,\ AE=AF=s-a.\) Here \(s=21\) gives \(BD=21-13=8\) and \(CD=21-15=6,\) matching the data.

    Q13

    A quadrilateral is drawn around a circle so that all four of its sides touch the circle. Prove that a pair of opposite sides of the quadrilateral subtends supplementary angles at the centre of the circle.

    Solution

    Given: a quadrilateral ABCD circumscribing a circle with centre O; the circle touches AB at P, BC at Q, CD at R and DA at S.  To prove: \(\angle AOB+\angle COD=180^\circ\) and \(\angle BOC+\angle AOD=180^\circ.\)  Construction: join OA, OB, OC, OD and the radii OP, OQ, OR, OS.

    Number the eight angles formed at O as in the figure:

    $$\begin{aligned} \angle1&=\angle AOP, &\quad \angle2&=\angle POB\\ \angle3&=\angle BOQ, &\quad \angle4&=\angle QOC\\ \angle5&=\angle COR, &\quad \angle6&=\angle ROD\\ \angle7&=\angle DOS, &\quad \angle8&=\angle SOA \end{aligned}$$

    Step 1 — four pairs of congruent triangles, one at each vertex.

    In \(\triangle OAP\) and \(\triangle OAS:\)

    \(OP=OS\) [radii of the same circle]

    \(AP=AS\) [Theorem 10.2 — tangents from the external point A]

    \(OA=OA\) [common side]

    \(\therefore\ \triangle OAP\cong\triangle OAS\) [SSS congruence rule]

    \(\therefore\ \angle1=\angle8\) [CPCT]

    In exactly the same way, using the tangents from B, from C and from D:

    $$\angle2=\angle3,\qquad \angle4=\angle5,\qquad \angle6=\angle7$$

    Step 2 — the eight angles fill the whole turn at O.

    $$\begin{aligned} &\angle1+\angle2+\angle3+\angle4\\ &\quad+\angle5+\angle6+\angle7+\angle8=360^\circ \end{aligned}$$

    Replace \(\angle3\) by \(\angle2,\) \(\angle5\) by \(\angle4,\) \(\angle7\) by \(\angle6\) and \(\angle8\) by \(\angle1:\)

    $$\begin{aligned} 2\,(\angle1+\angle2+\angle4+\angle6)&=360^\circ\\ \angle1+\angle2+\angle4+\angle6&=180^\circ \end{aligned}$$

    Call this last equation \((\ast).\)

    Step 3 — read off the two angles subtended by AB and by CD.

    P lies on AB, so \(\angle AOB=\angle1+\angle2.\)   R lies on CD, so \(\angle COD=\angle5+\angle6=\angle4+\angle6.\)

    $$\begin{aligned} &\angle AOB+\angle COD\\ &\quad=(\angle1+\angle2)+(\angle4+\angle6)\\ &\quad=180^\circ\qquad\text{[by }(\ast)] \end{aligned}$$

    Step 4 — the other pair of opposite sides.

    The four angles \(\angle AOB,\ \angle BOC,\ \angle COD,\ \angle AOD\) together make one complete turn about O, so

    $$\begin{aligned} &\angle BOC+\angle AOD\\ &\quad=360^\circ-(\angle AOB+\angle COD)\\ &\quad=360^\circ-180^\circ=180^\circ \end{aligned}$$

    So AB and CD subtend supplementary angles at O, and so do BC and AD.

    Hence proved.

    Do it directly if you prefer: \(\angle BOC=\angle3+\angle4=\angle2+\angle4\) and \(\angle AOD=\angle7+\angle8=\angle6+\angle1,\) whose sum is again \(\angle1+\angle2+\angle4+\angle6=180^\circ.\)

    Q8 and Q13 are the twin results about a quadrilateral that circumscribes a circle: the opposite sides add up equally, and the opposite sides subtend supplementary angles at the centre. Both start from the same first line — tangents from an external point are equal.

    The radius of a circle with centre \(O\) is 7 cm and \(P\) is a point 25 cm from \(O\). The length of the tangent from \(P\) to the circle is

    The length of the tangent drawn from a point \(A\) to a circle with centre \(O\) is 15 cm and \(OA=17\) cm. The radius of the circle is

    4. 32 cm
    Answer & solution

    • \(OP\) bisects \(\angle APB\) and \(\angle AOB\)

    Angles

    • \(\angle APB+\angle AOB=180^\circ\)
    • \(\angle APB=2\,\angle OPA=2\,\angle OAB\)
    • \(OP=2r\Rightarrow\angle APB=60^\circ\), \(\triangle PAB\) equilateral

    Shapes around a circle

    • Quadrilateral: \(AB+CD=BC+AD\)
    • Parallelogram \(\Rightarrow\) rhombus
    • Triangle: perimeter \(=2\times\) (sum of the three tangent lengths)

    Concentric circles

    • Chord of the big circle touching the small one
    • The point of contact bisects that chord
    • Half-chord \(=\sqrt{R^2-r^2}\)

    Results to remember

    • Theorem 10.1The tangent at any point of a circle is perpendicular to the radius through the point of contact.
    • Theorem 10.2The lengths of the tangents drawn from an external point to a circle are equal.
    • Length of a tangent\(PA=\sqrt{OP^2-r^2}\), where \(OP\) is the distance of \(P\) from the centre.
    • Angle pair\(\angle APB+\angle AOB=180^\circ\) for the two tangents from \(P\).
    • Half-angle result\(\angle APB=2\,\angle OAB\) and \(\angle APB=2\,\angle OPA\).
    • Quadrilateral round a circle\(AB+CD=BC+AD\); opposite sides subtend supplementary angles at the centre.
    • Triangle round a circlePerimeter \(=2(x+y+z)\), where \(x, y, z\) are the tangent lengths from \(A\), \(B\), \(C\); also \(x=s-a\), \(y=s-b\), \(z=s-c\).
    • Inscribed circle of a right triangle\(r=\dfrac{a+b-c}{2}\), where \(c\) is the hypotenuse.
    • Concentric circlesA chord of the outer circle touching the inner circle has length \(2\sqrt{R^2-r^2}\) and is bisected at the point of contact.

    Mistakes that cost marks

    • Taking \(\angle AOB=\angle APB\) for the two tangents from \(P\).They add up to \(180^\circ\). Use the quadrilateral \(OAPB\) with its two right angles.
    • Adding or subtracting the lengths instead of using Pythagoras.\(OP\) is the hypotenuse: \(PA=\sqrt{OP^2-r^2}\), never \(OP-r\).
    • Giving the half-chord as the answer in a concentric-circles question.The point of contact bisects the chord — double your answer.
    • Writing \(AB+BC=CD+AD\) for a quadrilateral circumscribing a circle.It is the opposite sides that pair up: \(AB+CD=BC+AD\).
    • Forgetting to halve in \(r=\dfrac{a+b-c}{2}\) for the incircle of a right triangle.\(6+8-10=4\) is twice the radius; the radius is 2 cm.
    • Writing a proof with no reasons — just a chain of statements.Every step needs its reason in brackets: [tangent \(\perp\) radius], [RHS], [CPCT], [radii].
    • Leaving out Given, To prove, Construction and the figure in a theorem question.They carry about a mark on their own in a 3- or 5-mark proof.
    • Saying “a parallelogram circumscribing a circle is a square”.It is a rhombus — the sides become equal, but the angles are not forced to be \(90^\circ\).

    Last-minute checklist

    What the board usually asks

    Circles sits in the Geometry unit and this chapter usually carries 3 to 6 marks in the paper. A typical paper has:

    • One or two MCQs — a tangent-length calculation or an angle between tangents: see Q4, Q9, Q12, Q17.
    • Sometimes an assertion–reason item on Theorem 10.1 or 10.2: see Q21, Q25.
    • A 2-mark numerical: concentric circles, or a side of a quadrilateral circumscribing a circle — Q28, Q31.
    • A 3-mark proof, most often Theorem 10.1 or Theorem 10.2 itself — Q36, Q37 — or the proof of \(AB+CD=BC+AD\) (Q42) or the rhombus result (Q43).
    • A 5-mark question combining a proof with a calculation — Q46, Q47, Q51.
    • A case study built on a wheel, a pond or a park — Q53–Q56.

    The two theorem proofs are the safest marks in the whole paper. Write them out from memory twice before the exam.
