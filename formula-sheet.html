1 Real Numbers

Open the chapter →

  • Fundamental Theorem of ArithmeticEvery composite number can be written as a product of primes, and this factorisation is unique apart from the order in which the primes are written.
  • HCF from the factorisationProduct of the smallest power of each common prime factor.
  • LCM from the factorisationProduct of the greatest power of each prime factor involved.
  • Product rule — two numbers only\(\text{HCF}(a,b)\times\text{LCM}(a,b)=a\times b\)
  • Coprime numbers\(\text{HCF}=1\), and then \(\text{LCM}=a\times b\).
  • Theorem 1.2If a prime \(p\) divides \(a^2\), then \(p\) divides \(a\) (here \(a\) is a positive integer).
  • Theorem 1.3\(\sqrt2\) is irrational. The same proof by contradiction works for \(\sqrt3\), \(\sqrt5\), \(\sqrt p\) for any prime \(p\): assume \(\sqrt p=\dfrac ab\) with \(a,b\) coprime and \(b\ne0\).
  • Rational with irrationalrational \(\pm\) irrational is irrational; (non-zero rational) \(\times\) or \(\div\) irrational is irrational. But irrational \(+\) irrational may be rational: \((2+\sqrt3)+(2-\sqrt3)=4\).
  • Which one — HCF or LCM?“Largest number that divides …”, “maximum capacity”, “greatest length” → HCF. “Smallest number divisible by …”, “ring together again”, “meet again” → LCM.
  • Leaving remainders \(r_1,r_2\)Largest number dividing \(a\) and \(b\) leaving remainders \(r_1\) and \(r_2\) is \(\text{HCF}(a-r_1,\ b-r_2)\).
  • Ending in the digit 0A number ends in 0 only if both 2 and 5 are among its prime factors, i.e. 10 divides it.

\(\text{HCF}\times\text{LCM}=\) product is true for two numbers only — never use it for three.

2 Polynomials

Open the chapter →

  • Zero of a polynomial\(k\) is a zero of \(p(x)\) if \(p(k)=0\). On the graph of \(y=p(x)\) it is the \(x\)-coordinate of a point where the graph meets the \(x\)-axis.
  • How many zeroesLinear: exactly 1. Quadratic: at most 2. Cubic: at most 3. Degree \(n\): at most \(n\).
  • Linear polynomial\(ax+b\ (a\ne0)\) has the single zero \(-\dfrac ba\); its graph is a straight line.
  • Sum of the zeroesFor \(ax^2+bx+c\): \(\ \alpha+\beta=-\dfrac ba=\dfrac{-(\text{coefficient of }x)}{\text{coefficient of }x^2}\)
  • Product of the zeroes\(\alpha\beta=\dfrac ca=\dfrac{\text{constant term}}{\text{coefficient of }x^2}\)
  • Polynomial from its zeroes\(k\bigl[x^2-(\alpha+\beta)x+\alpha\beta\bigr]\), \(k\) any non-zero real number — the sign in front of the sum is minus.
  • Shape of the graph\(y=ax^2+bx+c\) is a parabola: it opens upwards if \(a\gt0\), downwards if \(a\lt0\), and cuts the \(y\)-axis at \((0,\,c)\).
  • Cubic — for reference; the board asks the quadratic caseFor \(ax^3+bx^2+cx+d\): \(\ \alpha+\beta+\gamma=-\dfrac ba\), \(\ \alpha\beta+\beta\gamma+\gamma\alpha=\dfrac ca\), \(\ \alpha\beta\gamma=-\dfrac da\)

Write every expression in \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) with \(S=\alpha+\beta\) and \(P=\alpha\beta\) — never find the zeroes separately.

New polynomial \(=x^2-(\text{new sum})x+(\text{new product})\). For the reciprocal zeroes there is a shortcut: reverse the coefficients, so \(ax^2+bx+c\) becomes \(cx^2+bx+a\).

The sum is \(-\dfrac ba\), with a minus. Only the product \(\dfrac ca\) has no sign change — and coefficients carry their own signs, so for \(3x^2-7x-6\) you must use \(b=-7,\ c=-6\).

3 Pair of Linear Equations in Two Variables

Open the chapter →

  • Standard form\(a_1x+b_1y+c_1=0\) and \(a_2x+b_2y+c_2=0\). Bring every term to the left before you compare the ratios.
  • SubstitutionMake one variable the subject in one equation (easiest when its coefficient is \(\pm1\)), put that into the other equation, solve, then back-substitute.
  • EliminationMultiply the equations so that one variable has the same coefficient, add or subtract to remove it, solve, then back-substitute.
  • Graphical methodPlot two points of each line on graph paper. The point of intersection is the solution; read \(x\) first, then \(y\).
  • How many solutions — never solve, just compareCompare \(\dfrac{a_1}{a_2}\), \(\dfrac{b_1}{b_2}\), \(\dfrac{c_1}{c_2}\); see the table below.
  • Two-digit numbernumber \(=10x+y\), reversed \(=10y+x\); sum \(=11(x+y)\), difference \(=9(x-y)\).
  • Anglessupplementary \(x+y=180\); complementary \(x+y=90\); a cyclic quadrilateral has opposite angles adding to \(180^\circ\).
  • Fixed \(+\) running chargetotal \(=\) fixed charge \(+\) (number of chargeable units \(\times\) rate).
  • Two vehicles, gap \(d\)same direction \(t(u-v)=d\); towards each other \(t(u+v)=d\).
  • Boat and streamdownstream \(=u+v\), upstream \(=u-v\), with \(u\) the speed in still water.
  • Ratios givenincomes \(7:5\) → take \(7x\) and \(5x\); a second ratio needs a different letter, \(9y\) and \(5y\).
  • Triangle cut off by an axisWith the \(x\)-axis: base \(=\) gap between the two \(x\)-intercepts, height \(=y\) of the intersection point. With the \(y\)-axis it is the other way round. Area \(=\frac12\times\text{base}\times\text{height}\).

4 Quadratic Equations

Open the chapter →

  • Standard form\(ax^2+bx+c=0\), with \(a,b,c\) real and \(a\ne0\).
  • Root\(\alpha\) is a root if \(a\alpha^2+b\alpha+c=0\). A quadratic equation has at most two roots.
  • Splitting the middle termFind two numbers whose product is \(a\times c\) and whose sum is \(b\); split \(bx\) into those two parts and take common factors in pairs.
  • Zero product ruleIf \((px+q)(rx+s)=0\) then \(px+q=0\) or \(rx+s=0\).
  • Quadratic formula\(x=\dfrac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}\), valid when \(b^2-4ac\ge0\).
  • Discriminant\(D=b^2-4ac\) — see the table below.
  • Equal rootsWhen \(D=0\), both roots are \(-\dfrac{b}{2a}\).
  • Speed and worktime \(=\dfrac{\text{distance}}{\text{speed}}\), and one hour's work \(=\dfrac1{\text{time taken}}\).
  • Rectanglearea \(=l\times b\), perimeter \(=2(l+b)\).
  • Upstream / downstreamupstream speed \(=u-v\), downstream speed \(=u+v\), with \(u\) the speed in still water and \(v\) that of the stream.
  • Rejecting a rootA length, speed, age, or number of items cannot be negative — reject such a root in words; that sentence carries a mark.
\(D=b^2-4ac\)Nature of the rootsThe parabola \(y=ax^2+bx+c\)
\(D\gt0\)two distinct real rootscuts the \(x\)-axis at two points
\(D=0\)two equal real roots, both \(-\dfrac{b}{2a}\)touches the \(x\)-axis at one point

Write \(a\), \(b\), \(c\) with their signs before substituting. For \(3x^2-2x-8\), \(D=(-2)^2-4(3)(-8)=4+96=100\), not \(4-96\).

5 Arithmetic Progressions

Open the chapter →

  • An AP\(a,\ a+d,\ a+2d,\ a+3d,\ \ldots\) — a list in which each term differs from the one before it by the same fixed number \(d\).
  • Common difference\(d=a_{k+1}-a_k\) — always “later term minus earlier term”, so it can be negative or zero.
  • \(n\)th term (general term)\(a_n=a+(n-1)d\)
  • Number of terms\(n=\dfrac{l-a}{d}+1\), where \(l\) is the last term.
  • \(n\)th term from the end\(l-(n-1)d\)
  • Sum of the first \(n\) terms\(S_n=\dfrac n2\bigl[2a+(n-1)d\bigr]\)
  • Sum when the last term is known\(S_n=\dfrac n2(a+l)\)
  • Term from the sums\(a_n=S_n-S_{n-1}\), and \(a_1=S_1\).
  • Arithmetic meanIf \(a,b,c\) are in AP then \(b=\dfrac{a+c}{2}\), and \(2b=a+c\).
  • First \(n\) natural numbers\(1+2+3+\cdots+n=\dfrac{n(n+1)}{2}\)
  • First \(n\) odd numbers\(1+3+5+\cdots+(2n-1)=n^2\)
  • Reading \(a_n\) or \(S_n\) off a formulaIf \(a_n=pn+q\) then \(d=p\) and \(a=p+q\). If \(S_n\) is given, first work out \(a_n=S_n-S_{n-1}\).

\(a_n=a+(n-1)d\), not \(a+nd\): from the first term you take \(n-1\) steps, not \(n\). And after \((n-1)d=\ldots\) there is one more line — add 1 — because gaps and terms are different things.

6 Triangles

Open the chapter →

  • Similar polygonsTwo polygons of the same number of sides are similar if their corresponding angles are equal and their corresponding sides are in the same ratio. All circles are similar; all squares are similar; all equilateral triangles are similar.
  • Theorem 6.1 · Basic Proportionality Theorem (Thales)If a line drawn parallel to one side of a triangle intersects the other two sides in distinct points, the other two sides are divided in the same ratio. \(DE\parallel BC\Rightarrow\dfrac{AD}{DB}=\dfrac{AE}{EC}\)
  • Theorem 6.2 · Converse of BPTIf a line divides any two sides of a triangle in the same ratio, then the line is parallel to the third side.
  • The parallel segment\(DE\parallel BC\Rightarrow\triangle ADE\sim\triangle ABC\), so \(\dfrac{DE}{BC}=\dfrac{AD}{AB}=\dfrac{AE}{AC}\) — the whole side goes in the denominator.
  • Theorem 6.3 · AA (AAA) criterionIf in two triangles the corresponding angles are equal, the triangles are similar. Two equal pairs of angles are enough, because the third pair then follows.
  • Theorem 6.4 · SSS criterion\(\dfrac{AB}{PQ}=\dfrac{BC}{QR}=\dfrac{CA}{RP}\Rightarrow\triangle ABC\sim\triangle PQR\)
  • Theorem 6.5 · SAS criterion\(\angle A=\angle P\) and \(\dfrac{AB}{PQ}=\dfrac{AC}{PR}\Rightarrow\triangle ABC\sim\triangle PQR\). The equal angle must be the included angle.
  • Similar triangles with ratio \(k\)Corresponding sides, medians, altitudes, angle bisectors and perimeters are all in the same ratio \(k\).
  • Trapezium \(ABCD\), \(AB\parallel DC\), diagonals meeting at \(O\)\(\triangle OAB\sim\triangle OCD\), so \(\dfrac{OA}{OC}=\dfrac{OB}{OD}=\dfrac{AB}{DC}\)
  • Shadows at the same time of day\(\dfrac{\text{height}_1}{\text{shadow}_1}=\dfrac{\text{height}_2}{\text{shadow}_2}\), after converting to one unit.
  • From earlier classes, used constantlyCongruence (SAS, ASA, SSS, RHS) and CPCT; alternate and corresponding angles with a transversal; angle sum of a triangle \(=180^\circ\); the Pythagoras theorem — in a right triangle, \((\text{hypotenuse})^2\) equals the sum of the squares of the other two sides.

Write similarity with the letters in the order the equal angles match. \(\triangle ABC\sim\triangle PQR\) means \(A\leftrightarrow P\), \(B\leftrightarrow Q\), \(C\leftrightarrow R\) — the wrong order gives the wrong ratios and loses the marks.

7 Coordinate Geometry

Open the chapter →

  • Distance formula\(PQ=\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2}\)
  • Distance from the origin\(OP=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\)
  • Distance from the axesDistance of \((x,y)\) from the \(x\)-axis is \(|y|\); from the \(y\)-axis it is \(|x|\).
  • Section formula, ratio \(m_1:m_2\)\(\left(\dfrac{m_1x_2+m_2x_1}{m_1+m_2},\ \dfrac{m_1y_2+m_2y_1}{m_1+m_2}\right)\) — the multiplication is crossed.
  • Section formula, ratio \(k:1\)\(\left(\dfrac{kx_2+x_1}{k+1},\ \dfrac{ky_2+y_1}{k+1}\right)\) — use it whenever the ratio is what you have to find.
  • Mid-point\(\left(\dfrac{x_1+x_2}{2},\ \dfrac{y_1+y_2}{2}\right)\)
  • Points of trisectionThe two points divide the segment in the ratios \(1:2\) and \(2:1\).
  • Fourth vertex of a parallelogram \(ABCD\)The diagonals bisect each other: mid-point of \(AC=\) mid-point of \(BD\).
  • Ends of a diameterThe centre is their mid-point, so \(x_2=2h-x_1\) and \(y_2=2k-y_1\).
  • Equidistant points“\(P\) is equidistant from \(A\) and \(B\)” → write \(PA^2=PB^2\) and drop the square roots.
  • Collinear points\(A\), \(B\), \(C\) are collinear if the largest of the three distances equals the sum of the other two.
  • Naming a quadrilateralAll four sides equal and diagonals equal → square; all four sides equal, diagonals unequal → rhombus; opposite sides equal and diagonals equal → rectangle; only opposite sides equal → parallelogram.
  • Area of a rhombus\(\dfrac12\times d_1\times d_2\), the product of its diagonals.

In the section formula \(m_1\) goes with \(x_2\) and \(m_2\) with \(x_1\) — crossed. And keep the order of the ratio: the first number belongs to the first named point, so \(1:2\) is not \(2:1\).

8 Introduction to Trigonometry

Open the chapter →

  • The three basic ratios\(\sin A=\dfrac{\text{side opposite to }A}{\text{hypotenuse}}\), \(\ \cos A=\dfrac{\text{side adjacent to }A}{\text{hypotenuse}}\), \(\ \tan A=\dfrac{\text{side opposite to }A}{\text{side adjacent to }A}\)
  • The three reciprocals\(\operatorname{cosec}A=\dfrac1{\sin A}\), \(\ \sec A=\dfrac1{\cos A}\), \(\ \cot A=\dfrac1{\tan A}\)
  • Quotient forms\(\tan A=\dfrac{\sin A}{\cos A}\), \(\ \cot A=\dfrac{\cos A}{\sin A}\), \(\ \tan A\cot A=1\)
  • Identity 1\(\sin^2A+\cos^2A=1\), so \(\sin^2A=1-\cos^2A\) and \(\cos^2A=1-\sin^2A\).
  • Identity 2\(1+\tan^2A=\sec^2A\), so \(\sec^2A-\tan^2A=1\)  \((0^\circ\le A\lt90^\circ)\)
  • Identity 3\(1+\cot^2A=\operatorname{cosec}^2A\), so \(\operatorname{cosec}^2A-\cot^2A=1\)  \((0^\circ\lt A\le90^\circ)\)
  • Two products worth memorising\((\sec A+\tan A)(\sec A-\tan A)=1\) and \((\operatorname{cosec}A+\cot A)(\operatorname{cosec}A-\cot A)=1\)
  • Ratios depend on the angle, not the sizeGiven one ratio, take the two sides as \(k\times\) those numbers, find the third side by the Pythagoras theorem, and read off the rest. The \(k\) always cancels.
  • Size limits\(0\le\sin A\le1\) and \(0\le\cos A\le1\); \(\sec A\ge1\) and \(\operatorname{cosec}A\ge1\); \(\tan A\) can be any non-negative number.
  • Not defined\(\tan90^\circ\), \(\sec90^\circ\), \(\cot0^\circ\), \(\operatorname{cosec}0^\circ\).

Rebuilding the table in ten seconds. Write \(0,1,2,3,4\) under the five angles, divide each by 4, take the square root — that is the \(\sin\) row. Reverse it for \(\cos\). Divide \(\sin\) by \(\cos\) for \(\tan\). The last three rows are just the reciprocals.

The sign is minus in two of the three identities: \(\sec^2A-\tan^2A=1\) and \(\operatorname{cosec}^2A-\cot^2A=1\). Only \(\sin^2A+\cos^2A\) has a plus. And \(\sin A\) is one symbol — you cannot cancel the \(A\), and \(\sin2A\ne2\sin A\).

9 Some Applications of Trigonometry

Open the chapter →

  • Line of sightThe line drawn from the eye of the observer to the object being viewed.
  • Angle of elevationThe angle between the line of sight and the horizontal, when the object is above the horizontal level of the eye.
  • Angle of depressionThe angle between the line of sight and the horizontal, when the object is below the horizontal level of the eye.
  • Depression \(=\) elevationThe angle of depression of a point from the top equals the angle of elevation of the top from that point (alternate angles).
  • Height from distance\(h=d\tan\theta\)
  • Distance from height\(d=\dfrac h{\tan\theta}\)
  • Along the line of sight\(\ell=\dfrac h{\sin\theta}=\dfrac d{\cos\theta}\)
  • Shadow of a vertical pole\(s=\dfrac h{\tan\theta}\), where \(\theta\) is the Sun's altitude.
  • Observer's heightIf the eye is \(e\) metres above the ground, work with \(h-e\) and add \(e\) back at the end.
  • Broken treeThe broken part is the hypotenuse; height of the tree \(=\) standing part \(+\) broken part.
  • Two points, one towerSame side of the tower ⇒ the two ground distances are subtracted; opposite sides ⇒ they are added.
  • Rationalising\(\dfrac a{\sqrt3-1}=\dfrac{a(\sqrt3+1)}2\), \(\ \dfrac a{\sqrt3+1}=\dfrac{a(\sqrt3-1)}2\), \(\ \dfrac a{\sqrt3}=\dfrac{a\sqrt3}3\)
  • Speed\(\text{speed}=\dfrac{\text{distance covered}}{\text{time taken}}\); \(1\ \text{m/s}=\dfrac{18}5\) km/h; \(1\ \text{m/min}=\dfrac3{50}\) km/h.

10 Circles

Open the chapter →

  • TangentA line that meets a circle at exactly one point, the point of contact. A secant meets it at two points.
  • How many tangentsFrom a point inside the circle: none. From a point on the circle: exactly one. From a point outside: exactly two.
  • Theorem 10.1The tangent at any point of a circle is perpendicular to the radius through the point of contact.
  • Theorem 10.2The lengths of the two tangents drawn from an external point to a circle are equal.
  • Length of a tangent\(PA=\sqrt{OP^2-r^2}\), where \(OP\) is the distance of \(P\) from the centre — never \(OP-r\).
  • Angle pairFor the two tangents from \(P\): \(\angle APB+\angle AOB=180^\circ\).
  • Half-angle results\(\angle APB=2\,\angle OAB\) and \(\angle APB=2\,\angle OPA\); \(OP\) bisects \(\angle APB\) and \(\angle AOB\), and \(OP\) is the perpendicular bisector of the chord of contact \(AB\).
  • Quadrilateral circumscribing a circle\(AB+CD=BC+AD\) — the opposite sides pair up. Opposite sides also subtend supplementary angles at the centre.
  • Triangle circumscribing a circleIf \(x,y,z\) are the tangent lengths from \(A,B,C\) then the perimeter is \(2(x+y+z)\), and \(x=s-a\), \(y=s-b\), \(z=s-c\) with \(s\) the semi-perimeter.
  • Incircle of a right triangle\(r=\dfrac{a+b-c}2\), where \(c\) is the hypotenuse.
  • Concentric circlesA chord of the bigger circle that touches the smaller one has length \(2\sqrt{R^2-r^2}\) and is bisected at the point of contact.
  • From Class IXThe perpendicular from the centre to a chord bisects it; equal chords are equidistant from the centre; the longest chord is the diameter.

11 Areas Related to Circles

Open the chapter →

  • Circle, from Class IXcircumference \(=2\pi r=\pi d\), area \(=\pi r^2\). A full turn at the centre is \(360^\circ\).

  • Area of a sector\(A=\dfrac\theta{360}\times\pi r^2\)

13 Statistics

Open the chapter →

  • Class mark\(x_i=\dfrac{\text{upper limit}+\text{lower limit}}2\)
  • Mean — direct method\(\bar x=\dfrac{\Sigma f_ix_i}{\Sigma f_i}\)
  • Mean — assumed-mean method\(\bar x=a+\dfrac{\Sigma f_id_i}{\Sigma f_i}\), where \(d_i=x_i-a\)
  • Mean — step-deviation method\(\bar x=a+\left(\dfrac{\Sigma f_iu_i}{\Sigma f_i}\right)\times h\), where \(u_i=\dfrac{x_i-a}h\)
  • Mode of grouped data\(l+\left(\dfrac{f_1-f_0}{2f_1-f_0-f_2}\right)\times h\)
  • Median of grouped data\(l+\left(\dfrac{\frac n2-\text{cf}}{f}\right)\times h\)
  • Empirical relation\(3\,\text{Median}=\text{Mode}+2\,\text{Mean}\)
  • Modal class · median classModal class \(=\) the class with the greatest frequency. Median class \(=\) the first class whose cumulative frequency is greater than \(\dfrac n2\).
  • Ungrouped data, from Class IXMean \(=\dfrac{\text{sum of the observations}}n\). For the median, sort first: if \(n\) is odd it is the \(\left(\frac{n+1}2\right)\)th observation, if \(n\) is even it is the mean of the \(\left(\frac n2\right)\)th and \(\left(\frac n2+1\right)\)th.
  • Deviations from the mean\(\Sigma f_i(x_i-\bar x)=0\)
  • Changing every observationIf each \(x\) becomes \(px+q\), the mean becomes \(p\bar x+q\).
  • Mean of \(1,2,3,\ldots,n\)\(\dfrac{n+1}2\)
  • Discontinuous classesMake classes such as 10–19, 20–29 continuous first — 9.5–19.5, 19.5–29.5, … — before using the mode or median formula.
LetterWhat it means
\(l\)lower limit of the modal class (mode formula) or of the median class (median formula)
\(h\)class size — all classes must be equal
\(f_1\)frequency of the modal class
\(f_0,\ f_2\)frequency of the class before the modal class, and of the class after it
\(n\)total number of observations, \(\Sigma f_i\)
cfcumulative frequency of the class just before the median class
\(f\)frequency of the median class
\(a\)assumed mean — any convenient class mark, usually a middle one

cf belongs to the class just before the median class, never to the median class itself — and do not forget the “\(\times h\)” at the end of the step-deviation, mode and median formulas.

14 Probability

Open the chapter →

  • Theoretical (classical) probability\(P(E)=\dfrac{\text{number of outcomes favourable to }E}{\text{number of all possible outcomes}}\), the outcomes being equally likely.
  • Range\(0\le P(E)\le1\). A negative value, a value above 1, or a percentage above 100 is impossible.
  • Complement\(P(E)+P(\overline E)=1\), so \(P(\overline E)=1-P(E)\). Use it for “not”, “at least” and “neither … nor”.
  • Impossible and sure events\(P(\text{impossible event})=0\); \(P(\text{sure event})=1\).
  • Elementary eventsThe probabilities of all the elementary events of an experiment add up to 1.
  • \(n\) coins\(2^n\) equally likely outcomes — 2, 4 and 8 for one, two and three coins.
  • Two dice36 equally likely ordered pairs; the sums \(2,3,\ldots,12\) occur \(1,2,3,4,5,6,5,4,3,2,1\) times.
  • Deck of 52 cards4 suits × 13 cards · 26 red and 26 black · 12 face cards (K, Q, J only) · 4 aces · 4 of each rank.
  • Leap year\(366=52\times7+2\): 52 of each weekday plus 2 extra consecutive days, in 7 equally likely pairs — so \(P(53\ \text{Sundays})=\frac27\) in a leap year and \(\frac17\) in an ordinary year.
  • Geometrical probability\(P=\dfrac{\text{favourable area}}{\text{total area}}\) (or length), when every point is equally likely.
  • Cards removed firstChange the denominator: remove the 6 black face cards and the total becomes 46, not 52.
  • Always reduceGive the answer in lowest terms: \(\frac{12}{52}=\frac3{13}\), \(\ \frac6{36}=\frac16\).

Two coins give 4 outcomes, not 3 — HT and TH are different. For the same reason, two dice are ordered pairs: \((1,3)\) and \((3,1)\) both count.

★ Constants and conventions

  • πTake \(\pi=\dfrac{22}7\) unless the question itself says \(\pi=3.14\). Work in fractions and cancel; substitute a decimal only at the very last step.
  • Surds\(\sqrt2\approx1.414\), \(\ \sqrt3\approx1.732\) (a paper may print 1.41 and 1.73). Rationalise first, substitute the decimal last.
  • CalculatorsNot allowed. Board numbers are chosen so that the arithmetic works on paper — if it turns ugly, check the previous line.
  • UnitsEvery answer carries its unit: length cm or m, area \(\text{cm}^2\) or \(\text{m}^2\), volume \(\text{cm}^3\), \(\text{m}^3\) or litres, money ₹, angles in degrees. Convert to one unit before you start.
  • Length, area, volume\(1\ \text{m}=100\ \text{cm}\) · \(1\ \text{m}^2=10\,000\ \text{cm}^2\) · \(1\ \text{m}^3=10^6\ \text{cm}^3=1000\) litres · \(1\ \text{litre}=1000\ \text{cm}^3\).
  • Speed\(1\ \text{m/s}=\dfrac{18}5\) km/h · \(1\ \text{km/h}=\dfrac5{18}\) m/s.
  • RoundingRound only the final answer, to the number of decimals the question asks for. In money questions round to two decimals; never round in the middle of a calculation.
  • Pythagoras theoremFrom Class IX, used in almost every chapter: in a right triangle, \((\text{hypotenuse})^2=\) sum of the squares of the other two sides. Triples worth knowing: 3-4-5, 5-12-13, 8-15-17, 7-24-25, 20-21-29 — and every multiple of them.
  • The letters\(r\) radius · \(d\) diameter · \(h\) vertical height · \(l\) slant height (and last term of an AP) · \(\theta\) angle · \(\ell\) arc length · \(s\) semi-perimeter.
  • Showing the workState the formula, substitute, simplify, answer with unit. Examiners give step marks for the formula and the substitution even when the arithmetic slips.
