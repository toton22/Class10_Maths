Chapter 8 Introduction to Trigonometry

How tall is the Qutub Minar? How wide is that river? You cannot stretch a tape across either — but one acute angle and one measured length are enough. Trigonometry turns an angle into a ratio of sides, and that single idea runs through the rest of your Maths course.

    8.1 Introduction

    You already know a great deal about right triangles. This chapter adds one new idea to them: in a right triangle, the size of an acute angle decides the ratios of the sides — and nothing else does. That single sentence is the whole of trigonometry in Class X.

    Look at three situations where a right triangle appears by itself.

    • How tall is the Minar?

      A student stands on the ground and looks up at the top of the Qutub Minar. The ground, the tower and the line of sight close up into a right triangle. Can the height be found without climbing it?

    • How wide is the river?

      A girl on her balcony looks down at a flower pot on the far bank. If she knows how high she is sitting, can she find the width of the river?

    • How high is the balloon?

      A balloon is first spotted at a point A and, by the time the mother comes out, it has drifted to B. Can the height of B above the ground be found?

    In every case a length that cannot be measured is joined to a length that can, through one acute angle. Trigonometry is the tool that connects them.

    The word itself says what the subject does. It comes from the Greek tri (three), gon (sides) and metron (measure): the measurement of three-sided figures. Work on it was already being done in Egypt and Babylon, and early astronomers used it to estimate the distances of the stars and planets.

    Right triangle. A triangle with one angle of \(90^\circ\). The side opposite the right angle is the hypotenuse, and it is the longest side.

    Pythagoras theorem. In a right triangle, (hypotenuse)² = sum of the squares of the other two sides. You will use it in almost every question of this chapter.

    Angle sum. The two acute angles of a right triangle add up to \(90^\circ\).

    Only the ratios of an acute angle of a right triangle, plus the two boundary cases \(0^\circ\) and \(90^\circ\). You will (1) define the six ratios, (2) find them for \(0^\circ,\,30^\circ,\,45^\circ,\,60^\circ,\,90^\circ\), and (3) prove and use three identities that connect them.

    8.2 Trigonometric ratios

    Fix one acute angle of a right triangle. Every side of that triangle then gets a name relative to the angle you fixed. Once the sides are named, the six ratios almost write themselves.

    Naming the three sides

    Take \(\triangle ABC\), right-angled at \(B\), and fix attention on \(\angle A\).

    • Hypotenuse — the side facing the right angle, here \(AC\). It never changes, whichever acute angle you look at.
    • Side opposite to \(\angle A\) — the side that faces \(\angle A\), here \(BC\). It is the side that does not touch \(A\).
    • Side adjacent to \(\angle A\) — the remaining side, here \(AB\). It is an arm of \(\angle A\) itself.

    Now switch to \(\angle C\) in the same triangle. The hypotenuse is still \(AC\), but the other two names swap over: \(AB\) is now opposite and \(BC\) is now adjacent.

    “Opposite” and “adjacent” belong to the angle, not to the triangle. Change the angle and they exchange places. Before writing any ratio, put your finger on the marked angle and ask: which side faces it, and which side is an arm of it?

    Definition

    Let \(A\) be an acute angle of a right triangle. The six trigonometric ratios of \(A\) are

    $$\begin{aligned} \sin A&=\frac{\text{side opposite to }A}{\text{hypotenuse}}\\[2pt] \cos A&=\frac{\text{side adjacent to }A}{\text{hypotenuse}}\\[2pt] \tan A&=\frac{\text{side opposite to }A}{\text{side adjacent to }A} \end{aligned}$$

    and the three reciprocals

    $$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{cosec} A&=\frac{1}{\sin A}\\[2pt] \sec A&=\frac{1}{\cos A}\\[2pt] \cot A&=\frac{1}{\tan A} \end{aligned}$$

    Written with the letters of \(\triangle ABC\) (right-angled at \(B\)), the six ratios of \(\angle A\) are these.

    RatioIn wordsIn \(\triangle ABC\)Reciprocal of
    \(\sin A\)opposite ÷ hypotenuse\(\dfrac{BC}{AC}\)\(\operatorname{cosec} A\)
    \(\cos A\)adjacent ÷ hypotenuse\(\dfrac{AB}{AC}\)\(\sec A\)
    \(\tan A\)opposite ÷ adjacent\(\dfrac{BC}{AB}\)\(\cot A\)
    \(\operatorname{cosec} A\)hypotenuse ÷ opposite\(\dfrac{AC}{BC}\)\(\sin A\)
    \(\sec A\)hypotenuse ÷ adjacent\(\dfrac{AC}{AB}\)\(\cos A\)
    \(\cot A\)adjacent ÷ opposite\(\dfrac{AB}{BC}\)\(\tan A\)
    Two relations you will use constantly
    $$\tan A=\frac{\sin A}{\cos A},\qquad \cot A=\frac{\cos A}{\sin A}$$

    Check the first one: \(\dfrac{\sin A}{\cos A}=\dfrac{BC/AC}{AB/AC}=\dfrac{BC}{AB}=\tan A\). The \(AC\) cancels. The second follows by taking reciprocals.

    Write the three main ratios as \(\dfrac{P}{H},\ \dfrac{B}{H},\ \dfrac{P}{B}\), where \(P\) = perpendicular (opposite), \(B\) = base (adjacent) and \(H\) = hypotenuse. Reading the letters in order gives Pandit Badri Prasad, Har Har Bole — that is \(\sin=\frac PH,\ \cos=\frac BH,\ \tan=\frac PB\). The other three are just turned upside down.

    A ratio belongs to the angle, not to the triangle

    Draw \(\triangle ABC\) right-angled at \(B\). Now take any point \(P\) on \(AC\) and drop \(PM\perp AB\); also take a point \(Q\) on \(AC\) produced and drop \(QN\perp AB\). You get three different right triangles — \(\triangle AMP\), \(\triangle ABC\), \(\triangle ANQ\) — but all three contain the same angle \(A\) and a right angle, so by the AA criterion they are similar.

    Because the triangles are similar, \(\dfrac{AM}{AB}=\dfrac{AP}{AC}=\dfrac{MP}{BC}\), and rearranging gives \(\dfrac{MP}{AP}=\dfrac{BC}{AC}\). The same happens for every other ratio. So:

    The value of a trigonometric ratio depends only on the size of the angle, not on the size of the triangle you drew it in.

    \(\sin A\) is one symbol — the sine of the angle \(A\). It is not \(\sin\) multiplied by \(A\); “\(\sin\)” standing alone has no meaning. The same goes for \(\cos A,\ \tan A,\ \operatorname{cosec} A,\ \sec A,\ \cot A\).

    For convenience we write \(\sin^2A\) instead of \((\sin A)^2\), and \(\cos^2A\) instead of \((\cos A)^2\). But \(\sin^{-1}A\) does not mean \(\operatorname{cosec} A\) — it is a different idea studied in higher classes. Always write \(\operatorname{cosec} A=(\sin A)^{-1}\) in words, never as \(\sin^{-1}A\).

    The hypotenuse is the longest side, so the opposite and adjacent sides are each smaller than it. Therefore

    $$0\le\sin A\le1,\qquad 0\le\cos A\le1,$$

    while \(\sec A\) and \(\operatorname{cosec} A\), being their reciprocals, are always at least 1 wherever they are defined. \(\tan A\) and \(\cot A\), on the other hand, can take any value from 0 upwards.

    One ratio given — find the rest

    A ratio tells you the shape of the triangle, not its size. So turn the ratio into two sides with a common factor \(k\), get the third side by Pythagoras, and read off everything else.

    1. Draw a right triangle and mark the given angle.
    2. Write the given ratio as a fraction and let the two sides in it be (numerator)\(\,k\) and (denominator)\(\,k\), where \(k\gt0\).
    3. Find the third side using the Pythagoras theorem. The \(k\) stays throughout.
    4. Write the required ratios. Every \(k\) cancels, so the answers are pure numbers.

    Why introduce \(k\) at all? Because \(\sin A=\frac35\) does not mean the opposite side is 3 cm; it means opposite : hypotenuse \(=3:5\). Writing \(3k\) and \(5k\) keeps that honest — and \(k\) always cancels in the end, which is exactly the point made above.

    NCERT Example 1one ratio to six

    Given \(\tan A=\dfrac43\), find the other trigonometric ratios of the angle \(A\).

    Solution

    Draw \(\triangle ABC\) right-angled at \(B\), with \(\angle A\) marked.

    Here \(\tan A=\dfrac{BC}{AB}=\dfrac43\), so let \(BC=4k\) and \(AB=3k\), where \(k\) is a positive number.

    By the Pythagoras theorem,

    $$\begin{aligned} AC^2&=AB^2+BC^2\\ &=(3k)^2+(4k)^2\\ &=9k^2+16k^2=25k^2 \end{aligned}$$

    So \(AC=5k\).

    Now read the ratios off the triangle:

    $$\begin{aligned} \sin A&=\frac{BC}{AC}=\frac{4k}{5k}=\frac45\\[2pt] \cos A&=\frac{AB}{AC}=\frac{3k}{5k}=\frac35 \end{aligned}$$

    and the reciprocals

    $$\begin{aligned} \cot A&=\frac{1}{\tan A}=\frac34\\[2pt] \operatorname{cosec} A&=\frac{1}{\sin A}=\frac54\\[2pt] \sec A&=\frac{1}{\cos A}=\frac53 \end{aligned}$$

    \(\sin A=\frac45\), \(\cos A=\frac35\), \(\cot A=\frac34\), \(\operatorname{cosec} A=\frac54\), \(\sec A=\frac53\)

    NCERT Example 2equal sine, equal angle

    \(\angle B\) and \(\angle Q\) are acute angles with \(\sin B=\sin Q\). Prove that \(\angle B=\angle Q\).

    Solution

    Put each angle inside a right triangle: take \(\triangle ABC\) right-angled at \(C\) and \(\triangle PQR\) right-angled at \(R\).

    $$\sin B=\frac{AC}{AB},\qquad \sin Q=\frac{PR}{PQ}$$

    Since \(\sin B=\sin Q\),

    $$\frac{AC}{AB}=\frac{PR}{PQ}$$

    and therefore

    $$\frac{AC}{PR}=\frac{AB}{PQ}=k\ \ \text{(say)}\qquad\ldots(1)$$

    So \(AC=k\,PR\) and \(AB=k\,PQ\). By the Pythagoras theorem,

    $$\begin{aligned} BC&=\sqrt{AB^2-AC^2}\\[2pt] QR&=\sqrt{PQ^2-PR^2} \end{aligned}$$

    Therefore

    $$\begin{aligned} \frac{BC}{QR}&=\frac{\sqrt{k^2PQ^2-k^2PR^2}}{\sqrt{PQ^2-PR^2}}\\ &=\frac{k\sqrt{PQ^2-PR^2}}{\sqrt{PQ^2-PR^2}}=k\qquad\ldots(2) \end{aligned}$$

    From (1) and (2), \(\dfrac{AC}{PR}=\dfrac{AB}{PQ}=\dfrac{BC}{QR}\).

    The three pairs of corresponding sides are in the same ratio, so \(\triangle ACB\sim\triangle PRQ\). [SSS similarity]

    Corresponding angles of similar triangles are equal, and \(\angle B\) corresponds to \(\angle Q\).

    Hence \(\angle B=\angle Q\).

    This is the converse of the fact proved just above: equal angles force equal ratios, and equal ratios force equal angles. It is the reason a ratio can be used to identify an angle.

    NCERT Example 3two sides given

    In \(\triangle ACB\), right-angled at \(C\), \(AB=29\) units, \(BC=21\) units and \(\angle ABC=\theta\). Find the value of

    1. \(\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta\)
    2. \(\cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta\)
    Solution

    First find the third side. In \(\triangle ACB\), by the Pythagoras theorem,

    $$\begin{aligned} AC&=\sqrt{AB^2-BC^2}=\sqrt{29^2-21^2}\\ &=\sqrt{(29-21)(29+21)}\\ &=\sqrt{8\times50}=\sqrt{400}=20\ \text{units} \end{aligned}$$

    The angle \(\theta\) is at \(B\), so its opposite side is \(AC\) and its adjacent side is \(BC\).

    $$\begin{aligned} \sin\theta&=\frac{AC}{AB}=\frac{20}{29}\\[2pt] \cos\theta&=\frac{BC}{AB}=\frac{21}{29} \end{aligned}$$
    (i)
    $$\begin{aligned} &\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta\\ &=\left(\frac{21}{29}\right)^2+\left(\frac{20}{29}\right)^2\\ &=\frac{441+400}{841}=\frac{841}{841}=1 \end{aligned}$$

    \(\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta=1\)

    (ii)
    $$\begin{aligned} &\cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta\\ &=\left(\frac{21}{29}\right)^2-\left(\frac{20}{29}\right)^2\\ &=\frac{(21+20)(21-20)}{841}=\frac{41}{841} \end{aligned}$$

    \(\cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta=\dfrac{41}{841}\)

    Part (i) came out 1 — and it always does. That is the first identity, proved in Section 8.4.

    NCERT Example 4verify a statement

    In a right triangle \(ABC\), right-angled at \(B\), \(\tan A=1\). Verify that \(2\sin A\cos A=1\).

    Solution

    In \(\triangle ABC\), \(\tan A=\dfrac{BC}{AB}=1\), so \(BC=AB\).

    Let \(AB=BC=k\), where \(k\) is a positive number. By the Pythagoras theorem,

    $$\begin{aligned} AC&=\sqrt{AB^2+BC^2}\\ &=\sqrt{k^2+k^2}=k\sqrt2 \end{aligned}$$

    Therefore

    $$\begin{aligned} \sin A&=\frac{BC}{AC}=\frac{k}{k\sqrt2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\\[2pt] \cos A&=\frac{AB}{AC}=\frac{1}{\sqrt2} \end{aligned}$$

    Substituting in the left side,

    $$\begin{aligned} 2\sin A\cos A&=2\times\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\times\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\\ &=2\times\frac12=1 \end{aligned}$$

    \(2\sin A\cos A=1\), which is what had to be verified.

    NCERT Example 5sides given by a difference

    In \(\triangle OPQ\), right-angled at \(P\), \(OP=7\) cm and \(OQ-PQ=1\) cm. Find \(\sin Q\) and \(\cos Q\).

    Solution

    Given: \(\angle P=90^\circ\), \(OP=7\) cm, \(OQ-PQ=1\) cm, so \(OQ=1+PQ\).

    By the Pythagoras theorem in \(\triangle OPQ\),

    $$\begin{aligned} OQ^2&=OP^2+PQ^2\\ (1+PQ)^2&=7^2+PQ^2\\ 1+PQ^2+2\,PQ&=49+PQ^2\\ 2\,PQ&=48 \end{aligned}$$

    So \(PQ=24\) cm, and \(OQ=1+24=25\) cm.

    For \(\angle Q\): opposite side \(=OP=7\) cm, adjacent side \(=PQ=24\) cm, hypotenuse \(=OQ=25\) cm.

    $$\begin{aligned} \sin Q&=\frac{OP}{OQ}=\frac{7}{25}\\[2pt] \cos Q&=\frac{PQ}{OQ}=\frac{24}{25} \end{aligned}$$

    \(\sin Q=\dfrac{7}{25}\) and \(\cos Q=\dfrac{24}{25}\)

    The squares \(PQ^2\) cancel on the two sides — that is why a “sum or difference of two sides” question always collapses to a simple linear equation.

    “If \(\sin A=\frac{p}{q}\), find the other ratios” and “\(PR+QR=\ldots\), find \(\sin P\)” are the two commonest 2-mark questions from this section. Full marks need: a labelled figure, the sides written as multiples of \(k\), the Pythagoras step written out, and the final ratio in lowest terms.

    In \(\triangle ABC\), right-angled at \(B\), \(AB=8\) cm and \(BC=15\) cm. Write \(\sin C\) and \(\sec C\).

    Show answer

    \(AC=\sqrt{8^2+15^2}=\sqrt{64+225}=\sqrt{289}=17\) cm.

    For \(\angle C\), the opposite side is \(AB=8\) and the adjacent side is \(BC=15\). So \(\sin C=\dfrac{8}{17}\) and \(\cos C=\dfrac{15}{17}\), giving \(\sec C=\dfrac{17}{15}\).

    8.3 Trigonometric ratios of some specific angles

    For most angles the ratios are ugly decimals. For five angles — \(0^\circ,\ 30^\circ,\ 45^\circ,\ 60^\circ,\ 90^\circ\) — they are exact and short. Those five columns are used in almost every trigonometry question you will ever be set, so derive them once and then know them cold.

    The ratios of 45°

    Take \(\triangle ABC\) right-angled at \(B\) with one acute angle equal to \(45^\circ\). The two acute angles add up to \(90^\circ\), so the other one is \(45^\circ\) too. A triangle with two equal angles has the two opposite sides equal, so

    $$\angle A=\angle C=45^\circ\quad\Rightarrow\quad BC=AB.$$

    Let \(AB=BC=a\). By the Pythagoras theorem,

    $$\begin{aligned} AC^2&=AB^2+BC^2=a^2+a^2=2a^2\\ AC&=a\sqrt2 \end{aligned}$$

    Now read the ratios of the \(45^\circ\) angle at \(A\): opposite \(=BC=a\), adjacent \(=AB=a\), hypotenuse \(=AC=a\sqrt2\).

    $$\begin{aligned} \sin45^\circ&=\frac{BC}{AC}=\frac{a}{a\sqrt2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\\[2pt] \cos45^\circ&=\frac{AB}{AC}=\frac{a}{a\sqrt2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\\[2pt] \tan45^\circ&=\frac{BC}{AB}=\frac{a}{a}=1 \end{aligned}$$

    and taking reciprocals,

    $$\operatorname{cosec}45^\circ=\sqrt2,\quad \sec45^\circ=\sqrt2,\quad \cot45^\circ=1.$$

    The ratios of 30° and 60°

    These two come from one equilateral triangle. Let \(\triangle ABC\) be equilateral, so \(\angle A=\angle B=\angle C=60^\circ\). Draw the perpendicular \(AD\) from \(A\) to \(BC\).

    In \(\triangle ABD\) and \(\triangle ACD\): \(AB=AC\) (equilateral), \(AD=AD\) (common) and \(\angle ADB=\angle ADC=90^\circ\). So \(\triangle ABD\cong\triangle ACD\) [RHS], and therefore

    $$\begin{aligned} BD&=DC\\ \angle BAD=\angle CAD&=\tfrac12\times60^\circ=30^\circ \end{aligned}$$

    So \(\triangle ABD\) is right-angled at \(D\) with \(\angle BAD=30^\circ\) and \(\angle ABD=60^\circ\). Let the side of the equilateral triangle be \(AB=2a\). Then \(BD=\tfrac12\,BC=a\), and by the Pythagoras theorem

    $$\begin{aligned} AD^2&=AB^2-BD^2=(2a)^2-a^2=3a^2\\ AD&=a\sqrt3 \end{aligned}$$

    For \(30^\circ\) (the angle at \(A\)): opposite \(=BD=a\), adjacent \(=AD=a\sqrt3\), hypotenuse \(=AB=2a\).

    $$\begin{aligned} \sin30^\circ&=\frac{BD}{AB}=\frac{a}{2a}=\frac12\\[2pt] \cos30^\circ&=\frac{AD}{AB}=\frac{a\sqrt3}{2a}=\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\\[2pt] \tan30^\circ&=\frac{BD}{AD}=\frac{a}{a\sqrt3}=\frac{1}{\sqrt3} \end{aligned}$$ $$\operatorname{cosec}30^\circ=2,\quad \sec30^\circ=\frac{2}{\sqrt3},\quad \cot30^\circ=\sqrt3.$$

    For \(60^\circ\) (the angle at \(B\) in the same triangle \(ABD\)): opposite \(=AD=a\sqrt3\), adjacent \(=BD=a\), hypotenuse \(=AB=2a\).

    $$\begin{aligned} \sin60^\circ&=\frac{AD}{AB}=\frac{a\sqrt3}{2a}=\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\\[2pt] \cos60^\circ&=\frac{BD}{AB}=\frac{a}{2a}=\frac12\\[2pt] \tan60^\circ&=\frac{AD}{BD}=\frac{a\sqrt3}{a}=\sqrt3 \end{aligned}$$ $$\operatorname{cosec}60^\circ=\frac{2}{\sqrt3},\quad \sec60^\circ=2,\quad \cot60^\circ=\frac{1}{\sqrt3}.$$

    What happens at 0° and at 90°

    There is no right triangle with an angle of \(0^\circ\) or \(90^\circ\) as one of its acute angles, so these two values are defined by watching what the ratios approach.

    As \(\angle A\) gets smaller and smaller, \(\sin A=\dfrac{BC}{AC}\) gets closer and closer to 0, while \(\cos A=\dfrac{AB}{AC}\) gets closer and closer to 1. So we define

    $$\sin0^\circ=0,\qquad \cos0^\circ=1.$$

    The other four then follow from the definitions:

    $$\tan0^\circ=\frac{\sin0^\circ}{\cos0^\circ}=\frac01=0,\qquad \sec0^\circ=\frac{1}{\cos0^\circ}=1.$$

    But \(\cot0^\circ=\dfrac{1}{\tan0^\circ}\) and \(\operatorname{cosec}0^\circ=\dfrac{1}{\sin0^\circ}\) are not defined, because you cannot divide by 0.

    In the same way, as \(\angle A\) grows towards \(90^\circ\), the side \(AB\) shrinks towards 0 and \(AC\) becomes almost equal to \(BC\). So \(\sin A\to1\) and \(\cos A\to0\), and we define

    $$\sin90^\circ=1,\qquad \cos90^\circ=0.$$

    Hence \(\operatorname{cosec}90^\circ=1\) and \(\cot90^\circ=\dfrac{\cos90^\circ}{\sin90^\circ}=0\), while \(\tan90^\circ\) and \(\sec90^\circ\) are not defined (both would need division by \(\cos90^\circ=0\)).

If you prefer rationalised forms, \(\frac{1}{\sqrt2}=\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\), \(\frac{1}{\sqrt3}=\frac{\sqrt3}{3}\) and \(\frac{2}{\sqrt3}=\frac{2\sqrt3}{3}\) — both forms are correct.

    The whole table hangs on one line: \(\sin=\dfrac{\sqrt0}{2},\dfrac{\sqrt1}{2},\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2},\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2},\dfrac{\sqrt4}{2}\) for \(0^\circ,30^\circ,45^\circ,60^\circ,90^\circ\), which simplifies to \(0,\ \frac12,\ \frac{1}{\sqrt2},\ \frac{\sqrt3}{2},\ 1\). The cosine row is the same list backwards. Never memorise the other four rows — build them.

    Reading across the table: as \(\angle A\) increases from \(0^\circ\) to \(90^\circ\), \(\sin A\) increases from 0 to 1 and \(\cos A\) decreases from 1 to 0. That one sentence answers a whole family of true/false questions.

    NCERT Example 6find the missing sides

    In \(\triangle ABC\), right-angled at \(B\), \(AB=5\) cm and \(\angle ACB=30^\circ\). Find the lengths of \(BC\) and \(AC\).

    Solution

    Given: \(\angle B=90^\circ\), \(AB=5\) cm, \(\angle C=30^\circ\). To find: \(BC\) and \(AC\).

    Step 1 — find \(BC\). With respect to \(\angle C\), \(AB\) is the opposite side and \(BC\) is the adjacent side, so use the tangent.

    $$\begin{aligned} \tan C&=\frac{AB}{BC}\\ \tan30^\circ&=\frac{5}{BC}\\ \frac{1}{\sqrt3}&=\frac{5}{BC} \end{aligned}$$

    which gives \(BC=5\sqrt3\) cm.

    Step 2 — find \(AC\). With respect to \(\angle C\), \(AB\) is opposite and \(AC\) is the hypotenuse, so use the sine.

    $$\begin{aligned} \sin C&=\frac{AB}{AC}\\ \frac12&=\frac{5}{AC} \end{aligned}$$

    which gives \(AC=10\) cm.

    \(BC=5\sqrt3\) cm and \(AC=10\) cm

    Once \(BC\) is known you could instead use the Pythagoras theorem: \(AC=\sqrt{5^2+(5\sqrt3)^2}=\sqrt{25+75}=\sqrt{100}=10\) cm. Same answer — a good check.

    NCERT Example 7find the angles

    In \(\triangle PQR\), right-angled at \(Q\), \(PQ=3\) cm and \(PR=6\) cm. Find \(\angle QPR\) and \(\angle PRQ\).

    Solution

    Given: \(\angle Q=90^\circ\), \(PQ=3\) cm, \(PR=6\) cm.

    For \(\angle R\), the opposite side is \(PQ\) and the hypotenuse is \(PR\). So

    $$\sin R=\frac{PQ}{PR}=\frac36=\frac12$$

    From the table, the acute angle whose sine is \(\dfrac12\) is \(30^\circ\), so \(\angle PRQ=30^\circ\).

    The three angles of \(\triangle PQR\) add up to \(180^\circ\):

    $$\angle QPR=180^\circ-90^\circ-30^\circ=60^\circ$$

    \(\angle QPR=60^\circ\) and \(\angle PRQ=30^\circ\)

    Notice what has happened: one side and the hypotenuse were enough to fix both acute angles. In a right triangle, once you know any two parts apart from the right angle (two sides, or a side and an acute angle), everything else is determined.

    NCERT Example 8two angles, two equations

    If \(\sin(A-B)=\dfrac12\) and \(\cos(A+B)=\dfrac12\), where \(0^\circ\lt A+B\le90^\circ\) and \(A\gt B\), find \(A\) and \(B\).

    Solution

    Read each equation as “which angle has this ratio?”.

    \(\sin(A-B)=\dfrac12\) and from the table \(\sin30^\circ=\dfrac12\). Since \(A\gt B\), the angle \(A-B\) lies between \(0^\circ\) and \(90^\circ\), so

    $$A-B=30^\circ\qquad\ldots(1)$$

    \(\cos(A+B)=\dfrac12\) and from the table \(\cos60^\circ=\dfrac12\). Since \(0^\circ\lt A+B\le90^\circ\),

    $$A+B=60^\circ\qquad\ldots(2)$$

    Adding (1) and (2):

    $$2A=90^\circ\quad\Rightarrow\quad A=45^\circ$$

    Substituting \(A=45^\circ\) in (2): \(45^\circ+B=60^\circ\), so \(B=15^\circ\).

    \(A=45^\circ\) and \(B=15^\circ\)

    The restrictions \(0^\circ\lt A+B\le90^\circ\) and \(A\gt B\) are not decoration — they are what make the two angles unique. Quote them when you write “therefore \(A-B=30^\circ\)”.

    \(\sin(A-B)\) is not \(\sin A-\sin B\), and \(\cos(A+B)\) is not \(\cos A+\cos B\). The bracket is part of the angle. Check it once: \(\sin(60^\circ-30^\circ)=\sin30^\circ=\frac12\), but \(\sin60^\circ-\sin30^\circ=\frac{\sqrt3}{2}-\frac12\approx0.37\). Different.

    Evaluation questions (“find the value of \(2\tan^245^\circ+\cos^230^\circ-\sin^260^\circ\)”) are 1–2 marks and are pure table work. Write the substituted values first, in one line, then simplify — examiners give a mark for correct substitution even if the arithmetic slips.

    Evaluate \(\sin^230^\circ+\cos^245^\circ+\tan^260^\circ\).

    Show answer

    \(\left(\dfrac12\right)^2+\left(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\right)^2+(\sqrt3)^2=\dfrac14+\dfrac12+3=\dfrac{1+2+12}{4}=\dfrac{15}{4}\).

    8.4 Trigonometric identities

    Three short equations tie all six ratios together. They come straight out of the Pythagoras theorem, and once you have them you can go from any one ratio to any other without drawing a triangle at all.

    Definition

    An equation is called an identity when it is true for every value of the variables in it. An equation involving trigonometric ratios of an angle is a trigonometric identity if it is true for every value of that angle for which the ratios are defined.

    Compare the two. \(2x+3=7\) is an equation: it holds only for \(x=2\). \((x+1)^2=x^2+2x+1\) is an identity: it holds for every \(x\). In the same way \(\sin A=\frac12\) is an equation (it picks out \(A=30^\circ\)), while \(\sin^2A+\cos^2A=1\) is an identity.

    Where the three identities come from

    Take \(\triangle ABC\), right-angled at \(B\), and fix the acute angle \(A\). By the Pythagoras theorem,

    $$AB^2+BC^2=AC^2\qquad\ldots(1)$$

    Divide (1) by \(AC^2\).

    $$\begin{aligned} \frac{AB^2}{AC^2}+\frac{BC^2}{AC^2}&=\frac{AC^2}{AC^2}\\ \left(\frac{AB}{AC}\right)^2+\left(\frac{BC}{AC}\right)^2&=1\\ (\cos A)^2+(\sin A)^2&=1 \end{aligned}$$

    that is, \(\cos^2A+\sin^2A=1\). Since \(1\) and the ratios stay meaningful right up to the ends, this holds for every \(A\) with \(0^\circ\le A\le90^\circ\).

    Divide (1) by \(AB^2\).

    $$\begin{aligned} \frac{AB^2}{AB^2}+\frac{BC^2}{AB^2}&=\frac{AC^2}{AB^2}\\ 1+\left(\frac{BC}{AB}\right)^2&=\left(\frac{AC}{AB}\right)^2\\ 1+\tan^2A&=\sec^2A \end{aligned}$$

    Divide (1) by \(BC^2\).

    $$\begin{aligned} \frac{AB^2}{BC^2}+\frac{BC^2}{BC^2}&=\frac{AC^2}{BC^2}\\ \left(\frac{AB}{BC}\right)^2+1&=\left(\frac{AC}{BC}\right)^2\\ \cot^2A+1&=\operatorname{cosec}^2A \end{aligned}$$

    Nothing new was used — only the Pythagoras theorem and the definitions of the ratios. That is why these three are identities and not extra facts to remember separately.

    The three trigonometric identities
    $$\sin^2A+\cos^2A=1\qquad (0^\circ\le A\le90^\circ)$$ $$1+\tan^2A=\sec^2A\qquad (0^\circ\le A\lt90^\circ)$$ $$\cot^2A+1=\operatorname{cosec}^2A\qquad (0^\circ\lt A\le90^\circ)$$

    The end points are left out exactly where a ratio stops existing: \(\tan A\) and \(\sec A\) are not defined at \(90^\circ\), and \(\cot A\) and \(\operatorname{cosec} A\) are not defined at \(0^\circ\).

    Each identity is a right triangle in disguise, with the hypotenuse alone on the right: \((\sin A,\cos A,1)\), \((1,\tan A,\sec A)\), \((\cot A,1,\operatorname{cosec} A)\). Notice the pattern of the last two: the “co-” ratios sit together (\(\cot\) with \(\operatorname{cosec}\)), and the plain ones sit together (\(\tan\) with \(\sec\)).

    Learn the rearranged forms too — they are what you actually substitute:

    $$\sin^2A=1-\cos^2A,\quad \cos^2A=1-\sin^2A,$$ $$\sec^2A-\tan^2A=1,\; \operatorname{cosec}^2A-\cot^2A=1.$$

    Any one ratio gives all six

    Once you have the identities, you no longer need a triangle. Suppose \(\tan A=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt3}\). Then

    $$\cot A=\frac{1}{\tan A}=\sqrt3$$ $$\begin{aligned} \sec^2A&=1+\tan^2A=1+\frac13=\frac43\\ \Rightarrow\quad \sec A&=\frac{2}{\sqrt3} \end{aligned}$$ $$\cos A=\frac{1}{\sec A}=\frac{\sqrt3}{2}$$ $$\begin{aligned} \sin A&=\sqrt{1-\cos^2A}=\sqrt{1-\frac34}=\frac12\\ \Rightarrow\quad \operatorname{cosec} A&=2 \end{aligned}$$

    Only the positive square root is taken, because all six ratios of an acute angle are positive — the sides of a triangle are lengths.

    NCERT Example 9everything in one ratio

    Express \(\cos A\), \(\tan A\) and \(\sec A\) in terms of \(\sin A\).

    Solution

    Start from the first identity, \(\sin^2A+\cos^2A=1\). So

    $$\begin{aligned} \cos^2A&=1-\sin^2A\\ \text{i.e.}\quad \cos A&=\pm\sqrt{1-\sin^2A} \end{aligned}$$

    \(A\) is an acute angle of a right triangle, so \(\cos A\) is positive. Therefore

    $$\cos A=\sqrt{1-\sin^2A}$$

    Now use \(\tan A=\dfrac{\sin A}{\cos A}\) and \(\sec A=\dfrac{1}{\cos A}\):

    $$\begin{aligned} \tan A&=\frac{\sin A}{\sqrt{1-\sin^2A}}\\[2pt] \sec A&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\sin^2A}} \end{aligned}$$

    \(\cos A=\sqrt{1-\sin^2A}\), \(\tan A=\frac{\sin A}{\sqrt{1-\sin^2A}}\), \(\sec A=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\sin^2A}}\)

    Proving an identity

    You are given an equation and asked to show that the two sides always agree. The rules of the game: work on one side at a time and turn it into the other. Never move a term across the “=” as if it were an equation to solve.

    1. Pick the messier sidestart with whichever side has more terms, more fractions or more different ratios. Usually that is the LHS.
    2. Convert to \(\sin\) and \(\cos\)write every \(\tan,\cot,\sec,\operatorname{cosec}\) in terms of \(\sin\) and \(\cos\). This alone finishes most questions.
    3. Simplifytake an LCM, cancel common factors, or use \(\sin^2A+\cos^2A=1\) to replace \(1-\sin^2A\) by \(\cos^2A\) (and the other way round).
    4. Rationalise if a stubborn \(\pm1\) appearsmultiply numerator and denominator by the conjugate, and use \(\sec^2\theta-\tan^2\theta=1\) or \(\operatorname{cosec}^2\theta-\cot^2\theta=1\).
    5. Stop at the other sidewrite “= RHS” and finish with Hence proved.
    NCERT Example 10convert to sin and cos

    Prove that \(\sec A\,(1-\sin A)(\sec A+\tan A)=1\).

    Solution

    Take the left side and write everything in terms of \(\sin A\) and \(\cos A\).

    $$\begin{aligned} \text{LHS}&=\sec A\,(1-\sin A)(\sec A+\tan A)\\ &=\frac{1}{\cos A}\cdot(1-\sin A)\cdot\frac{1+\sin A}{\cos A}\\ &=\frac{(1-\sin A)(1+\sin A)}{\cos^2 A}\\ &=\frac{1-\sin^2A}{\cos^2A}\\ &=\frac{\cos^2A}{\cos^2A}=1=\text{RHS} \end{aligned}$$

    Line 3 uses \((a-b)(a+b)=a^2-b^2\); line 5 uses \(\sin^2A+\cos^2A=1\), so \(1-\sin^2A=\cos^2A\).

    Hence proved.

    NCERT Example 11take a common factor

    Prove that \(\dfrac{\cot A-\cos A}{\cot A+\cos A}=\dfrac{\operatorname{cosec} A-1}{\operatorname{cosec} A+1}\).

    Solution

    Replace \(\cot A\) by \(\dfrac{\cos A}{\sin A}\) on the left.

    $$\begin{aligned} \text{LHS}&=\frac{\cot A-\cos A}{\cot A+\cos A}\\ &=\frac{\dfrac{\cos A}{\sin A}-\cos A}{\dfrac{\cos A}{\sin A}+\cos A} \end{aligned}$$

    Take \(\cos A\) common from the numerator and from the denominator:

    $$\begin{aligned} &=\frac{\cos A\left(\dfrac{1}{\sin A}-1\right)}{\cos A\left(\dfrac{1}{\sin A}+1\right)}\\ &=\frac{\dfrac{1}{\sin A}-1}{\dfrac{1}{\sin A}+1}\\ &=\frac{\operatorname{cosec} A-1}{\operatorname{cosec} A+1}=\text{RHS} \end{aligned}$$

    Hence proved.

    The whole proof is one idea: \(\cos A\) is a common factor of both the numerator and the denominator, so it cancels. Spotting a common factor before taking an LCM saves half a page.

    NCERT Example 12rationalising an identity

    Prove that \(\dfrac{\sin\theta-\cos\theta+1}{\sin\theta+\cos\theta-1}=\dfrac{1}{\sec\theta-\tan\theta}\), using the identity \(\sec^2\theta=1+\tan^2\theta\).

    Solution

    The right side is built from \(\sec\theta\) and \(\tan\theta\), so first turn the left side into those. Divide the numerator and the denominator by \(\cos\theta\).

    $$\begin{aligned} \text{LHS}&=\frac{\sin\theta-\cos\theta+1}{\sin\theta+\cos\theta-1}\\ &=\frac{\tan\theta-1+\sec\theta}{\tan\theta+1-\sec\theta} \end{aligned}$$

    Now multiply the numerator and the denominator by \((\tan\theta-\sec\theta)\).

    $$\begin{aligned} &=\frac{\{(\tan\theta+\sec\theta)-1\}(\tan\theta-\sec\theta)}{\{(\tan\theta-\sec\theta)+1\}(\tan\theta-\sec\theta)} \end{aligned}$$

    Expand only the numerator:

    $$\begin{aligned} &(\tan\theta+\sec\theta)(\tan\theta-\sec\theta)\\ &\qquad-(\tan\theta-\sec\theta)\\ &=(\tan^2\theta-\sec^2\theta)\\ &\qquad-(\tan\theta-\sec\theta)\\ &=-1-\tan\theta+\sec\theta \end{aligned}$$

    using \(\sec^2\theta-\tan^2\theta=1\). So the left side is

    $$\begin{aligned} &\frac{-1-\tan\theta+\sec\theta}{(\tan\theta-\sec\theta+1)(\tan\theta-\sec\theta)}\\[2pt] &=\frac{-(1+\tan\theta-\sec\theta)}{(\tan\theta-\sec\theta+1)(\tan\theta-\sec\theta)}\\[2pt] &=\frac{-1}{\tan\theta-\sec\theta}\\[2pt] &=\frac{1}{\sec\theta-\tan\theta}=\text{RHS} \end{aligned}$$

    Hence proved.

    The bracket \((1+\tan\theta-\sec\theta)\) in the numerator is exactly the bracket \((\tan\theta-\sec\theta+1)\) in the denominator, so it cancels and only the minus sign survives. That cancellation is the point of multiplying by the conjugate.

    Two habits that lose marks in identity questions:

    1. Cross-multiplying. Writing “\(\text{LHS}=\text{RHS}\Rightarrow\) (numerator of LHS)(denominator of RHS) = …” assumes what you are asked to prove. Work down one side only.

    2. Forgetting to name the identity used. Write \(\text{\small[}\sin^2A+\cos^2A=1\text{]}\) beside the line where you use it. It is worth a mark in a 3-mark proof.

    Identity proofs carry 2 or 3 marks and appear in every paper. If a question has \(1\pm\sin\theta\) or \(\sec\theta\pm\tan\theta\) in a denominator, reach for the conjugate at once. If it is a mixture of \(\tan,\cot,\sec,\operatorname{cosec}\), convert everything to \(\sin\) and \(\cos\) — it is slower but it never fails.

    Prove that \((1+\cot^2\theta)(1-\cos\theta)(1+\cos\theta)=1\).

    Show answer

    \((1+\cot^2\theta)=\operatorname{cosec}^2\theta\) and \((1-\cos\theta)(1+\cos\theta)=1-\cos^2\theta=\sin^2\theta\).

    So the left side \(=\operatorname{cosec}^2\theta\times\sin^2\theta=\dfrac{\sin^2\theta}{\sin^2\theta}=1\). Hence proved.

    8.5 Summary

    The whole chapter in six lines. If you can write each of these from memory, with the figure that goes with it, you are ready for the exercises.

    1. In a right triangle \(ABC\), right-angled at \(B\): \(\sin A=\dfrac{\text{side opposite to }A}{\text{hypotenuse}}\), \(\cos A=\dfrac{\text{side adjacent to }A}{\text{hypotenuse}}\), \(\tan A=\dfrac{\text{side opposite to }A}{\text{side adjacent to }A}\).
    2. \(\operatorname{cosec} A=\dfrac{1}{\sin A}\); \(\sec A=\dfrac{1}{\cos A}\); \(\tan A=\dfrac{1}{\cot A}\); \(\tan A=\dfrac{\sin A}{\cos A}\).
    3. If one trigonometric ratio of an acute angle is known, all the remaining ratios of that angle can be found.
    4. The values of the six ratios at \(0^\circ,\ 30^\circ,\ 45^\circ,\ 60^\circ\) and \(90^\circ\) are those of Table 8.1 above.
    5. \(\sin A\) and \(\cos A\) never exceed 1, while \(\sec A\) \((0^\circ\le A\lt90^\circ)\) and \(\operatorname{cosec} A\) \((0^\circ\lt A\le90^\circ)\) are always greater than or equal to 1.
    6. \(\sin^2A+\cos^2A=1\); \(\sec^2A-\tan^2A=1\) for \(0^\circ\le A\lt90^\circ\); \(\operatorname{cosec}^2A=1+\cot^2A\) for \(0^\circ\lt A\le90^\circ\).

    Older editions also had a section on the trigonometric ratios of complementary angles. That section is not part of the current NCERT chapter, so it is not taught here and no solution on this site uses its formulas. Everything you need is done directly from the triangle.

    Exercise 8.1

    11 questions · naming the sides of a right triangle, writing the six ratios, and finding all the ratios when one of them is given

    In every question, first fix the angle that is being asked about. “Opposite” and “adjacent” are decided by that angle; the hypotenuse is always the side facing the right angle. Where a ratio is given, write the two sides as multiples of \(k\) and get the third side from the Pythagoras theorem — the \(k\) cancels at the end.

    Q1

    In \(\triangle ABC\), right-angled at \(B\), \(AB=24\) cm and \(BC=7\) cm. Determine:

    1. \(\sin A\) and \(\cos A\)
    2. \(\sin C\) and \(\cos C\)
    Solution

    Given: \(\angle B=90^\circ\), \(AB=24\) cm, \(BC=7\) cm. To find: the sine and cosine of \(\angle A\) and of \(\angle C\).

    Step 1 — find the hypotenuse. By the Pythagoras theorem,

    $$\begin{aligned} AC^2&=AB^2+BC^2\\ &=24^2+7^2\\ &=576+49=625 \end{aligned}$$

    So \(AC=\sqrt{625}=25\) cm.

    (i)

    For \(\angle A\): opposite side \(=BC=7\) cm, adjacent side \(=AB=24\) cm, hypotenuse \(=AC=25\) cm.

    $$\begin{aligned} \sin A&=\frac{BC}{AC}=\frac{7}{25}\\[2pt] \cos A&=\frac{AB}{AC}=\frac{24}{25} \end{aligned}$$

    \(\sin A=\dfrac{7}{25},\quad \cos A=\dfrac{24}{25}\)

    (ii)

    For \(\angle C\): the names swap. Opposite side \(=AB=24\) cm, adjacent side \(=BC=7\) cm, hypotenuse \(=AC=25\) cm.

    $$\begin{aligned} \sin C&=\frac{AB}{AC}=\frac{24}{25}\\[2pt] \cos C&=\frac{BC}{AC}=\frac{7}{25} \end{aligned}$$

    \(\sin C=\dfrac{24}{25},\quad \cos C=\dfrac{7}{25}\)

    Notice that \(\sin A=\cos C\) and \(\cos A=\sin C\). That is not a coincidence — the side that is opposite to \(A\) is adjacent to \(C\). Read it off the triangle every time; do not learn it as a formula.

    Q2

    In the figure, find \(\tan P-\cot R\). Here \(\triangle PQR\) is right-angled at \(Q\), with \(PQ=12\) cm and \(PR=13\) cm.

    Solution

    Given: \(\angle Q=90^\circ\), \(PQ=12\) cm, \(PR=13\) cm (the hypotenuse). To find: \(\tan P-\cot R\).

    Step 1 — find the third side. By the Pythagoras theorem,

    $$\begin{aligned} QR^2&=PR^2-PQ^2\\ &=13^2-12^2\\ &=169-144=25 \end{aligned}$$

    So \(QR=5\) cm.

    Step 2 — write each ratio. For \(\angle P\): opposite \(=QR=5\), adjacent \(=PQ=12\).

    $$\tan P=\frac{QR}{PQ}=\frac{5}{12}$$

    For \(\angle R\): opposite \(=PQ=12\), adjacent \(=QR=5\).

    $$\cot R=\frac{QR}{PQ}=\frac{5}{12}$$

    Step 3 — subtract.

    $$\tan P-\cot R=\frac{5}{12}-\frac{5}{12}=0$$

    \(\tan P-\cot R=0\)

    Both ratios are \(\dfrac{QR}{PQ}\): \(QR\) is opposite to \(P\) and adjacent to \(R\), while \(PQ\) is adjacent to \(P\) and opposite to \(R\). So the two fractions are the same fraction, written from two different corners.

    Q3

    If \(\sin A=\dfrac34\), calculate \(\cos A\) and \(\tan A\).

    Solution

    Given: \(\sin A=\dfrac34\). To find: \(\cos A\) and \(\tan A\).

    Draw \(\triangle ABC\) right-angled at \(B\) with \(\angle A\) marked. Then

    $$\sin A=\frac{BC}{AC}=\frac34$$

    so let \(BC=3k\) and \(AC=4k\), where \(k\) is a positive number.

    Find the third side. By the Pythagoras theorem,

    $$\begin{aligned} AB^2&=AC^2-BC^2\\ &=(4k)^2-(3k)^2\\ &=16k^2-9k^2=7k^2 \end{aligned}$$

    So \(AB=\sqrt7\,k\) (a length, so the positive root).

    Write the required ratios.

    $$\cos A=\frac{AB}{AC}=\frac{\sqrt7\,k}{4k}=\frac{\sqrt7}{4}$$ $$\tan A=\frac{BC}{AB}=\frac{3k}{\sqrt7\,k}=\frac{3}{\sqrt7}$$

    \(\cos A=\dfrac{\sqrt7}{4}\) and \(\tan A=\dfrac{3}{\sqrt7}\)

    The official answer leaves \(\tan A\) as \(\dfrac{3}{\sqrt7}\). If your teacher wants a rationalised denominator, write \(\dfrac{3}{\sqrt7}=\dfrac{3\sqrt7}{7}\) — the two are the same number.

    Q4

    Given \(15\cot A=8\), find \(\sin A\) and \(\sec A\).

    Solution

    Given: \(15\cot A=8\), that is \(\cot A=\dfrac{8}{15}\). To find: \(\sin A\) and \(\sec A\).

    Draw \(\triangle ABC\) right-angled at \(B\) with \(\angle A\) marked. Since \(\cot A=\dfrac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{opposite}}=\dfrac{AB}{BC}=\dfrac{8}{15}\), let

    $$AB=8k,\qquad BC=15k\qquad (k\gt0)$$

    Find the hypotenuse.

    $$\begin{aligned} AC^2&=AB^2+BC^2\\ &=(8k)^2+(15k)^2\\ &=64k^2+225k^2=289k^2 \end{aligned}$$

    So \(AC=17k\).

    Write the required ratios.

    $$\sin A=\frac{BC}{AC}=\frac{15k}{17k}=\frac{15}{17}$$ $$\begin{aligned} \cos A&=\frac{AB}{AC}=\frac{8k}{17k}=\frac{8}{17}\\ \Rightarrow\quad \sec A&=\frac{1}{\cos A}=\frac{17}{8} \end{aligned}$$

    \(\sin A=\dfrac{15}{17}\) and \(\sec A=\dfrac{17}{8}\)

    Do not read \(15\cot A=8\) as \(\cot A=\dfrac{15}{8}\). Divide by 15 first: \(\cot A=\dfrac{8}{15}\).

    Q5

    Given \(\sec\theta=\dfrac{13}{12}\), calculate all the other trigonometric ratios of \(\theta\).

    Solution

    Given: \(\sec\theta=\dfrac{13}{12}\). To find: \(\sin\theta,\ \cos\theta,\ \tan\theta,\ \cot\theta,\ \operatorname{cosec}\theta\).

    Draw a right triangle \(ABC\), right-angled at \(B\), with \(\angle A=\theta\). Since

    $$\sec\theta=\frac{\text{hypotenuse}}{\text{adjacent}}=\frac{AC}{AB}=\frac{13}{12},$$

    let \(AC=13k\) and \(AB=12k\), where \(k\gt0\).

    Find the third side.

    $$\begin{aligned} BC^2&=AC^2-AB^2\\ &=(13k)^2-(12k)^2\\ &=169k^2-144k^2=25k^2 \end{aligned}$$

    So \(BC=5k\).

    Now write every ratio.

    $$\begin{aligned} \sin\theta&=\frac{BC}{AC}=\frac{5k}{13k}=\frac{5}{13}\\[2pt] \cos\theta&=\frac{AB}{AC}=\frac{12k}{13k}=\frac{12}{13}\\[2pt] \tan\theta&=\frac{BC}{AB}=\frac{5k}{12k}=\frac{5}{12}\\[2pt] \cot\theta&=\frac{1}{\tan\theta}=\frac{12}{5}\\[2pt] \operatorname{cosec}\theta&=\frac{1}{\sin\theta}=\frac{13}{5} \end{aligned}$$

    \(\sin\theta=\frac{5}{13}\), \(\cos\theta=\frac{12}{13}\), \(\tan\theta=\frac{5}{12}\), \(\cot\theta=\frac{12}{5}\), \(\operatorname{cosec}\theta=\frac{13}{5}\)

    Quick check with the first identity: \(\left(\frac{5}{13}\right)^2+\left(\frac{12}{13}\right)^2=\frac{25+144}{169}=1\). ✓

    Q6

    \(\angle A\) and \(\angle B\) are acute angles such that \(\cos A=\cos B\). Show that \(\angle A=\angle B\).

    Solution

    Given: \(\angle A\) and \(\angle B\) are acute and \(\cos A=\cos B\). To prove: \(\angle A=\angle B\).

    Construction. Place the two angles in one triangle \(ABC\), with \(\angle A\) at \(A\) and \(\angle B\) at \(B\). Draw \(CD\perp AB\), meeting \(AB\) at \(D\). Since both \(\angle A\) and \(\angle B\) are acute, \(D\) lies between \(A\) and \(B\).

    Proof. In right \(\triangle ADC\) (right angle at \(D\)) and right \(\triangle BDC\) (right angle at \(D\)),

    $$\cos A=\frac{AD}{AC},\qquad \cos B=\frac{BD}{BC}$$

    Since \(\cos A=\cos B\),

    $$\frac{AD}{AC}=\frac{BD}{BC} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{AD}{BD}=\frac{AC}{BC}=k\ \ \text{(say)}$$

    so \(AD=k\,BD\) and \(AC=k\,BC\).

    Now apply the Pythagoras theorem to each right triangle and use these two results:

    $$\begin{aligned} CD^2&=AC^2-AD^2\\ &=k^2BC^2-k^2BD^2\\ &=k^2\left(BC^2-BD^2\right)\\ &=k^2\,CD^2 \end{aligned}$$

    [in \(\triangle BDC\), \(BC^2-BD^2=CD^2\)]

    Since \(CD\ne0\), dividing by \(CD^2\) gives \(k^2=1\), and as \(k\) is a ratio of lengths, \(k=1\).

    Therefore \(AC=BC\), so \(\triangle ABC\) is isosceles.

    In a triangle, the angles opposite equal sides are equal. \(\angle A\) is opposite \(BC\) and \(\angle B\) is opposite \(AC\), and \(BC=AC\).

    Hence \(\angle A=\angle B\).

    The same argument works with \(\sin\) or \(\tan\) in place of \(\cos\) (see NCERT Example 2 in the Learn tab). The moral: for acute angles, equal ratio means equal angle — which is why a ratio can name an angle.

    Q7

    If \(\cot\theta=\dfrac78\), evaluate:

    1. \(\dfrac{(1+\sin\theta)(1-\sin\theta)}{(1+\cos\theta)(1-\cos\theta)}\)
    2. \(\cot^2\theta\)
    Solution

    Given: \(\cot\theta=\dfrac78\).

    Draw \(\triangle ABC\) right-angled at \(B\) with \(\angle A=\theta\). Then \(\cot\theta=\dfrac{AB}{BC}=\dfrac78\), so let \(AB=7k\) and \(BC=8k\).

    $$\begin{aligned} AC^2&=AB^2+BC^2\\ &=49k^2+64k^2=113k^2 \end{aligned}$$

    So \(AC=\sqrt{113}\,k\), and

    $$\begin{aligned} \sin\theta&=\frac{8k}{\sqrt{113}\,k}=\frac{8}{\sqrt{113}}\\[2pt] \cos\theta&=\frac{7k}{\sqrt{113}\,k}=\frac{7}{\sqrt{113}} \end{aligned}$$
    (i)

    Use \((a+b)(a-b)=a^2-b^2\) on the numerator and on the denominator:

    $$\begin{aligned} \frac{(1+\sin\theta)(1-\sin\theta)}{(1+\cos\theta)(1-\cos\theta)} &=\frac{1-\sin^2\theta}{1-\cos^2\theta}\\ &=\frac{1-\dfrac{64}{113}}{1-\dfrac{49}{113}}\\ &=\frac{\dfrac{49}{113}}{\dfrac{64}{113}}=\frac{49}{64} \end{aligned}$$

    \(\dfrac{49}{64}\)

    (ii)
    $$\cot^2\theta=\left(\frac78\right)^2=\frac{49}{64}$$

    \(\dfrac{49}{64}\)

    The two answers are equal, and they had to be: \(1-\sin^2\theta=\cos^2\theta\) and \(1-\cos^2\theta=\sin^2\theta\), so part (i) is just \(\dfrac{\cos^2\theta}{\sin^2\theta}=\cot^2\theta\). Doing it that way you can write the answer in one line, without ever finding \(AC\).

    Q8

    If \(3\cot A=4\), check whether \(\dfrac{1-\tan^2A}{1+\tan^2A}=\cos^2A-\sin^2A\) or not.

    Solution

    Given: \(3\cot A=4\), that is \(\cot A=\dfrac43\), so \(\tan A=\dfrac34\). To check: whether the two sides are equal.

    Draw \(\triangle ABC\) right-angled at \(B\) with \(\angle A\) marked. Since \(\tan A=\dfrac{BC}{AB}=\dfrac34\), let \(AB=4k\) and \(BC=3k\).

    $$\begin{aligned} AC^2&=(4k)^2+(3k)^2=25k^2\\ AC&=5k \end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned} \sin A&=\frac{3k}{5k}=\frac35\\[2pt] \cos A&=\frac{4k}{5k}=\frac45 \end{aligned}$$

    Left-hand side.

    $$\begin{aligned} \frac{1-\tan^2A}{1+\tan^2A} &=\frac{1-\left(\frac34\right)^2}{1+\left(\frac34\right)^2}\\ &=\frac{1-\dfrac{9}{16}}{1+\dfrac{9}{16}}\\ &=\frac{\dfrac{7}{16}}{\dfrac{25}{16}}=\frac{7}{25} \end{aligned}$$

    Right-hand side.

    $$\begin{aligned} \cos^2A-\sin^2A&=\left(\frac45\right)^2-\left(\frac35\right)^2\\ &=\frac{16}{25}-\frac{9}{25}=\frac{7}{25} \end{aligned}$$

    Both sides equal \(\dfrac{7}{25}\).

    Write every one of the other trigonometric ratios of \(\angle A\) using \(\sec A\) alone.

    Solution

    To find: \(\cos A\), \(\sin A\), \(\tan A\), \(\cot A\) and \(\operatorname{cosec}A\), each written with \(\sec A\) only. The identity that contains \(\sec A\) is \(1+\tan^2A=\sec^2A\). Again \(A\) is acute, so all roots are positive.

    Step 1 — \(\cos A\).

    \(\cos A=\dfrac{1}{\sec A}\) [\(\cos\) and \(\sec\) are reciprocals]

    Step 2 — \(\tan A\).

    \(1+\tan^2A=\sec^2A\) [identity]

    \(\tan^2A=\sec^2A-1\)

    \(\tan A=\sqrt{\sec^2A-1}\) [positive root]

    Step 3 — \(\sin A\).

    \(\sin^2A=1-\cos^2A\) [\(\sin^2A+\cos^2A=1\)]

    \(\sin^2A=1-\dfrac{1}{\sec^2A}\) [Step 1]

    \(\sin^2A=\dfrac{\sec^2A-1}{\sec^2A}\)

    \(\sin A=\dfrac{\sqrt{\sec^2A-1}}{\sec A}\) [positive root]

    Step 4 — \(\operatorname{cosec}A\).

    \(\operatorname{cosec}A=\dfrac{1}{\sin A}=\dfrac{\sec A}{\sqrt{\sec^2A-1}}\) [reciprocal of Step 3]

    Step 5 — \(\cot A\).

    \(\cot A=\dfrac{1}{\tan A}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\sec^2A-1}}\) [reciprocal of Step 2]

    \(\sin A=\dfrac{\sqrt{\sec^2A-1}}{\sec A}\),   \(\cos A=\dfrac{1}{\sec A}\),   \(\tan A=\sqrt{\sec^2A-1}\),   \(\cot A=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\sec^2A-1}}\),   \(\operatorname{cosec}A=\dfrac{\sec A}{\sqrt{\sec^2A-1}}\)

    Second way (the triangle in the figure). In \(\triangle ABC\), right-angled at \(B\), put \(AC=\sec A\) and \(AB=1\), so that \(\sec A=\dfrac{AC}{AB}\) is correct. By Pythagoras \(BC=\sqrt{\sec^2A-1}\). Reading the five ratios straight off the triangle gives exactly the answers above.

    Two habits worth building: (1) start from the identity that already holds the ratio you are given; (2) get one new ratio first, then take reciprocals — you never have to work out all five from scratch.

    Q3

    Choose the correct option in each part, and justify your choice.

    1. \(9\sec^2A-9\tan^2A=\)  (A) \(1\)  (B) \(9\)  (C) \(8\)  (D) \(0\)
    2. \((1+\tan\theta+\sec\theta)(1+\cot\theta-\operatorname{cosec}\theta)=\)  (A) \(0\)  (B) \(1\)  (C) \(2\)  (D) \(-1\)
    3. \((\sec A+\tan A)(1-\sin A)=\)  (A) \(\sec A\)  (B) \(\sin A\)  (C) \(\operatorname{cosec}A\)  (D) \(\cos A\)
    4. \(\dfrac{1+\tan^2A}{1+\cot^2A}=\)  (A) \(\sec^2A\)  (B) \(-1\)  (C) \(\cot^2A\)  (D) \(\tan^2A\)
    Solution

    Method: simplify the given expression until it collapses to one of the four options. Never substitute a convenient angle and stop there — the board wants the working.

    (i)

    \(9\sec^2A-9\tan^2A\)

    \(=9\,(\sec^2A-\tan^2A)\) [take 9 common]

    \(=9\times1\) [\(1+\tan^2A=\sec^2A\), so \(\sec^2A-\tan^2A=1\)]

    \(=9\)

    (B) \(9\)

    (ii)

    Put everything in \(\sin\theta\) and \(\cos\theta\).

    \((1+\tan\theta+\sec\theta)(1+\cot\theta-\operatorname{cosec}\theta)\)

    \(=\left(1+\dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}+\dfrac{1}{\cos\theta}\right)\) \(\times\left(1+\dfrac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}-\dfrac{1}{\sin\theta}\right)\)

    \(=\dfrac{\cos\theta+\sin\theta+1}{\cos\theta}\times\dfrac{\sin\theta+\cos\theta-1}{\sin\theta}\) [one denominator in each bracket]

    \(=\dfrac{(\sin\theta+\cos\theta)^2-1}{\sin\theta\cos\theta}\) [\((a+b)(a-b)=a^2-b^2\), with \(a=\sin\theta+\cos\theta\) and \(b=1\)]

    \(=\dfrac{\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta+2\sin\theta\cos\theta-1}{\sin\theta\cos\theta}\)

    \(=\dfrac{1+2\sin\theta\cos\theta-1}{\sin\theta\cos\theta}\) [\(\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1\)]

    \(=\dfrac{2\sin\theta\cos\theta}{\sin\theta\cos\theta}=2\)

    (C) \(2\)

    (iii)

    \((\sec A+\tan A)(1-\sin A)\)

    \(=\left(\dfrac{1}{\cos A}+\dfrac{\sin A}{\cos A}\right)(1-\sin A)\) [in \(\sin\) and \(\cos\)]

    \(=\dfrac{(1+\sin A)(1-\sin A)}{\cos A}\)

    \(=\dfrac{1-\sin^2A}{\cos A}\) [\((a+b)(a-b)=a^2-b^2\)]

    \(=\dfrac{\cos^2A}{\cos A}\) [\(1-\sin^2A=\cos^2A\)]

    \(=\cos A\)

    (D) \(\cos A\)

    (iv)

    \(\dfrac{1+\tan^2A}{1+\cot^2A}\)

    \(=\dfrac{\sec^2A}{\operatorname{cosec}^2A}\) [\(1+\tan^2A=\sec^2A\) and \(1+\cot^2A=\operatorname{cosec}^2A\)]

    \(=\dfrac{1}{\cos^2A}\times\dfrac{\sin^2A}{1}\) [reciprocals]

    \(=\dfrac{\sin^2A}{\cos^2A}=\tan^2A\)

    (D) \(\tan^2A\)

    (i) B   (ii) C   (iii) D   (iv) D

    In (iv), \(\dfrac{1+\tan^2A}{1+\cot^2A}\) is not \(\sec^2A\). \(\sec^2A\) is only the top. Divide by the bottom as well, and the answer turns into \(\tan^2A\) — option (A) is the trap.

    Q4

    Prove the following identities. In every part the angle is acute, and small enough or large enough for all the ratios written down to exist.

    1. \((\operatorname{cosec}\theta-\cot\theta)^2=\dfrac{1-\cos\theta}{1+\cos\theta}\)
    2. \(\dfrac{\cos A}{1+\sin A}+\dfrac{1+\sin A}{\cos A}=2\sec A\)
    3. \(\dfrac{\tan\theta}{1-\cot\theta}+\dfrac{\cot\theta}{1-\tan\theta}=1+\sec\theta\operatorname{cosec}\theta\)  (Hint: write the whole expression in \(\sin\theta\) and \(\cos\theta\).)
    4. \(\dfrac{1+\sec A}{\sec A}=\dfrac{\sin^2A}{1-\cos A}\)  (Hint: simplify the two sides separately.)
    5. \(\dfrac{\cos A-\sin A+1}{\cos A+\sin A-1}=\operatorname{cosec}A+\cot A\), using \(\operatorname{cosec}^2A=1+\cot^2A\)
    6. \(\sqrt{\dfrac{1+\sin A}{1-\sin A}}=\sec A+\tan A\)
    7. \(\dfrac{\sin\theta-2\sin^3\theta}{2\cos^3\theta-\cos\theta}=\tan\theta\)
    8. \((\sin A+\operatorname{cosec}A)^2+(\cos A+\sec A)^2=7+\tan^2A+\cot^2A\)
    9. \((\operatorname{cosec}A-\sin A)(\sec A-\cos A)=\dfrac{1}{\tan A+\cot A}\)  (Hint: simplify the two sides separately.)
    10. \(\left(\dfrac{1+\tan^2A}{1+\cot^2A}\right)=\left(\dfrac{1-\tan A}{1-\cot A}\right)^2=\tan^2A\)
    Solution

    How every one of these is written in the exam: start from the side that is heavier, change it one step at a time, name the identity used at each step, and stop only when the last line is the other side. Never move terms across the \(=\) sign — you are not solving an equation, you are rewriting one side.

    (i)

    \(\text{LHS}=(\operatorname{cosec}\theta-\cot\theta)^2\)

    \(=\left(\dfrac{1}{\sin\theta}-\dfrac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}\right)^2\) [\(\operatorname{cosec}\theta=\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\), \(\cot\theta=\frac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}\)]

    \(=\left(\dfrac{1-\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}\right)^2\) [same denominator]

    \(=\dfrac{(1-\cos\theta)^2}{\sin^2\theta}\) [square top and bottom]

    \(=\dfrac{(1-\cos\theta)^2}{1-\cos^2\theta}\) [\(\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1\)]

    \(=\dfrac{(1-\cos\theta)^2}{(1-\cos\theta)(1+\cos\theta)}\) [\(a^2-b^2=(a-b)(a+b)\)]

    \(=\dfrac{1-\cos\theta}{1+\cos\theta}=\text{RHS}\) [cancel one \((1-\cos\theta)\)]

    Hence proved.
    (ii)

    \(\text{LHS}=\dfrac{\cos A}{1+\sin A}+\dfrac{1+\sin A}{\cos A}\)

    \(=\dfrac{\cos^2A+(1+\sin A)^2}{(1+\sin A)\cos A}\) [one common denominator]

    \(=\dfrac{\cos^2A+1+2\sin A+\sin^2A}{(1+\sin A)\cos A}\) [\((a+b)^2=a^2+2ab+b^2\)]

    \(=\dfrac{(\sin^2A+\cos^2A)+1+2\sin A}{(1+\sin A)\cos A}\) [regroup]

    \(=\dfrac{2+2\sin A}{(1+\sin A)\cos A}\) [\(\sin^2A+\cos^2A=1\)]

    \(=\dfrac{2(1+\sin A)}{(1+\sin A)\cos A}\) [take 2 common]

    \(=\dfrac{2}{\cos A}\) [cancel \((1+\sin A)\)]

    \(=2\sec A=\text{RHS}\) [\(\frac{1}{\cos A}=\sec A\)]

    Hence proved.
    (iii)

    \(\text{LHS}=\dfrac{\tan\theta}{1-\cot\theta}+\dfrac{\cot\theta}{1-\tan\theta}\)

    \(=\dfrac{\frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}}{1-\frac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}}+\dfrac{\frac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}}{1-\frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}}\) [everything in \(\sin\theta\) and \(\cos\theta\)]

    \(=\dfrac{\frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}}{\frac{\sin\theta-\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}}+\dfrac{\frac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}}{\frac{\cos\theta-\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}}\) [tidy each small denominator]

    \(=\dfrac{\sin^2\theta}{\cos\theta\,(\sin\theta-\cos\theta)}+\dfrac{\cos^2\theta}{\sin\theta\,(\cos\theta-\sin\theta)}\) [divide by turning upside down]

    \(=\dfrac{\sin^2\theta}{\cos\theta\,(\sin\theta-\cos\theta)}-\dfrac{\cos^2\theta}{\sin\theta\,(\sin\theta-\cos\theta)}\) [\(\cos\theta-\sin\theta=-(\sin\theta-\cos\theta)\)]

    \(=\dfrac{\sin^3\theta-\cos^3\theta}{\sin\theta\cos\theta\,(\sin\theta-\cos\theta)}\) [one common denominator]

    Factorise the top on its own:

    \(\sin^3\theta-\cos^3\theta\)

    \(=(\sin\theta-\cos\theta)\) \((\sin^2\theta+\sin\theta\cos\theta+\cos^2\theta)\) [\(a^3-b^3=(a-b)(a^2+ab+b^2)\)]

    \(=(\sin\theta-\cos\theta)\,(1+\sin\theta\cos\theta)\) [\(\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1\)]

    Put that back:

    \(\text{LHS}=\dfrac{(\sin\theta-\cos\theta)(1+\sin\theta\cos\theta)}{\sin\theta\cos\theta\,(\sin\theta-\cos\theta)}\)

    \(=\dfrac{1+\sin\theta\cos\theta}{\sin\theta\cos\theta}\) [cancel \((\sin\theta-\cos\theta)\)]

    \(=\dfrac{1}{\sin\theta\cos\theta}+1\) [split the fraction]

    \(=1+\sec\theta\operatorname{cosec}\theta=\text{RHS}\) [\(\frac{1}{\cos\theta}=\sec\theta\), \(\frac{1}{\sin\theta}=\operatorname{cosec}\theta\)]

    Hence proved.
    (iv)

    The two sides look nothing alike, so simplify each on its own.

    Left side.

    \(\text{LHS}=\dfrac{1+\sec A}{\sec A}\)

    \(=\dfrac{1}{\sec A}+\dfrac{\sec A}{\sec A}\) [split the fraction]

    \(=\cos A+1\) [\(\frac{1}{\sec A}=\cos A\)]

    Right side.

    \(\text{RHS}=\dfrac{\sin^2A}{1-\cos A}\)

    \(=\dfrac{1-\cos^2A}{1-\cos A}\) [\(\sin^2A=1-\cos^2A\)]

    \(=\dfrac{(1-\cos A)(1+\cos A)}{1-\cos A}\) [\(a^2-b^2=(a-b)(a+b)\)]

    \(=1+\cos A\) [cancel \((1-\cos A)\)]

    Both sides reduce to \(1+\cos A\), so \(\text{LHS}=\text{RHS}\).

    Hence proved.
    (v)

    The right side is made of \(\operatorname{cosec}A\) and \(\cot A\), so turn the left side into those two. Divide every term above and below by \(\sin A\).

    \(\text{LHS}=\dfrac{\cos A-\sin A+1}{\cos A+\sin A-1}\)

    \(=\dfrac{\frac{\cos A}{\sin A}-\frac{\sin A}{\sin A}+\frac{1}{\sin A}}{\frac{\cos A}{\sin A}+\frac{\sin A}{\sin A}-\frac{1}{\sin A}}\) [divide top and bottom by \(\sin A\)]

    \(=\dfrac{\cot A-1+\operatorname{cosec}A}{\cot A+1-\operatorname{cosec}A}\) [\(\frac{\cos A}{\sin A}=\cot A\), \(\frac{1}{\sin A}=\operatorname{cosec}A\)]

    Write \(D=\cot A+1-\operatorname{cosec}A\) for the bottom line, and work on the top alone. The \(-1\) in it may be replaced by \(-(\operatorname{cosec}^2A-\cot^2A)\), because \(\operatorname{cosec}^2A=1+\cot^2A\) makes \(\operatorname{cosec}^2A-\cot^2A=1\).

    Top \(=\cot A+\operatorname{cosec}A-1\)

    \(=(\cot A+\operatorname{cosec}A)\) \(-(\operatorname{cosec}^2A-\cot^2A)\) [\(1=\operatorname{cosec}^2A-\cot^2A\)]

    \(=(\cot A+\operatorname{cosec}A)\) \(-(\operatorname{cosec}A-\cot A)(\operatorname{cosec}A+\cot A)\) [\(a^2-b^2=(a-b)(a+b)\)]

    \(=(\cot A+\operatorname{cosec}A)\) \(\big[1-(\operatorname{cosec}A-\cot A)\big]\) [take \((\cot A+\operatorname{cosec}A)\) common]

    \(=(\cot A+\operatorname{cosec}A)\,D\) [the square bracket is \(\cot A+1-\operatorname{cosec}A\), which is \(D\)]

    Putting the top back over the bottom,

    \(\text{LHS}=\dfrac{(\cot A+\operatorname{cosec}A)\,D}{D}\)

    \(=\cot A+\operatorname{cosec}A\) [cancel \(D\)]

    \(=\operatorname{cosec}A+\cot A=\text{RHS}\)

    Hence proved.
    (vi)

    \(\text{LHS}=\sqrt{\dfrac{1+\sin A}{1-\sin A}}\)

    \(=\sqrt{\dfrac{(1+\sin A)(1+\sin A)}{(1-\sin A)(1+\sin A)}}\) [multiply inside by \(\frac{1+\sin A}{1+\sin A}\)]

    \(=\sqrt{\dfrac{(1+\sin A)^2}{1-\sin^2A}}\) [\(a^2-b^2=(a-b)(a+b)\)]

    \(=\sqrt{\dfrac{(1+\sin A)^2}{\cos^2A}}\) [\(\sin^2A+\cos^2A=1\)]

    \(=\dfrac{1+\sin A}{\cos A}\) [\(A\) acute, so \(1+\sin A\gt0\) and \(\cos A\gt0\)]

    \(=\dfrac{1}{\cos A}+\dfrac{\sin A}{\cos A}\) [split the fraction]

    \(=\sec A+\tan A=\text{RHS}\) [\(\frac{1}{\cos A}=\sec A\), \(\frac{\sin A}{\cos A}=\tan A\)]

    Hence proved.

    The trick in line 2 is the same “rationalising” move you use on \(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt3-1}\): multiply above and below by the conjugate so that the bottom becomes a difference of squares.

    (vii)

    \(\text{LHS}=\dfrac{\sin\theta-2\sin^3\theta}{2\cos^3\theta-\cos\theta}\)

    \(=\dfrac{\sin\theta\,(1-2\sin^2\theta)}{\cos\theta\,(2\cos^2\theta-1)}\) [take \(\sin\theta\) and \(\cos\theta\) common]

    Work on the bracket on top:

    \(1-2\sin^2\theta=1-2(1-\cos^2\theta)\) [\(\sin^2\theta=1-\cos^2\theta\)]

    \(=1-2+2\cos^2\theta=2\cos^2\theta-1\)

    The two brackets are the same, so

    \(\text{LHS}=\dfrac{\sin\theta\,(2\cos^2\theta-1)}{\cos\theta\,(2\cos^2\theta-1)}\) [put the bracket back]

    \(=\dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}\) [cancel \((2\cos^2\theta-1)\)]

    \(=\tan\theta=\text{RHS}\)

    Hence proved.
    (viii)

    \(\text{LHS}=(\sin A+\operatorname{cosec}A)^2+(\cos A+\sec A)^2\)

    \(=\sin^2A+2\sin A\operatorname{cosec}A+\operatorname{cosec}^2A\) \(+\ \cos^2A+2\cos A\sec A+\sec^2A\) [\((a+b)^2=a^2+2ab+b^2\), twice]

    \(=(\sin^2A+\cos^2A)+2\sin A\operatorname{cosec}A\) \(+\ 2\cos A\sec A+\operatorname{cosec}^2A+\sec^2A\) [regroup]

    \(=1+2(1)+2(1)+\operatorname{cosec}^2A+\sec^2A\) [\(\sin A\operatorname{cosec}A=1\), \(\cos A\sec A=1\), \(\sin^2A+\cos^2A=1\)]

    \(=5+\operatorname{cosec}^2A+\sec^2A\)

    \(=5+(1+\cot^2A)+(1+\tan^2A)\) [\(\operatorname{cosec}^2A=1+\cot^2A\), \(\sec^2A=1+\tan^2A\)]

    \(=7+\tan^2A+\cot^2A=\text{RHS}\)

    Hence proved.
    (ix)

    Simplify each side on its own.

    Left side.

    \(\text{LHS}=(\operatorname{cosec}A-\sin A)(\sec A-\cos A)\)

    \(=\left(\dfrac{1}{\sin A}-\sin A\right)\) \(\left(\dfrac{1}{\cos A}-\cos A\right)\) [in \(\sin\) and \(\cos\)]

    \(=\dfrac{1-\sin^2A}{\sin A}\times\dfrac{1-\cos^2A}{\cos A}\) [one denominator in each bracket]

    \(=\dfrac{\cos^2A}{\sin A}\times\dfrac{\sin^2A}{\cos A}\) [\(1-\sin^2A=\cos^2A\), \(1-\cos^2A=\sin^2A\)]

    \(=\sin A\cos A\) [cancel one \(\sin A\) and one \(\cos A\)]

    Right side.

    \(\text{RHS}=\dfrac{1}{\tan A+\cot A}\)

    \(=\dfrac{1}{\frac{\sin A}{\cos A}+\frac{\cos A}{\sin A}}\) [in \(\sin\) and \(\cos\)]

    \(=\dfrac{1}{\frac{\sin^2A+\cos^2A}{\sin A\cos A}}\) [add the two fractions]

    \(=\dfrac{1}{\frac{1}{\sin A\cos A}}\) [\(\sin^2A+\cos^2A=1\)]

    \(=\sin A\cos A\) [turn the fraction upside down]

    Both sides come to \(\sin A\cos A\), so \(\text{LHS}=\text{RHS}\).

    Hence proved.
    (x)

    There are two statements here. Show that each of the two expressions equals \(\tan^2A\); that also makes them equal to each other.

    First expression.

    \(\dfrac{1+\tan^2A}{1+\cot^2A}\)

    \(=\dfrac{\sec^2A}{\operatorname{cosec}^2A}\) [\(1+\tan^2A=\sec^2A\), \(1+\cot^2A=\operatorname{cosec}^2A\)]

    \(=\dfrac{1}{\cos^2A}\times\dfrac{\sin^2A}{1}\) [reciprocals]

    \(=\dfrac{\sin^2A}{\cos^2A}=\tan^2A\)  …(1)

    Second expression. First tidy the denominator:

    \(1-\cot A=1-\dfrac{1}{\tan A}\) [\(\cot A=\frac{1}{\tan A}\)]

    \(=\dfrac{\tan A-1}{\tan A}\) [one denominator]

    So

    \(\dfrac{1-\tan A}{1-\cot A}=\dfrac{1-\tan A}{\frac{\tan A-1}{\tan A}}\)

    \(=\dfrac{(1-\tan A)\tan A}{\tan A-1}\) [divide by turning upside down]

    \(=\dfrac{-(\tan A-1)\tan A}{\tan A-1}\) [\(1-\tan A=-(\tan A-1)\)]

    \(=-\tan A\) [cancel \((\tan A-1)\)]

    \(\therefore\ \left(\dfrac{1-\tan A}{1-\cot A}\right)^2=(-\tan A)^2=\tan^2A\)  …(2)

    From (1) and (2), both expressions equal \(\tan^2A\), so

    \(\left(\dfrac{1+\tan^2A}{1+\cot^2A}\right)=\left(\dfrac{1-\tan A}{1-\cot A}\right)^2=\tan^2A\)

    Hence proved.

    The minus sign in \(-\tan A\) disappears only because of the square. Losing the bracket and writing \(\tan A\) at that step still reaches the right answer, but it is wrong mathematics and examiners cut a mark for it.

    The three moves that finish nine of these ten proofs: (1) write every ratio as \(\sin\) and \(\cos\); (2) make one denominator; (3) use \(\sin^2+\cos^2=1\), or \(a^2-b^2=(a-b)(a+b)\), to create a factor you can cancel. Try them in that order before looking for anything cleverer.

    A proof is marked line by line. Write \(\text{LHS}=\ldots\) at the start, keep every line under the one before it, and finish with \(=\text{RHS}\) and the words “Hence proved”. Jumping three steps at once loses the method marks even when the last line is right.

    By symmetry \(AC=AB\), so each rafter is about 6.92 m long.

    \(4\sqrt3\approx6.92\) m each

    OR: With a pitch of \(60^\circ\) and \(BD\) still 6 m,

    $$\begin{aligned} \tan 60^\circ&=\frac{AD'}{6}\\ AD'&=6\sqrt3=10.38\ \text{m} \end{aligned}$$

    Increase in height \(=6\sqrt3-2\sqrt3=4\sqrt3=6.92\) m.

    New height \(\approx10.38\) m; it is about 6.92 m taller.

    Check part (iii) with the Pythagoras theorem: \(6^2+(2\sqrt3)^2=36+12=48=(4\sqrt3)^2\) ✓

    Introduction to Trigonometry

    The six ratios

    • Right triangle only; \(\theta\) is one acute angle
    • \(\sin=\dfrac{\text{opp}}{\text{hyp}}\), \(\cos=\dfrac{\text{adj}}{\text{hyp}}\), \(\tan=\dfrac{\text{opp}}{\text{adj}}\)
    • Hypotenuse faces the right angle — it is never “adjacent”
    • The ratio depends on the angle, not on the size of the triangle

    Reciprocals and quotients

    • \(\operatorname{cosec}\theta=\dfrac{1}{\sin\theta}\), \(\sec\theta=\dfrac{1}{\cos\theta}\), \(\cot\theta=\dfrac{1}{\tan\theta}\)
    • \(\tan\theta=\dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}\), \(\cot\theta=\dfrac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}\)
    • Pair them up: \(\sin\)–\(\operatorname{cosec}\), \(\cos\)–\(\sec\), \(\tan\)–\(\cot\)

    One ratio → all the rest

    • Write the ratio as \(\dfrac{\text{side}}{\text{side}}\) and put in a \(k\)
    • Third side by the Pythagoras theorem
    • Or use an identity: \(\sec^2=1+\tan^2\), \(\operatorname{cosec}^2=1+\cot^2\)

    Standard angles

    • \(0^\circ,30^\circ,45^\circ,60^\circ,90^\circ\) — learn the table
    • \(\sin\) rises \(0\to1\); \(\cos\) falls \(1\to0\)
    • \(\tan 90^\circ\), \(\sec 90^\circ\), \(\cot 0^\circ\), \(\operatorname{cosec}0^\circ\) are not defined

    The three identities

    • \(\sin^2A+\cos^2A=1\) — all \(A\)
    • \(\sec^2A-\tan^2A=1\) — for \(0^\circ\le A\lt 90^\circ\)
    • \(\operatorname{cosec}^2A-\cot^2A=1\) — for \(0^\circ\lt A\le 90^\circ\)

    Proving an identity

    • Start from the messier side; never move terms across the “=”
    • Convert everything to \(\sin\) and \(\cos\) when stuck
    • Multiply by the conjugate when you see \(1\pm\sin\) or \(1\pm\cos\)
    • End with “= RHS” and write “Hence proved”

    Formulas and facts to remember

    • The three basic ratios\(\sin A=\dfrac{\text{side opposite to }A}{\text{hypotenuse}}\), \(\cos A=\dfrac{\text{side adjacent to }A}{\text{hypotenuse}}\), \(\tan A=\dfrac{\text{side opposite to }A}{\text{side adjacent to }A}\)
    • The three reciprocals\(\operatorname{cosec}A=\dfrac{1}{\sin A}\), \(\ \sec A=\dfrac{1}{\cos A}\), \(\ \cot A=\dfrac{1}{\tan A}\)
    • Quotient forms\(\tan A=\dfrac{\sin A}{\cos A}\), \(\ \cot A=\dfrac{\cos A}{\sin A}\), \(\ \tan A\cot A=1\)
    • Identity 1\(\sin^2A+\cos^2A=1\), so \(\sin^2A=1-\cos^2A\) and \(\cos^2A=1-\sin^2A\)
    • Identity 2\(1+\tan^2A=\sec^2A\), so \(\sec^2A-\tan^2A=1\)  \((0^\circ\le A\lt 90^\circ)\)
    • Identity 3\(1+\cot^2A=\operatorname{cosec}^2A\), so \(\operatorname{cosec}^2A-\cot^2A=1\)  \((0^\circ\lt A\le 90^\circ)\)
    • Two products worth memorising\((\sec A+\tan A)(\sec A-\tan A)=1\) and \((\operatorname{cosec}A+\cot A)(\operatorname{cosec}A-\cot A)=1\)
    • Size limits\(0\le\sin A\le 1\) and \(0\le\cos A\le 1\); \(\sec A\ge 1\) and \(\operatorname{cosec}A\ge 1\); \(\tan A\) can be any non-negative number
    • Not defined\(\tan 90^\circ\), \(\sec 90^\circ\), \(\cot 0^\circ\), \(\operatorname{cosec}0^\circ\)

    The table you must know by heart

    \(\cos A\)1\(\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\)\(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\)\(\dfrac12\)0
    \(\tan A\)0\(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt3}\)1\(\sqrt3\)not defined

    How to rebuild it in ten seconds. Write \(0,1,2,3,4\) under the five angles, divide each by 4, take the square root — that is the \(\sin\) row. Reverse it for \(\cos\). Divide \(\sin\) by \(\cos\) for \(\tan\). The last three rows are just the reciprocals.

    Mistakes that cost marks

    • Writing \(\sec^2A+\tan^2A=1\).The sign is minus: \(\sec^2A-\tan^2A=1\). Same for \(\operatorname{cosec}^2A-\cot^2A=1\). Only \(\sin^2A+\cos^2A\) has a plus.
    • Treating \(\sin A\) as \(\sin\times A\) and cancelling the \(A\).\(\sin\) alone means nothing. \(\dfrac{\sin A}{A}\) is not \(\sin\), and \(\sin 2A\ne 2\sin A\).
    • Taking the hypotenuse as a side of the angle.The hypotenuse is always opposite the right angle. The “adjacent” side is the other arm of your angle.
    • Crossing terms over the “=” sign while proving an identity.Work on one side only until it becomes the other side, or simplify both sides separately to the same expression.
    • Answering \(2\theta\) when \(\theta\) is asked (or \(A+B\) when \(A\) is asked).After solving, read the question again and write the quantity actually asked for.
    • Saying \(\sin\theta=\dfrac54\) or \(\cos\theta=1.2\) is possible.Both \(\sin\) and \(\cos\) lie between 0 and 1 for an acute angle, because the hypotenuse is the longest side.
    • Writing \(\tan 90^\circ=\infty\) or \(\cot 0^\circ=\infty\).Write not defined. That is the phrase the marking scheme wants.
    • Leaving out “\(\theta\) is acute” when taking a square root.\(\cos\theta=\pm\sqrt{1-\sin^2\theta}\); you keep the positive sign because the angle is acute — say so.

    Which method when

    One ratio given, others asked
    Draw the right triangle, put \(k\) on the two known sides, find the third by Pythagoras. Then read all six ratios off the figure.
    Expression in \(\sin\) and \(\cos\), one ratio given
    Divide the numerator and the denominator by \(\cos\theta\) so that everything becomes \(\tan\theta\). You never need the angle.
    Only standard angles
    Substitute from the table first, then simplify. Remember \(\sin^260^\circ\) means \((\sin 60^\circ)^2=\dfrac34\) — put the value in, then square it.
    \(\sin(A+B)\) or \(\cos(A-B)\) given
    Match the value to the table, get two linear equations in \(A\) and \(B\), and solve by adding and subtracting.
    Prove an identity, mixed ratios
    Turn everything into \(\sin\) and \(\cos\), take the LCM, then use \(\sin^2+\cos^2=1\).
    You see \(1+\sin\), \(1-\cos\), or a square root of such a fraction
    Multiply the numerator and the denominator by the conjugate; \(1-\sin^2\) becomes \(\cos^2\).
    \(\sec\theta+\tan\theta\) or \(\operatorname{cosec}\theta-\cot\theta\) given
    Use \(\sec^2-\tan^2=1\) to get the other factor at once, then add and subtract the two equations.
    Eliminate \(\theta\) from two equations
    Make \(\sin\theta\) and \(\cos\theta\) (or \(\sec\theta\) and \(\tan\theta\)) the subjects, square, and add or subtract so that an identity removes \(\theta\).

    Last-minute checklist

    What the board usually asks

    Unit V (Trigonometry) carries 12 marks in all, and this chapter normally takes the larger share of it. A typical paper has:

    • Two or three 1-mark questions: a value at a standard angle, or an expression that collapses by an identity — see Q5, Q8, Q16, Q20.
    • Often one assertion–reason item on an identity or on a “not defined” value — see Q22, Q25.
    • A 2-mark question: one ratio given and the others asked (Q26, Q27), an evaluation with standard angles (Q28), or finding \(A\) and \(B\) (Q30).
    • A 3-mark identity to prove — the conjugate type and the “convert to \(\sin\) and \(\cos\)” type are the favourites: Q41, Q43, Q44.
    • A 5-mark question: a long identity (Q48, Q52), a “\(\sec\theta+\tan\theta=p\)” proof (Q50), or the derivation of the standard values (Q47).
    • A case study built on one right triangle — a ladder, a ramp, a kite or a roof: Q54–Q57.

    Almost every mark in this chapter comes from two things: the table of standard values, and the three identities. Get those perfect and the rest is algebra.
