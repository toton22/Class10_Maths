Chapter 12 Surface Areas and Volumes

A capsule, a tent, a bird-bath, a gulab jamun — none of them is one shape. Each is two or three of the solids you already know, stuck together or scooped out, and this chapter teaches you to measure them.

    12.1 Where this chapter fits

    In Class IX you measured single solids — a cuboid, a cylinder, a cone, a sphere. Look around and you will notice that almost nothing real is a single solid. A medicine capsule is a cylinder with a hemisphere at each end. A circus tent is a cylinder wearing a cone. A bird-bath is a cylinder with a bowl scooped out of it.

    This chapter adds nothing new to the list of formulas. It teaches one new habit: break the object into the basic solids you already know, then decide carefully what to add and what to leave out.

    The formulas you must already know

    These come from Class IX. Nothing in Chapter 12 works until you can write them without thinking, so read this table until you can reproduce it on a blank page.

    SolidCurved / lateral surface areaTotal surface areaVolume
    Cuboid \(l,b,h\)\(2(l+b)h\)\(2(lb+bh+hl)\)\(lbh\)
    Cube \(a\)\(4a^2\)\(6a^2\)\(a^3\)
    Cylinder \(r,h\)\(2\pi rh\)\(2\pi r(r+h)\)\(\pi r^2h\)
    Cone \(r,h,l\)\(\pi rl\)\(\pi r(l+r)\)\(\tfrac13\pi r^2h\)
    Sphere \(r\)\(4\pi r^2\)\(4\pi r^2\)\(\tfrac43\pi r^3\)
    Hemisphere \(r\)\(2\pi r^2\)\(3\pi r^2\)\(\tfrac23\pi r^3\)

    A hemisphere has two different “areas” and students mix them up. Its curved surface is half the sphere, \(2\pi r^2\). Its flat face is a circle, \(\pi r^2\). Only when you want both do you write \(3\pi r^2\). In this chapter the flat face is almost always glued to something, so the piece you usually need is \(2\pi r^2\).

    Slant height of a cone
    $$l=\sqrt{r^2+h^2}$$

    \(r\) = base radius, \(h\) = vertical height, \(l\) = slant height. The radius, the height and the slant height form a right-angled triangle, so this is just Pythagoras. Every question that asks for the curved surface of a cone needs \(l\) first.

    Take \(\pi=\dfrac{22}{7}\) unless the question says \(\pi=3.14\). The NCERT exercises in this chapter say so at the top. Working with \(\dfrac{22}{7}\) is easier when a radius is a multiple of 7 or of 3.5 — and the book chooses such radii on purpose.

    Units: \(1\text{ m}=100\text{ cm}\), so \(1\text{ m}^2=10\,000\text{ cm}^2\) and \(1\text{ m}^3=10^6\text{ cm}^3\). Also \(1\text{ litre}=1000\text{ cm}^3\).

    • Surface area: only what you can touch

      The faces where two solids are glued go inside. They are gone. Never count them.

    • Volume: nothing is lost

      The space inside is still there after joining, so volumes simply add. Scoop something out and you subtract.

    • Same radius, always

      Two solids can be joined smoothly only if their circular faces match, so the radius is usually shared. Use that to pull hidden measurements out of a question.

    A cone has base radius 6 cm and height 8 cm. What is its slant height, and what is its curved surface area (take \(\pi=3.14\))?

    Show answer

    \(l=\sqrt{6^2+8^2}=\sqrt{100}=10\) cm, so CSA \(=\pi rl=3.14\times6\times10=188.4\text{ cm}^2\). Note how the answer would be wrong if you used 8 instead of 10.

    12.2 Surface area of a combination of solids

    Take the oil tanker you see on the back of a truck. It is a cylinder with a hemisphere closing each end. To paint it you need the area of its outside — so the question becomes: after the three pieces are joined, which surfaces are still on the outside?

    So for the tanker (and for a medicine capsule, which is the same shape):

    $$\text{TSA of the new solid}=\underbrace{2\pi r^2}_{\text{hemisphere}}+\underbrace{2\pi rh}_{\text{cylinder}}+\underbrace{2\pi r^2}_{\text{hemisphere}}$$

    Every term is a curved surface area. No flat circle appears anywhere.

    The rule

    The total surface area of a combination of solids is the sum of the areas of all the surfaces that are still exposed after the solids are joined. A face that is glued to another face is inside the solid and is never counted.

    This is the mistake that costs the most marks in this chapter:

    $$\text{TSA of the combination}\;\ne\;\text{TSA of piece 1}+\text{TSA of piece 2}$$

    Adding the two total surface areas counts the joined faces twice — once from each piece — when the honest count is zero times.

    A method that always works

    1. Name the pieces. Write “cone + hemisphere”, “cylinder − cone”, and so on. Draw them.
    2. Write the shared measurements. The joined circles have the same radius. Use the total height to get a missing height: height of cone = total height − radius of hemisphere, and so on.
    3. List every surface of every piece, curved and flat.
    4. Cross out the glued faces. If the two circles are of different sizes, a ring of the bigger one is left over — keep that ring.
    5. Add what is left, substitute, and write the answer with the unit \(\text{cm}^2\) or \(\text{m}^2\).

    The word surmounted means “standing on top of”. “A cone surmounted on a hemisphere” = the hemisphere is at the bottom, the cone sits on it. “A cylinder surmounted by a cone” = the cone is on top. Read it slowly — it tells you which solid carries which, and that decides the picture you draw.

    NCERT Example 1cone on hemisphere

    Rasheed is given a plain wooden spinning top (lattu) for his birthday and wants to colour it with crayons. The top is a cone standing on a hemisphere. Its whole height is 5 cm and the diameter at the widest part is 3.5 cm. Find the area he has to colour. \(\left(\text{Take }\pi=\dfrac{22}{7}\right)\)

    Solution

    Given: total height 5 cm, diameter 3.5 cm. To find: the area to be coloured, that is the total surface area of the top.

    The colour goes on every surface you can see, so

    $$\text{TSA of the top}=\text{CSA of hemisphere}+\text{CSA of cone}$$

    Radius \(r=\dfrac{3.5}{2}=1.75\text{ cm}\) — the same for both pieces.

    Hemisphere:

    $$\begin{aligned}\text{CSA}&=\tfrac12(4\pi r^2)=2\pi r^2\\&=2\times\frac{22}{7}\times\frac{3.5}{2}\times\frac{3.5}{2}\\&=19.25\text{ cm}^2\end{aligned}$$

    Cone: the hemisphere takes up a height equal to its radius, so

    $$h=5-1.75=3.25\text{ cm}$$ $$\begin{aligned}l&=\sqrt{r^2+h^2}=\sqrt{(1.75)^2+(3.25)^2}\\&=\sqrt{13.625}\approx3.7\text{ cm}\end{aligned}$$ $$\text{CSA}=\pi rl=\frac{22}{7}\times1.75\times3.7=20.35\text{ cm}^2$$

    Adding the two, and taking \(\dfrac{22}{7}\times1.75=\dfrac{11}{2}\) out as a common factor,

    $$\begin{aligned}\text{TSA}&=\frac{22}{7}\times\frac{3.5}{2}\,(3.5+3.7)\\&=\frac{11}{2}\times7.2=39.6\text{ cm}^2\end{aligned}$$

    Area to be coloured \(\approx39.6\text{ cm}^2\)

    The slant height is \(\sqrt{13.625}=3.691\ldots\), rounded to 3.7 cm as the book does. Carrying the exact value would give 39.55 cm², so write “approximately”.

    “Total surface area of the top” is not (TSA of the cone) + (TSA of the hemisphere). That sum would include the base circle of the cone and the flat face of the hemisphere, and both are buried inside the toy.

    NCERT Example 2cube plus hemisphere

    A decorative block is made from two solids — a cube and a hemisphere. The base is a cube of edge 5 cm, and a hemisphere of diameter 4.2 cm is fixed on its top face. Find the total surface area of the block. \(\left(\text{Take }\pi=\dfrac{22}{7}\right)\)

    Solution

    Given: cube of edge \(a=5\) cm; hemisphere of radius \(r=\dfrac{4.2}{2}=2.1\) cm. To find: total surface area of the block.

    Here the hemisphere is smaller than the top face, so the top face is not completely covered. What happens on the top face is:

    • a circle of radius 2.1 cm is hidden under the hemisphere — subtract \(\pi r^2\);
    • the dome of the hemisphere is added — add \(2\pi r^2\).
    $$\begin{aligned}\text{TSA}&=6a^2-\pi r^2+2\pi r^2\\&=6a^2+\pi r^2\end{aligned}$$

    Surface area of the cube \(=6\times5\times5=150\text{ cm}^2\), and

    $$\pi r^2=\frac{22}{7}\times\frac{4.2}{2}\times\frac{4.2}{2}=13.86\text{ cm}^2$$ $$\text{TSA}=150+13.86=163.86\text{ cm}^2$$

    \(163.86\text{ cm}^2\)

    Notice the neat shortcut hiding here: because the flat face lost is \(\pi r^2\) and the dome gained is \(2\pi r^2\), putting a hemisphere on a flat face adds exactly \(\pi r^2\).

    Three one-line results worth memorising, for a solid of flat surface area \(S\) and a hemisphere of radius \(r\):

    • hemisphere stuck on a flat face: new area \(=S+\pi r^2\)  (lose \(\pi r^2\), gain \(2\pi r^2\));
    • hemisphere scooped out of a flat face: new area \(=S+\pi r^2\) — the same! (lose \(\pi r^2\), gain the bowl \(2\pi r^2\));
    • volume: stuck on \(\Rightarrow +\tfrac23\pi r^3\), scooped out \(\Rightarrow -\tfrac23\pi r^3\).

    So for area a bump and a dent do the same thing; for volume they do opposite things.

    NCERT Example 3the leftover ring

    A wooden toy rocket is a cone mounted on a cylinder. The whole rocket is 26 cm tall and the conical part is 6 cm of that. The base of the cone has diameter 5 cm while the base of the cylinder has diameter 3 cm. The conical part is to be painted orange and the cylindrical part yellow. Find the area painted in each colour. \(\left(\text{Take }\pi=3.14\right)\)

    Solution

    Given: cone — radius \(r=2.5\) cm, height \(h=6\) cm; cylinder — radius \(r'=1.5\) cm, height \(h'=26-6=20\) cm. To find: orange area and yellow area.

    $$l=\sqrt{r^2+h^2}=\sqrt{(2.5)^2+6^2}=\sqrt{42.25}=6.5\text{ cm}$$

    Orange (the cone). The cone rests on the cylinder, but its base is the bigger circle. Only a circle of radius 1.5 cm is hidden; the rest of the cone's base — a ring — faces downwards and is still visible, so it is painted too.

    $$\begin{aligned}\text{orange}&=\pi rl+\pi r^2-\pi (r')^2\\&=\pi\big[(2.5\times6.5)+(2.5)^2-(1.5)^2\big]\\&=\pi\,[16.25+6.25-2.25]\\&=3.14\times20.25=63.585\text{ cm}^2\end{aligned}$$

    Yellow (the cylinder). Its curved surface, plus its bottom circle (the top circle is covered by the cone).

    $$\begin{aligned}\text{yellow}&=2\pi r'h'+\pi (r')^2=\pi r'(2h'+r')\\&=3.14\times1.5\times(40+1.5)\\&=4.71\times41.5=195.465\text{ cm}^2\end{aligned}$$

    Orange \(=63.585\text{ cm}^2\), yellow \(=195.465\text{ cm}^2\)

    The ring idea is a favourite of examiners. Whenever the two circular faces that meet have different radii \(R\) and \(r\), the exposed ring has area \(\pi R^2-\pi r^2=\pi(R^2-r^2)\). Write that line explicitly — it usually carries a mark of its own.

    NCERT Example 4hollow, not a lump

    Mayank makes a bird-bath for his garden: a cylinder with a hemispherical hollow scooped out of its top. The cylinder is 1.45 m high and has radius 30 cm. Find the total surface area of the bird-bath. \(\left(\text{Take }\pi=\dfrac{22}{7}\right)\)

    Solution

    Given: \(h=1.45\text{ m}=145\text{ cm}\), \(r=30\text{ cm}\). To find: total surface area.

    The bowl is scooped out of the top, so the flat top circle is gone and the curved inside of the hemisphere takes its place. Both have the same radius 30 cm.

    $$\begin{aligned}\text{TSA}&=\text{CSA of cylinder}+\text{CSA of hemisphere}\\&=2\pi rh+2\pi r^2=2\pi r(h+r)\\&=2\times\frac{22}{7}\times30\times(145+30)\\&=2\times\frac{22}{7}\times30\times175\\&=33\,000\text{ cm}^2\end{aligned}$$

    Converting, \(1\text{ m}^2=10\,000\text{ cm}^2\), so \(33\,000\text{ cm}^2=3.3\text{ m}^2\).

    \(33\,000\text{ cm}^2=3.3\text{ m}^2\)

    Change 1.45 m into 145 cm before substituting. Mixing metres and centimetres in one formula is the commonest source of a wrong answer here.

    When a solid has a hemispherical dent of the same radius as the flat face it is cut from, the area does not change in a complicated way: the flat circle \(\pi r^2\) is replaced by the bowl \(2\pi r^2\). Say it in words in your answer — “the circular top is replaced by the curved surface of the hemisphere” — and the examiner can follow your thinking.

    A solid is a cylinder of radius \(r\) and height \(h\) with a hemisphere of radius \(r\) stuck on each flat end. Write its total surface area.

    Show answer

    Both flat circles of the cylinder are covered, and both flat faces of the hemispheres are hidden, so only curved surfaces survive: \(2\pi rh+2\pi r^2+2\pi r^2=2\pi r(h+2r)\). This is the capsule of Exercise 12.1 Q6.

    12.3 Volume of a combination of solids

    Surface area needed care, because joining two solids destroys some surfaces. Volume needs much less care, because joining two solids destroys nothing. The space inside piece one plus the space inside piece two is the space inside the new solid.

    Volume of a combination
    $$V=V_1+V_2\quad\text{(pieces joined)}\qquad V=V_1-V_2\quad\text{(one scooped out of the other)}$$

    Add when a solid is stuck on. Subtract when a cavity, depression or hollow is cut out. There is no third case.

    Surface area

    Joining destroys the two faces that touch.

    So you must think about which surfaces survive, and a leftover ring may appear.

    A bump and a dent of the same radius change the area by the same amount, \(+\pi r^2\).

    Volume

    Joining destroys nothing.

    So you just add the volumes of the pieces — no surfaces to worry about.

    A bump adds \(\tfrac23\pi r^3\); a dent of the same size takes the same amount away.

    Capacity means the volume of what a container can hold, measured from the inside. For a thin-walled vessel the inside and outside measurements are practically the same, and the book then says so (“assume the inner and outer dimensions are nearly the same”). If a question gives a thickness, capacity uses the inner radius.

    NCERT Example 5cuboid plus half cylinder

    Shanta runs an industry in a shed shaped like a cuboid with a half cylinder on top. The base of the shed is 7 m × 15 m and the cuboidal part is 8 m high. Find the volume of air the shed can hold. If the machinery inside takes up 300 m³ and there are 20 workers, each taking up about 0.08 m³, how much air is left in the shed? \(\left(\text{Take }\pi=\dfrac{22}{7}\right)\)

    Solution

    Given: cuboid \(15\text{ m}\times7\text{ m}\times8\text{ m}\); half cylinder of diameter 7 m and length 15 m. To find: volume of air, empty and then with machinery and workers.

    The roof is half a cylinder lying on its side. Its circular face has diameter 7 m, so \(r=\dfrac72=3.5\text{ m}\), and its length (the height of the cylinder) is 15 m.

    $$\begin{aligned}V&=\text{cuboid}+\tfrac12\times\text{cylinder}\\&=15\times7\times8+\frac12\times\frac{22}{7}\times\frac72\times\frac72\times15\end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned}&=840+288.75\\&=1128.75\text{ m}^3\end{aligned}$$

    Space taken by the machinery \(=300\text{ m}^3\).

    Space taken by the workers \(=20\times0.08=1.6\text{ m}^3\).

    $$\begin{aligned}\text{air left}&=1128.75-(300+1.6)\\&=1128.75-301.6\\&=827.15\text{ m}^3\end{aligned}$$

    Volume of the shed \(=1128.75\text{ m}^3\); air left \(=827.15\text{ m}^3\)

    The 15 m is the length of the half cylinder, not a radius or a height of the cuboid's face. Match each number to the right letter before substituting.

    NCERT Example 6apparent vs actual

    A juice seller serves his customers in glasses like the one shown. The inner diameter of the cylindrical glass is 5 cm, but the bottom of the glass bulges upward as a hemisphere, which cuts down how much juice it holds. If the glass is 10 cm high, find its apparent capacity and its actual capacity. \(\left(\text{Use }\pi=3.14\right)\)

    Solution

    Given: inner diameter 5 cm so \(r=2.5\) cm, height \(h=10\) cm. To find: apparent capacity and actual capacity.

    Apparent capacity — what the glass looks like it holds, that is the whole cylinder:

    $$\begin{aligned}V_{\text{cyl}}&=\pi r^2h\\&=3.14\times2.5\times2.5\times10\\&=196.25\text{ cm}^3\end{aligned}$$

    The raised hemisphere at the bottom takes up part of that space, so the juice is less by the volume of the hemisphere:

    $$\begin{aligned}V_{\text{hemi}}&=\tfrac23\pi r^3\\&=\tfrac23\times3.14\times2.5\times2.5\times2.5\\&=32.71\text{ cm}^3\end{aligned}$$

    Actual capacity:

    $$\begin{aligned}V&=196.25-32.71\\&=163.54\text{ cm}^3\end{aligned}$$

    Apparent capacity \(=196.25\text{ cm}^3\); actual capacity \(=163.54\text{ cm}^3\)

    \(\tfrac23\times3.14\times15.625=32.708\overline{3}\), written as 32.71 cm³. The customer is cheated of nearly 33 cm³ — about one sixth of the glass.

    NCERT Example 7toy inside a cylinder

    A solid toy is a hemisphere with a right circular cone standing on it. The cone is 2 cm high and the base has diameter 4 cm. Find the volume of the toy. If a right circular cylinder just encloses the toy, find by how much the volume of the cylinder exceeds the volume of the toy. \(\left(\text{Take }\pi=3.14\right)\)

    Solution

    Given: cone of height \(h=2\) cm on a hemisphere; base diameter 4 cm. To find: volume of the toy, and the excess volume of the circumscribing cylinder.

    The cone stands on the flat face of the hemisphere, so both have the same radius

    $$r=\tfrac12\times4=2\text{ cm}$$ $$\begin{aligned}V_{\text{toy}}&=\tfrac23\pi r^3+\tfrac13\pi r^2h\\&=\tfrac23\times3.14\times2^3+\tfrac13\times3.14\times2^2\times2\\&=16.7466\ldots+8.3733\ldots\\&=25.12\text{ cm}^3\end{aligned}$$

    The circumscribing cylinder. “Just encloses” means it touches the toy all round, so

    • its radius \(=HP=BO=2\) cm (the widest part of the toy);
    • its height \(=EH=AO+OP=2+2=4\) cm (apex of the cone down to the bottom of the hemisphere, and \(OP\) is a radius).
    $$\begin{aligned}V_{\text{cyl}}&=\pi r^2H=3.14\times2^2\times4\\&=50.24\text{ cm}^3\end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned}\text{difference}&=50.24-25.12\\&=25.12\text{ cm}^3\end{aligned}$$

    Volume of the toy \(=25.12\text{ cm}^3\); the cylinder holds \(25.12\text{ cm}^3\) more

    A pleasant accident: the difference happens to equal the volume of the toy, so the toy fills exactly half of the cylinder. Do not treat that as a rule — it works only for these numbers.

    How the marks usually fall in a 3-mark volume question: 1 mark for identifying the pieces and their radii/heights, 1 mark for writing and substituting into the correct volume formulas, 1 mark for the arithmetic and the unit. Always write the formula line first — you earn that mark even if the arithmetic slips.

    Units are cubed, not squared: \(\text{cm}^3\), \(\text{m}^3\). And when a question mixes units — “height 1.45 m, radius 30 cm” — change everything to the smaller unit first. \(1\text{ m}^3=1\,000\,000\text{ cm}^3\), so a mistake here is huge.

    A cone of radius \(r\) and height \(h\) is hollowed out of a cylinder of the same radius and the same height. What fraction of the cylinder is left?

    Show answer

    \(V=\pi r^2h-\tfrac13\pi r^2h=\tfrac23\pi r^2h\). Two-thirds of the cylinder is left. (This is why the cone is called “one-third of its cylinder”.)

    12.4 Summary

    The whole chapter is two sentences long. Everything else is the six Class IX formulas and careful reading.

    1. Break it upName the basic solids the object is made of — cuboid, cube, cylinder, cone, sphere, hemisphere — and mark the radius and height of each on your figure.
    2. For surface area, keep only what is exposedAdd the curved surfaces you can touch. Drop every face that is glued. Keep a ring if the two joined circles have different radii.
    3. For volume, just add (or subtract)Stuck on \(\Rightarrow\) add its volume. Scooped out \(\Rightarrow\) subtract its volume.
    4. Finish properlyOne unit throughout, \(\pi=\dfrac{22}{7}\) unless told otherwise, and the answer with \(\text{cm}^2\), \(\text{m}^2\), \(\text{cm}^3\) or \(\text{m}^3\).
    The object says…It meansDo this

    Two topics that used to be in this chapter — converting a solid from one shape to another and the frustum of a cone — are no longer in the syllabus and will not be asked. What remains is exactly this: surface areas and volumes of combinations.

    A solid cylinder of radius \(r\) and height \(h\) has a hemisphere of radius \(r\) scooped out of one end and another hemisphere of radius \(r\) stuck on to the other end. Compared with the plain cylinder, what happens to the volume and what happens to the total surface area?

    Show answer

    Volume: \(-\tfrac23\pi r^3+\tfrac23\pi r^3=0\) — unchanged, still \(\pi r^2h\).

    Surface area: each end swaps a flat \(\pi r^2\) for a curved \(2\pi r^2\), so the area rises by \(\pi r^2\) at each end: \(2\pi rh+2\pi r^2+2\pi r^2=2\pi r(h+2r)\), which is \(2\pi r^2\) more than the plain cylinder's \(2\pi r(h+r)\).

    Exercise 12.1

    9 questions · surface area of a combination of solids · take \(\pi=\dfrac{22}{7}\) unless the question says otherwise

    Q1

    Two cubes, each of volume 64 cm³, are joined end to end. Find the surface area of the cuboid that is formed.

    Solution

    Given: two equal cubes, each of volume 64 cm³, joined face to face. To find: the surface area of the cuboid formed.

    Step 1 — the edge of each cube. For a cube of edge \(a\), volume \(=a^3\), so

    $$a^3=64\ \Rightarrow\ a=\sqrt[3]{64}=4\text{ cm}$$

    Step 2 — the cuboid. Joining the two cubes end to end doubles one edge only:

    $$l=4+4=8\text{ cm},\qquad b=4\text{ cm},\qquad h=4\text{ cm}$$

    Step 3 — its surface area.

    $$\begin{aligned}\text{SA}&=2(lb+bh+hl)\\&=2(8\times4+4\times4+4\times8)\\&=2(32+16+32)\\&=2\times80=160\text{ cm}^2\end{aligned}$$

    160 cm²

    Check it the other way: the two cubes have \(12\) faces of area \(16\text{ cm}^2\) each, that is \(192\text{ cm}^2\). Two of those faces are glued together and vanish, so \(192-2\times16=160\text{ cm}^2\). The answer is not \(2\times96=192\).

    Q2

    A vessel is made of a hollow hemisphere with a hollow cylinder standing on it. The hemisphere has diameter 14 cm and the vessel is 13 cm high in all. Find the inner surface area of the vessel.

    Solution

    Given: diameter of hemisphere 14 cm, total height of vessel 13 cm. To find: inner surface area.

    Both pieces are hollow and they share the same radius:

    $$r=\frac{14}{2}=7\text{ cm}$$

    The hemisphere takes up a height equal to its radius, so the cylinder's height is

    $$h=13-7=6\text{ cm}$$

    Inside the vessel you can touch only the curved wall of the cylinder and the curved inside of the bowl — there is no floor between them, and no lid.

    $$\begin{aligned}\text{inner SA}&=2\pi rh+2\pi r^2=2\pi r(h+r)\\&=2\times\frac{22}{7}\times7\times(6+7)\\&=44\times13\\&=572\text{ cm}^2\end{aligned}$$

    572 cm²

    A common slip is to take 13 cm as the height of the cylinder. The 13 cm is the height of the whole vessel, bowl included.

    Q3

    A toy is a cone of radius 3.5 cm standing on a hemisphere of the same radius. The toy is 15.5 cm tall in all. Find its total surface area.

    Solution

    Given: \(r=3.5\) cm for both pieces, total height 15.5 cm. To find: total surface area of the toy.

    Step 1 — height of the cone. The hemisphere contributes a height equal to its radius:

    $$h=15.5-3.5=12\text{ cm}$$

    Step 2 — slant height of the cone.

    $$\begin{aligned}l&=\sqrt{r^2+h^2}=\sqrt{(3.5)^2+12^2}\\&=\sqrt{12.25+144}=\sqrt{156.25}\\&=12.5\text{ cm}\end{aligned}$$

    Step 3 — add the two visible surfaces. The circle where the cone meets the hemisphere is hidden, so

    $$\begin{aligned}\text{TSA}&=\pi rl+2\pi r^2=\pi r(l+2r)\\&=\frac{22}{7}\times3.5\times(12.5+7)\\&=11\times19.5\\&=214.5\text{ cm}^2\end{aligned}$$

    214.5 cm²

    Q4

    A cubical block of side 7 cm has a hemisphere fixed on top of it. What is the greatest diameter the hemisphere can have? Find the surface area of the solid so formed.

    Solution

    Given: cube of edge \(a=7\) cm with a hemisphere on one face. To find: the greatest possible diameter, and the surface area of the solid.

    Greatest diameter. The flat face of the hemisphere is a circle drawn on a square face of side 7 cm. The largest circle that fits inside that square is the one that touches all four sides, so its diameter equals the edge of the cube:

    $$d=7\text{ cm},\qquad r=\frac72=3.5\text{ cm}$$

    Surface area. The cube loses the circle under the hemisphere and gains the dome:

    $$\begin{aligned}\text{SA}&=6a^2-\pi r^2+2\pi r^2\\&=6a^2+\pi r^2\\&=6\times7\times7+\frac{22}{7}\times3.5\times3.5\\&=294+38.5\\&=332.5\text{ cm}^2\end{aligned}$$

    Greatest diameter = 7 cm; surface area = 332.5 cm²

    A hemisphere of diameter more than 7 cm would hang over the edges of the face, so it could not be “fixed on” it.

    Q5

    A hemispherical depression is cut out of one face of a cubical wooden block, in such a way that the diameter \(l\) of the hemisphere is equal to the edge of the cube. Find the surface area of the remaining solid.

    Solution

    Given: cube of edge \(l\); hemispherical depression of diameter \(l\) cut from one face. To find: the surface area of what is left.

    Radius of the hemisphere:

    $$r=\frac{l}{2}$$

    Cutting the bowl does two things to the surface. The circle of radius \(r\) on that face is removed, and the curved inside of the bowl appears in its place. No other face changes.

    $$\begin{aligned}\text{SA}&=6l^2-\pi r^2+2\pi r^2\\&=6l^2+\pi r^2\\&=6l^2+\pi\left(\frac{l}{2}\right)^{\!2}\\&=6l^2+\frac{\pi l^2}{4}\end{aligned}$$

    Taking \(\dfrac{l^2}{4}\) common,

    $$\text{SA}=\frac{l^2}{4}\left(24+\pi\right)$$

    \(\dfrac{l^2}{4}\left(\pi+24\right)\) square units

    Leave the answer in terms of \(l\) and \(\pi\) — no number is given. If you prefer, \(\dfrac{l^2}{4}(24+\pi)\approx6.7854\,l^2\).

    Q6

    A medicine capsule is shaped like a cylinder with a hemisphere stuck on each of its two ends. The whole capsule is 14 mm long and 5 mm in diameter. Find its surface area.

    Solution

    Given: total length 14 mm, diameter 5 mm. To find: surface area of the capsule.

    Radius of the cylinder and of both hemispheres:

    $$r=\frac{5}{2}=2.5\text{ mm}$$

    Each hemisphere adds a length equal to its radius, and there are two of them, so the cylinder alone is

    $$h=14-2(2.5)=14-5=9\text{ mm}$$

    Every flat circle is buried inside, so only curved surfaces are left:

    $$\begin{aligned}\text{SA}&=2\pi rh+2\times2\pi r^2\\&=2\pi r(h+2r)\\&=2\times\frac{22}{7}\times2.5\times(9+5)\\&=2\times\frac{22}{7}\times2.5\times14\\&=220\text{ mm}^2\end{aligned}$$

    220 mm²

    Q7

    A tent is a cylinder with a cone on top. The cylindrical part is 2.1 m high and 4 m across; the conical top has slant height 2.8 m. Find the area of the canvas used for the tent, and the cost of that canvas at ₹ 500 per m². (The floor of the tent is not covered with canvas.)

    Solution

    Given: \(r=\dfrac42=2\) m, height of cylinder \(h=2.1\) m, slant height of cone \(l=2.8\) m. To find: area of canvas and its cost.

    Canvas covers the curved wall of the cylinder and the curved roof of the cone. The circle where they meet is inside the tent, and the floor is not covered, so neither is counted.

    $$\begin{aligned}\text{canvas}&=2\pi rh+\pi rl=\pi r(2h+l)\\&=\frac{22}{7}\times2\times(2\times2.1+2.8)\\&=\frac{22}{7}\times2\times(4.2+2.8)\\&=\frac{22}{7}\times2\times7\\&=44\text{ m}^2\end{aligned}$$

    Cost at ₹ 500 per m²:

    $$\text{cost}=44\times500=₹\,22\,000$$

    Canvas = 44 m²; cost = ₹ 22 000

    The 2.8 m is the slant height, so use it directly in \(\pi rl\). Do not run it through \(\sqrt{r^2+h^2}\) again.

    Q8

    A solid cylinder is 2.4 cm high and 1.4 cm in diameter. A conical cavity of the same height and the same diameter is hollowed out of it. Find the total surface area of the solid that remains, correct to the nearest cm².

    Solution

    Given: cylinder with \(r=\dfrac{1.4}{2}=0.7\) cm and \(h=2.4\) cm; a cone of the same \(r\) and \(h\) is removed from the top. To find: total surface area of the remaining solid, to the nearest cm².

    Step 1 — slant height of the cone.

    $$\begin{aligned}l&=\sqrt{r^2+h^2}=\sqrt{(0.7)^2+(2.4)^2}\\&=\sqrt{0.49+5.76}=\sqrt{6.25}\\&=2.5\text{ cm}\end{aligned}$$

    Step 2 — which surfaces are there? Three of them:

    1. the curved surface of the cylinder, \(2\pi rh\);
    2. the flat circular base at the bottom, \(\pi r^2\) — it is untouched;
    3. the curved surface of the cone, \(\pi rl\), which is now the inner wall of the hollow.

    The top circle of the cylinder is completely gone, because the cavity has the same diameter as the cylinder.

    $$\begin{aligned}\text{TSA}&=2\pi rh+\pi r^2+\pi rl\\&=\pi r(2h+r+l)\\&=\frac{22}{7}\times0.7\times(4.8+0.7+2.5)\\&=2.2\times8.0\\&=17.6\text{ cm}^2\end{aligned}$$

    Rounding to the nearest cm²:

    ≈ 18 cm²

    Term by term: \(2\pi rh=10.56\), \(\pi r^2=1.54\), \(\pi rl=5.5\); their sum is \(17.6\text{ cm}^2\). Do not also add the top circle — it no longer exists.

    Q9

    A wooden article is made by scooping a hemisphere out of each end of a solid cylinder. The cylinder is 10 cm high and its base has radius 3.5 cm. Find the total surface area of the article.

    Solution

    Given: cylinder with \(r=3.5\) cm and \(h=10\) cm; a hemisphere of the same radius scooped out of each end. To find: total surface area of the article.

    Each end loses its flat circle and gains the curved inside of a bowl. So the surfaces are: the curved wall of the cylinder, plus two hemispherical bowls.

    $$\begin{aligned}\text{TSA}&=2\pi rh+2\times2\pi r^2\\&=2\pi rh+4\pi r^2\end{aligned}$$

    Working out each piece with \(\pi=\dfrac{22}{7}\):

    $$2\pi rh=2\times\frac{22}{7}\times3.5\times10=220\text{ cm}^2$$ $$4\pi r^2=4\times\frac{22}{7}\times3.5\times3.5=154\text{ cm}^2$$ $$\text{TSA}=220+154=374\text{ cm}^2$$

    374 cm²

    Both radii are 3.5 cm, so the bowls exactly replace the two flat ends. If the hemispheres had been stuck on instead of scooped out, the surface area would have been the same 374 cm² — but the volume would have gone up instead of down.

    Unless a question says otherwise, take \(\pi=\dfrac{22}{7}\). Where a question prints \(\pi=3.14\), use exactly that value — the official answers are worked out with it.

    The one idea behind this whole exercise. When two solids are joined, nothing is lost from the volume — the volume of the combined solid is simply the sum of the volumes of its parts. When a piece is scooped out, or a solid is dropped into a liquid, you subtract. Compare this with surface area (Exercise 12.1), where the two joined faces do disappear.

    Q1

    A solid is made of a cone standing on a hemisphere. Both have the same radius, 1 cm, and the height of the cone is equal to its radius. Find the volume of the solid in terms of \(\pi\).

    Solution

    Given: radius of the cone = radius of the hemisphere = \(r=1\) cm; height of the cone \(h=r=1\) cm. To find: the volume of the solid, in terms of \(\pi\).

    The two pieces are joined, so their volumes add.

    $$\begin{aligned} V&=V_{\text{cone}}+V_{\text{hemisphere}}\\ &=\frac13\pi r^2h+\frac23\pi r^3 \end{aligned}$$

    Putting \(r=1\) cm and \(h=1\) cm:

    $$\begin{aligned} V&=\frac13\pi(1)^2(1)+\frac23\pi(1)^3\\ &=\frac{\pi}{3}+\frac{2\pi}{3}\\ &=\frac{3\pi}{3}=\pi\ \text{cm}^3 \end{aligned}$$

    \(\pi\) cm³

    “In terms of \(\pi\)” means leave the symbol \(\pi\) standing — do not put \(\tfrac{22}{7}\) in its place. Notice also that because \(h=r\) here, \(\tfrac13\pi r^2h+\tfrac23\pi r^3=\tfrac13\pi r^3+\tfrac23\pi r^3=\pi r^3\); with \(r=1\) that is \(\pi\). A neat way to check your answer.

    Q2

    Rachel, an engineering student, was asked to make a model out of thin aluminium sheet in the shape of a cylinder with a cone fixed at each of its two ends. The model is 3 cm in diameter and 12 cm long, and each cone is 2 cm high. Find the volume of air contained in the model. (Take the outer and inner measurements of the model to be nearly the same.)

    Solution

    Given: diameter of the model = 3 cm, total length = 12 cm, height of each cone \(h_1=2\) cm. To find: the volume of air inside the model.

    The sheet is thin, so the air fills the whole model. Volume of air = volume of the model = volume of the cylinder + volumes of the two cones.

    Step 1 — radius, and the height of the cylinder.

    $$r=\frac{3}{2}\ \text{cm}=1.5\ \text{cm}$$ $$\begin{aligned} h_2&=\text{total length}-\text{the two cone heights}\\ &=12-(2+2)=8\ \text{cm} \end{aligned}$$

    Step 2 — add the three volumes.

    $$\begin{aligned} V&=\pi r^2h_2+2\times\frac13\pi r^2h_1\\ &=\pi r^2\left(h_2+\frac{2h_1}{3}\right) \end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned} V&=\frac{22}{7}\times\frac32\times\frac32\times\left(8+\frac43\right)\\ &=\frac{22}{7}\times\frac94\times\frac{28}{3}\\ &=\frac{22\times9\times28}{7\times4\times3}\\ &=\frac{5544}{84}=66\ \text{cm}^3 \end{aligned}$$

    66 cm³ of air

    The most common slip here is to use 12 cm as the height of the cylinder. The 12 cm is the length of the whole model, so the two cone heights must be taken off first. A useful check: the two cones together are worth \(\tfrac23\times2=\tfrac43\) cm of extra cylinder height, so the answer is \(\pi r^2\times\left(8+\tfrac43\right)\).

    Q3

    A gulab jamun holds sugar syrup up to about 30% of its volume. Each one is shaped like a cylinder with a hemisphere at either end, 5 cm long and 2.8 cm in diameter. Find, approximately, how much syrup there would be in 45 such gulab jamuns.

    Solution

    Given: total length 5 cm, diameter 2.8 cm, so \(r=1.4\) cm; syrup = 30% of the volume; 45 pieces. To find: the volume of syrup in 45 gulab jamuns.

    Step 1 — length of the cylindrical part. Each hemispherical end sticks out by one radius, so the two ends use up \(2r=2.8\) cm of the length.

    $$h=5-2.8=2.2\ \text{cm}$$

    Step 2 — volume of one gulab jamun. The two hemispheres have the same radius, so together they make one whole sphere.

    $$\begin{aligned} V&=\pi r^2h+2\times\frac23\pi r^3\\ &=\pi r^2h+\frac43\pi r^3 \end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned} \pi r^2h&=\frac{22}{7}\times1.4\times1.4\times2.2\\ &=13.552\ \text{cm}^3\\[2pt] \frac43\pi r^3&=\frac43\times\frac{22}{7}\times(1.4)^3\\ &=\frac43\times\frac{22}{7}\times2.744\\ &=\frac{34.496}{3}\ \text{cm}^3 \end{aligned}$$

    Step 3 — volume of 45 of them.

    $$\begin{aligned} 45V&=45\times13.552+45\times\frac{34.496}{3}\\ &=609.84+15\times34.496\\ &=609.84+517.44\\ &=1127.28\ \text{cm}^3 \end{aligned}$$

    Step 4 — the syrup is 30% of this.

    $$\begin{aligned} \text{syrup}&=\frac{30}{100}\times1127.28\\ &=338.184\ \text{cm}^3 \end{aligned}$$

    about 338 cm³ of syrup

    Keep \(\dfrac{34.496}{3}\) as a fraction until you multiply by 45: since \(45\div3=15\), the 3 cancels exactly and nothing is rounded on the way. If you round \(\tfrac43\pi r^3\) to 11.5 cm³ first, the answer drifts by a cubic centimetre or more.

    Q4

    A pen stand made of wood is in the shape of a cuboid with four cone-shaped depressions cut into it to hold pens. The cuboid measures 15 cm by 10 cm by 3.5 cm, and each depression has radius 0.5 cm and depth 1.4 cm. Find the volume of wood in the entire stand.

    Solution

    Given: cuboid \(l=15\) cm, \(b=10\) cm, \(h=3.5\) cm; four conical depressions, each of radius \(r=0.5\) cm and depth \(h_1=1.4\) cm. To find: the volume of wood in the stand.

    The wood in the depressions has been scooped out, so

    $$\text{wood}=\text{cuboid}-4\times(\text{one cone})$$

    Step 1 — volume of the cuboid.

    $$\begin{aligned} V_{\text{cuboid}}&=l\,b\,h\\ &=15\times10\times3.5\\ &=525\ \text{cm}^3 \end{aligned}$$

    Step 2 — volume of one conical depression.

    $$\begin{aligned} V_{\text{cone}}&=\frac13\pi r^2h_1\\ &=\frac13\times\frac{22}{7}\times0.5\times0.5\times1.4\\ &=\frac13\times\frac{22}{7}\times0.35\\ &=\frac13\times1.1=\frac{1.1}{3}\ \text{cm}^3 \end{aligned}$$

    Step 3 — subtract all four.

    $$\begin{aligned} 4\times\frac{1.1}{3}&=\frac{4.4}{3}=1.4667\ \text{cm}^3\ (\text{approx.})\\[2pt] \text{Volume of wood}&=525-\frac{4.4}{3}\\ &=525-1.4667\\ &=523.5333\ \text{cm}^3\ (\text{approx.}) \end{aligned}$$

    523.53 cm³ of wood (to two decimal places)

    Two traps here. First, the depressions are holes, so their volume is subtracted, never added. Second, the depth 1.4 cm is the height of each cone — it has nothing to do with the 3.5 cm thickness of the block (the cones are only 1.4 cm deep, so they do not go through it).

    Q5

    A vessel is in the form of an inverted cone — its vertex points downwards. Its height is 8 cm and the radius of its open top is 5 cm. It is filled with water right up to the brim. Lead shots, each a sphere of radius 0.5 cm, are then dropped into the vessel, and one fourth of the water flows out. Find how many lead shots were dropped in.

    Solution

    Given: cone-shaped vessel with \(h=8\) cm and \(r=5\) cm, full of water; each lead shot is a sphere of radius \(r_1=0.5\) cm; the water that flows out is \(\tfrac14\) of what the vessel held. To find: the number of lead shots, \(n\).

    The vessel is full, so every shot that sinks pushes out exactly its own volume of water. Therefore

    $$n\times(\text{volume of one shot})=\text{volume of water that flowed out}$$

    Step 1 — volume of the vessel (= volume of water it held).

    $$\begin{aligned} V&=\frac13\pi r^2h\\ &=\frac13\times\pi\times5\times5\times8\\ &=\frac{200\pi}{3}\ \text{cm}^3 \end{aligned}$$

    Step 2 — the water that flowed out is one fourth of this.

    $$\begin{aligned} V_{\text{out}}&=\frac14\times\frac{200\pi}{3}\\ &=\frac{50\pi}{3}\ \text{cm}^3 \end{aligned}$$

    Step 3 — volume of one lead shot.

    $$\begin{aligned} V_{\text{shot}}&=\frac43\pi r_1^{\,3}=\frac43\pi(0.5)^3\\ &=\frac43\pi\times\frac18=\frac{\pi}{6}\ \text{cm}^3 \end{aligned}$$

    Step 4 — divide.

    $$\begin{aligned} n&=\frac{V_{\text{out}}}{V_{\text{shot}}}=\frac{50\pi/3}{\pi/6}\\ &=\frac{50\pi}{3}\times\frac{6}{\pi}\\ &=\frac{300}{3}=100 \end{aligned}$$

    100 lead shots were dropped into the vessel

    Do not reach for \(\tfrac{22}{7}\) here. Both volumes carry a \(\pi\), and it cancels when you divide — so keeping \(\pi\) as a symbol makes the whole question one line of cancelling. Whenever a question asks “how many small solids …”, it is a division of one volume by another.

    Q6

    A solid iron pole is made of a cylinder of height 220 cm and base diameter 24 cm, with a second cylinder of height 60 cm and radius 8 cm fixed on top of it. Given that 1 cm³ of iron has a mass of about 8 g, find the mass of the pole. (Use \(\pi=3.14\).)

    Solution

    Given: lower cylinder — diameter 24 cm so \(r_1=12\) cm, \(h_1=220\) cm; upper cylinder — \(r_2=8\) cm, \(h_2=60\) cm; \(\pi=3.14\); 1 cm³ of iron has mass 8 g. To find: the mass of the pole.

    The pole is solid, so its volume is the sum of the volumes of the two cylinders.

    Step 1 — volume of the lower cylinder.

    $$\begin{aligned} V_1&=\pi r_1^{\,2}h_1\\ &=3.14\times12\times12\times220\\ &=3.14\times31680\\ &=99475.2\ \text{cm}^3 \end{aligned}$$

    Step 2 — volume of the upper cylinder.

    $$\begin{aligned} V_2&=\pi r_2^{\,2}h_2\\ &=3.14\times8\times8\times60\\ &=3.14\times3840\\ &=12057.6\ \text{cm}^3 \end{aligned}$$

    Step 3 — total volume.

    $$\begin{aligned} V&=V_1+V_2\\ &=99475.2+12057.6\\ &=111532.8\ \text{cm}^3 \end{aligned}$$

    Step 4 — turn volume into mass.

    $$\begin{aligned} \text{mass}&=111532.8\times8\ \text{g}\\ &=892262.4\ \text{g}\\ &=\frac{892262.4}{1000}\ \text{kg}=892.2624\ \text{kg} \end{aligned}$$

    about 892.26 kg

    Read the two cylinders carefully: the question gives the diameter of the lower one (24 cm, so \(r_1=12\) cm) but the radius of the upper one (8 cm — do not halve it). At the end remember 1 kg = 1000 g, so the grams are divided by 1000, not by 100.

    Q7

    A solid is made of a right circular cone of height 120 cm and radius 60 cm standing on a hemisphere of radius 60 cm. It is placed upright inside a right circular cylinder that is full of water, so that it touches the bottom of the cylinder. The cylinder has radius 60 cm and height 180 cm. Find the volume of the water left in the cylinder.

    Solution

    Given: cone \(h_1=120\) cm, \(r=60\) cm; hemisphere \(r=60\) cm; cylinder \(r=60\) cm, \(H=180\) cm, full of water. To find: the volume of water left in the cylinder.

    The solid pushes out its own volume of water, so

    $$\text{water left}=V_{\text{cylinder}}-V_{\text{solid}}$$

    Check that the solid really fits. Its height is \(60+120=180\) cm, the same as the height of the cylinder, and its greatest radius is 60 cm, the same as the cylinder's. So the solid stands inside with its tip just at the top — all of it is under water, and all of it counts.

    Step 1 — volume of the cylinder. Keep \(\pi\) as a symbol for now.

    $$\begin{aligned} V_{\text{cylinder}}&=\pi r^2H\\ &=\pi\times60\times60\times180\\ &=648000\pi\ \text{cm}^3 \end{aligned}$$

    Step 2 — volume of the solid.

    $$\begin{aligned} V_{\text{cone}}&=\frac13\pi r^2h_1\\ &=\frac13\times\pi\times60\times60\times120\\ &=144000\pi\ \text{cm}^3\\[2pt] V_{\text{hemisphere}}&=\frac23\pi r^3=\frac23\times\pi\times(60)^3\\ &=144000\pi\ \text{cm}^3 \end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned} V_{\text{solid}}&=144000\pi+144000\pi\\ &=288000\pi\ \text{cm}^3 \end{aligned}$$

    Step 3 — subtract, then put \(\pi=\dfrac{22}{7}\).

    $$\begin{aligned} \text{water left}&=648000\pi-288000\pi\\ &=360000\pi\\ &=360000\times\frac{22}{7}\\ &=\frac{7920000}{7}\\ &=1131428.57\ \text{cm}^3\ (\text{approx.}) \end{aligned}$$

    Step 4 — change to cubic metres. Since \(1\ \text{m}^3=100\times100\times100=10^6\ \text{cm}^3\),

    $$\begin{aligned} \text{water left}&=\frac{1131428.57}{1000000}\ \text{m}^3\\ &=1.131\ \text{m}^3\ (\text{approx.}) \end{aligned}$$

    about 1.131 m³, that is about 1131428.57 cm³

    Carrying \(\pi\) through Steps 1–3 turns the subtraction into \(648000\pi-288000\pi\) — one line, no long decimals. Convert to m³ only at the end, and divide by \(10^6\), not by 100: a cubic metre is a million cubic centimetres.

    Q8

    A glass vessel has a spherical body with a cylindrical neck on top. The neck is 8 cm long and 2 cm in diameter, and the spherical part is 8.5 cm in diameter. By measuring the water it holds, a child works out its volume as 345 cm³. Taking these as the inside measurements and \(\pi=3.14\), check whether she is correct.

    Solution

    Given: spherical part — diameter 8.5 cm so \(R=4.25\) cm; neck — a cylinder of length \(h=8\) cm and diameter 2 cm so \(r=1\) cm; \(\pi=3.14\). To check: whether the volume is 345 cm³.

    The vessel holds water in the ball and in the neck, so the two volumes are added.

    Step 1 — volume of the spherical part.

    $$\begin{aligned} V_1&=\frac43\pi R^3\\ &=\frac43\times3.14\times(4.25)^3\\ &=\frac43\times3.14\times76.765625\\ &=\frac43\times241.0440625\\ &=321.39\ \text{cm}^3\ (\text{2 d.p.}) \end{aligned}$$

    Step 2 — volume of the cylindrical neck.

    $$\begin{aligned} V_2&=\pi r^2h\\ &=3.14\times1\times1\times8\\ &=25.12\ \text{cm}^3 \end{aligned}$$

    Step 3 — the volume the vessel holds.

    $$\begin{aligned} V&=V_1+V_2\\ &=321.39+25.12\\ &=346.51\ \text{cm}^3 \end{aligned}$$

    Since \(346.51\ne345\), the child's figure is wrong — she is about 1.5 cm³ short.

    She is not correct. The vessel holds about 346.51 cm³, not 345 cm³.

    Two things to be careful about. The sphere's formula needs the radius \(4.25\) cm, not the diameter 8.5 cm; and \((4.25)^3=76.765625\) must be worked out in full — rounding it to 76.8 already moves the answer. Also, the neck is a proper cylinder of its own: leaving it out gives 321.39 cm³, which is nowhere near.

    Surface Areas and Volumes

    The five basic solids

    • Cuboid, cube, cylinder, cone, sphere
    • A hemisphere is half a sphere
    • Know CSA, TSA and volume of each
    • Cone: \(l=\sqrt{r^2+h^2}\)

    Surface area of a combination

    • Add only the surfaces you can see
    • The two faces that touch are gone
    • Never add the two TSAs
    • Unequal radii leave a ring to count

    Volume of a combination

    • Volumes simply add — nothing is lost
    • Solid taken out → subtract its volume
    • Displaced water = volume of the solid
    • 1000 cm³ = 1 litre, \(1\text{ m}^3=10^6\text{ cm}^3\)

    Hollowed out and scooped

    • Cavity: drop the flat face, add the curved one
    • Cylinder − cone (same \(r, h\)) \(=\tfrac23\pi r^2h\)
    • Hemisphere on / in a cube: \(6a^2+\pi r^2\)
    • Greatest diameter on a cube = the edge

    Reading the height right

    • Total height = cone \(h\) + hemisphere \(r\)
    • Capsule: cylinder length \(=L-2r\)
    • Diameter given → halve it first
    • Slant height is never the height

    Cost, mass, capacity

    • Cost = area × rate (canvas, paint, polish)
    • Mass = volume × mass of 1 cm³
    • Tent, canvas: curved surfaces only
    • Watch the units before you multiply

    Formulas to remember

    • Cuboid\(V=lbh\), TSA \(=2(lb+bh+hl)\), LSA \(=2h(l+b)\)
    • Cube\(V=a^3\), TSA \(=6a^2\)
    • Cylinder\(V=\pi r^2h\), CSA \(=2\pi rh\), TSA \(=2\pi r(r+h)\)
    • Cone\(V=\tfrac13\pi r^2h\), CSA \(=\pi rl\), TSA \(=\pi r(l+r)\)
    • Slant height\(l=\sqrt{r^2+h^2}\)
    • Sphere\(V=\tfrac43\pi r^3\), surface area \(=4\pi r^2\)
    • Hemisphere\(V=\tfrac23\pi r^3\), CSA \(=2\pi r^2\), TSA (solid) \(=3\pi r^2\)
    • Combination — surface areaAdd the exposed surfaces only; the faces in contact are not counted.
    • Combination — volumeVolumes add; a hollowed-out part is subtracted.
    • Same base, same heightcone : hemisphere : cylinder \(=1:2:3\)
    • CapsuleSurface area \(=2\pi rL\), where \(L\) is the total length.

    Mistakes that cost marks

    • Adding the two total surface areas when solids are joined.The faces that touch are hidden. Add the curved surfaces you can see.
    • Taking the total height of a toy as the height of the cone.A hemisphere adds its radius: \(h_{\text{cone}}=\)total height\(-r\).
    • Using \(2\pi r^2\) for the total surface area of a solid hemisphere.A solid hemisphere has a flat face too: TSA \(=3\pi r^2\).
    • Forgetting the \(\tfrac13\) in the volume of a cone.\(V=\tfrac13\pi r^2h\). A cone is one third of its cylinder.
    • Putting the diameter into the formula instead of the radius.Write \(r=\tfrac d2\) on the very first line of the answer.
    • Using \(h\) where the question needs \(l\) (or the other way round).CSA of a cone needs \(l\); volume needs \(h\). Find the missing one by \(l^2=r^2+h^2\).
    • Charging for canvas or paint over the base of a tent.Read the question: “the base is not covered” means curved surfaces only.
    • Mixing metres with centimetres, or forgetting 1 litre \(=1000\) cm³.Convert everything to one unit before substituting.
