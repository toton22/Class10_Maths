Chapter 3 Pair of Linear Equations in Two Variables

One equation in two unknowns has endless answers. Add a second condition and, nearly always, exactly one pair (x, y) survives — the point where two straight lines cross. Learn to find it by drawing, by substitution and by elimination.

    3.1 Two unknowns, two conditions

    One equation in two unknowns is not enough to pin them down — it has endless solutions. Give a second condition and, usually, exactly one pair \((x,\,y)\) survives. This chapter is about finding that pair: first by drawing, then by algebra.

    • Draw it

      Each equation is a straight line. Where the two lines cross, both equations are true at once.

    • Classify it

      From the coefficients alone, say whether the pair has one solution, none, or infinitely many — without drawing anything.

    • Solve it

      Two clean algebraic methods, substitution and elimination, and a steady way of turning a word problem into two equations.

    From Class IX: an equation of the form \(ax+by+c=0\), where \(a\), \(b\) and \(c\) are real numbers and \(a\) and \(b\) are not both zero, is called a linear equation in two variables \(x\) and \(y\).

    A solution of such an equation is a pair of numbers \((x,\,y)\) that makes the two sides equal. Every linear equation in two variables has infinitely many solutions, and the graph of all of them is a straight line.

    The condition “\(a\) and \(b\) not both zero” is often written \(a^2+b^2\ne0\).

    A day at the fair

    Akhila visits a village fair. She takes some rides on the Giant Wheel and plays Hoopla (the game where you throw a ring over an object and keep whatever it covers) a few times. She tells her friends two things: the number of Hoopla games she played was half the number of rides she took, and altogether she spent ₹ 20. A ride costs ₹ 3 and one game of Hoopla costs ₹ 4. How many rides and how many games was that?

    Guessing case by case is slow. Name the unknowns instead:

    Let \(x\)= number of rides on the Giant Wheel
    Let \(y\)= number of times she played Hoopla

    “Hoopla games are half the rides” gives \(y=\tfrac12x\), that is, \(x-2y=0\). “₹ 3 per ride and ₹ 4 per game came to ₹ 20” gives \(3x+4y=20\). So the situation is caught by two equations at the same time:

    $$\begin{aligned} x-2y&=0 &&\cdots(1)\\ 3x+4y&=20 &&\cdots(2) \end{aligned}$$

    Neither equation alone fixes \(x\) and \(y\). Together they do. Such a couple of equations has a name.

    Definition

    Two linear equations in the same two variables \(x\) and \(y\) form a pair of linear equations in two variables. Its general form is

    $$\begin{aligned} a_1x+b_1y+c_1&=0\\ a_2x+b_2y+c_2&=0 \end{aligned}$$

    where \(a_1,b_1,c_1,a_2,b_2,c_2\) are real numbers, with \(a_1^2+b_1^2\ne0\) and \(a_2^2+b_2^2\ne0\).

    A solution of the pair is a pair of values \((x,\,y)\) that satisfies both equations at once.

    Before you compare coefficients, always push everything to the left side so that each equation reads “\(\ldots=0\)”. \(3x+4y=20\) becomes \(3x+4y-20=0\), so \(c_2=-20\), not \(20\). Sign slips here cost more marks in this chapter than anything else.

    Only three things can happen to a pair of linear equations: it has exactly one solution, no solution, or infinitely many solutions. There is never a pair with exactly two, or exactly five, solutions. Section 3.2 shows why, with a picture.

    Which of these is a pair of linear equations in two variables? (a) \(2x+3y=7\) and \(x-y=1\) (b) \(x^2+y=4\) and \(x+y=2\) (c) \(3x-5=0\) and \(2x+7y=9\)

    Show answer

    (a) Yes. (b) No — \(x^2\) makes the first equation non-linear. (c) Yes: \(3x-5=0\) is \(3x+0\cdot y-5=0\), which is allowed because \(a=3\) and \(b=0\) are not both zero.

    3.2 The graphical method

    Draw both equations on one sheet of graph paper. Each is a straight line, and a point that lies on both lines is a solution of both equations. So solving the pair means finding the common points of the two lines.

    Akhila's answer, read off a graph

    Take the pair from Section 3.1. For each equation find two convenient solutions (a third is a useful check) and make a small table of values.

    \(x-2y=0\), i.e. \(y=\dfrac{x}{2}\)
    \(x\)\(0\)\(4\)\(6\)
    \(y\)\(0\)\(2\)\(3\)
    \(3x+4y=20\), i.e. \(y=\dfrac{20-3x}{4}\)
    \(x\)\(0\)\(4\)\(8\)
    \(y\)\(5\)\(2\)\(-1\)

    Check in words, never only in symbols: 4 rides at ₹ 3 is ₹ 12, 2 games at ₹ 4 is ₹ 8, and \(12+8=20\) ✓. Also 2 is half of 4 ✓.

    1. Write each equation with \(y\) (or \(x\)) alone, so values are easy to compute.
    2. For each equation choose two or three convenient values of \(x\) — usually the ones that make \(y\) a whole number — and make a value table.
    3. Plot the points of the first table and join them with a ruled line; do the same for the second table. Extend both lines across the sheet and label each line with its equation.
    4. Read the coordinates of the point where the lines cross. That pair \((x,\,y)\) is the solution.
    5. Substitute it back into both original equations to confirm.

    Take the two points of a table far apart. If you plot \((0,5)\) and \((1,4.25)\), a tiny slip in either point swings the line badly and your crossing point comes out wrong. Wide points, sharp pencil, long line.

    What can two lines do?

    Two straight lines drawn on the same sheet have only three possible relationships, and each one tells you the number of solutions immediately.

    The three words to use

    A pair of linear equations that has at least one solution is called consistent.

    A pair that has no solution is called inconsistent.

    A pair in which the two equations are really the same equation in disguise has infinitely many solutions; it is called dependent. A dependent pair is always consistent.

    “Consistent” means the two statements can hold together — they do not fight. “Inconsistent” means they contradict each other, like “the total is ₹ 4” and “the total is ₹ 12” about the same purchase. If a question asks “consistent or inconsistent?”, it is really asking “does a solution exist?”.

    Deciding the case without drawing

    Drawing is slow. Compare the coefficients instead. Write the pair as \(a_1x+b_1y+c_1=0\) and \(a_2x+b_2y+c_2=0\) and look at the three ratios \(\dfrac{a_1}{a_2}\), \(\dfrac{b_1}{b_2}\) and \(\dfrac{c_1}{c_2}\). Here are three pairs whose graphs are known:

    Pair of equations\(\dfrac{a_1}{a_2}\)\(\dfrac{b_1}{b_2}\)\(\dfrac{c_1}{c_2}\)LinesSolutions
    \(\begin{aligned}x-2y&=0\\3x+4y-20&=0\end{aligned}\)\(\tfrac13\)\(\tfrac{-2}{4}\)\(\tfrac{0}{-20}\)intersectingexactly one
    \(\begin{aligned}2x+3y-9&=0\\4x+6y-18&=0\end{aligned}\)\(\tfrac24\)\(\tfrac36\)\(\tfrac{-9}{-18}\)coincidentinfinitely many
    \(\begin{aligned}x+2y-4&=0\\2x+4y-12&=0\end{aligned}\)\(\tfrac12\)\(\tfrac24\)\(\tfrac{-4}{-12}\)parallelnone

    In the first row \(\frac{a_1}{a_2}=\frac13\) but \(\frac{b_1}{b_2}=-\frac12\) — the first two ratios differ. In the second row all three ratios equal \(\frac12\). In the third row the first two are \(\frac12\) but the last is \(\frac13\). That is the whole rule.

    The ratio test
    $$\frac{a_1}{a_2}\ne\frac{b_1}{b_2}\;\Rightarrow\;\text{one solution}$$ $$\frac{a_1}{a_2}=\frac{b_1}{b_2}=\frac{c_1}{c_2}\;\Rightarrow\;\text{infinitely many}$$ $$\frac{a_1}{a_2}=\frac{b_1}{b_2}\ne\frac{c_1}{c_2}\;\Rightarrow\;\text{no solution}$$

    In words: intersecting lines, consistent in the first case; coincident lines, dependent and consistent in the second; parallel lines, inconsistent in the third.

    Both equations must first be written in the form \(ax+by+c=0\), and the top row of every ratio must come from the same equation. The converse is also true: parallel lines force \(\frac{a_1}{a_2}=\frac{b_1}{b_2}\ne\frac{c_1}{c_2}\), and so on.

    How to use it

    1. Shift everything to the left: \(\ldots=0\).
    2. Write the three ratios, keeping signs.
    3. Reduce each to lowest terms.
    4. Compare \(\frac{a_1}{a_2}\) with \(\frac{b_1}{b_2}\) first — that single comparison already separates “one solution” from the other two cases.

    What to write in the exam

    State the ratios, reduce, compare, then give both conclusions: the kind of lines and the number of solutions, with the word consistent or inconsistent.

    Example: “\(\frac{a_1}{a_2}=\frac{b_1}{b_2}=\frac{c_1}{c_2}=\frac12\), so the lines are coincident and the pair has infinitely many solutions; it is consistent (dependent).”

    Two traps with the ratios.

    Order. Once you call one equation “first”, every numerator must come from it. Writing \(\frac{a_1}{a_2}\) but \(\frac{b_2}{b_1}\) turns a correct pair into a wrong answer.

    The \(c\) ratio. Only look at \(\frac{c_1}{c_2}\) after you know \(\frac{a_1}{a_2}=\frac{b_1}{b_2}\). If \(c_2=0\) the fraction \(\frac{c_1}{c_2}\) is undefined; in that case check instead whether \(c_1\) is also 0 — both zero means coincident, otherwise parallel.

    NCERT Example 1graphical check

    Decide from a graph whether the pair of equations

    $$\begin{aligned} x+3y&=6 &&\cdots(1)\\ 2x-3y&=12 &&\cdots(2) \end{aligned}$$

    is consistent. If it is, solve it graphically.

    Solution

    Make \(y\) the subject in each equation and find two solutions of each.

    \(y=\dfrac{6-x}{3}\)
    \(x\)\(0\)\(6\)
    \(y\)\(2\)\(0\)
    \(y=\dfrac{2x-12}{3}\)
    \(x\)\(0\)\(3\)
    \(y\)\(-4\)\(-2\)

    Plot \(\mathrm A(0,2)\) and \(\mathrm B(6,0)\) and join them for equation (1); plot \(\mathrm P(0,-4)\) and \(\mathrm Q(3,-2)\) and join them for equation (2). Extend both lines.

    The lines are not parallel — they cut each other at the single point \(\mathrm B(6,0)\), which lies on both. So a solution exists, and the pair is consistent.

    Check: \(x+3y=6+3(0)=6\) ✓ and \(2x-3y=12-0=12\) ✓.

    The pair is consistent; \(x=6,\ y=0\).

    The ratio test agrees: \(\dfrac{a_1}{a_2}=\dfrac12\) and \(\dfrac{b_1}{b_2}=\dfrac{3}{-3}=-1\). Since \(\dfrac{a_1}{a_2}\ne\dfrac{b_1}{b_2}\), the lines intersect and there is exactly one solution.

    NCERT Example 2coincident lines

    Find graphically whether the pair

    $$\begin{aligned} 5x-8y+1&=0 &&\cdots(1)\\ 3x-\frac{24}{5}y+\frac35&=0 &&\cdots(2) \end{aligned}$$

    has no solution, a unique solution, or infinitely many solutions.

    Solution

    Equation (2) has awkward fractions. Clear them by multiplying the whole equation by \(\dfrac53\) — multiplying an equation by a non-zero number does not change its graph:

    $$\begin{aligned} \frac53\left(3x-\frac{24}{5}y+\frac35\right)&=0\\ 5x-8y+1&=0 \end{aligned}$$

    This is exactly equation (1). So the two equations describe the same line: the lines are coincident.

    Because every point of that one line satisfies both equations, the pair has infinitely many solutions.

    The pair has infinitely many solutions (coincident lines; dependent and consistent).

    Same conclusion from the ratios: \(\dfrac{a_1}{a_2}=\dfrac53\), \(\dfrac{b_1}{b_2}=\dfrac{-8}{-\frac{24}{5}}=8\times\dfrac{5}{24}=\dfrac53\), \(\dfrac{c_1}{c_2}=\dfrac{1}{\frac35}=\dfrac53\). All three are equal.

    When an equation carries fractions, multiply it through by the LCM of the denominators before you compare anything or plot anything. \(3x-\frac{24}{5}y+\frac35=0\) becomes \(15x-24y+3=0\), and dividing by 3 gives \(5x-8y+1=0\). Same line, far friendlier numbers.

    NCERT Example 3word problem

    At a sale, Champa bought some pants and some skirts. Asked how many of each, she said: “The number of skirts is two less than twice the number of pants I bought. The number of skirts is also four less than four times the number of pants.” Find how many pants and how many skirts Champa bought.

    Solution

    Let \(x\) = the number of pants and \(y\) = the number of skirts.

    “Skirts are two less than twice the pants” gives \(y=2x-2\). “Skirts are four less than four times the pants” gives \(y=4x-4\). So

    $$\begin{aligned} y&=2x-2 &&\cdots(1)\\ y&=4x-4 &&\cdots(2) \end{aligned}$$

    Find two solutions of each equation.

    \(y=2x-2\)
    \(x\)\(2\)\(0\)
    \(y\)\(2\)\(-2\)
    \(y=4x-4\)
    \(x\)\(0\)\(1\)
    \(y\)\(-4\)\(0\)

    The two lines meet at \(\mathrm Q(1,0)\), so \(x=1\) and \(y=0\).

    Check: \(2x-2=2(1)-2=0=y\) ✓ and \(4x-4=4(1)-4=0=y\) ✓.

    Champa bought 1 pant and no skirt.

    An answer of “0 skirts” feels wrong, but it is not. The two sentences in the question are both true only for 1 pant and 0 skirts. In word problems always check the answer against the words, not just the symbols — and only reject it if the words themselves make it impossible (a negative number of skirts, a fraction of a person, and so on).

    1. Name the unknownsWrite “Let \(x=\ldots\), \(y=\ldots\)” in full, with units. This line alone carries marks.
    2. Turn each sentence into an equationOne fact, one equation. Two facts must give two equations.
    3. SolveGraph, substitution or elimination — whatever the question allows.
    4. Answer in words“The cost of one pencil is ₹ 3.” Never leave a bare \(x=3\).
    5. Check against the storyPut the numbers back into the original sentences, not into your own equations.

    Graph questions in the board paper come in two shapes. “Solve graphically” wants the full job: value tables, a clean drawing on graph paper, both lines labelled with their equations, the crossing point named and the answer stated. “Is the pair consistent?” or “How many solutions?” wants only the three ratios and one sentence — no graph at all. Reading the verb saves you ten minutes.

    Some questions add a third line, usually the \(x\)-axis (\(y=0\)) or the \(y\)-axis (\(x=0\)), and ask for the vertices of the triangle formed. Treat the axis as one more equation: solve it with each of your two lines and you have the other two vertices.

    Without drawing, say how many solutions each pair has: (a) \(2x+y=6,\ 4x+2y=12\) (b) \(x+y=5,\ 2x+2y=12\) (c) \(3x-y=2,\ x+y=6\).

    Show answer

    (a) \(\frac24=\frac12=\frac{-6}{-12}\) — all equal, so coincident lines and infinitely many solutions (consistent, dependent).

    (b) \(\frac12=\frac12\) but \(\frac{-5}{-12}=\frac{5}{12}\ne\frac12\) — parallel lines, no solution (inconsistent).

    (c) \(\frac{3}{1}\ne\frac{-1}{1}\) — intersecting lines, exactly one solution: \(x=2,\ y=4\).

    3.3 Algebraic methods

    A graph is honest but blunt. It can only show you a point you can actually read off the paper. When the crossing point is \(\left(\sqrt3,\;2\sqrt7\right)\) or \((-1.75,\;3.3)\) or \(\left(\dfrac{4}{13},\;\dfrac{1}{19}\right)\), no amount of care with a pencil will give you those numbers. Algebra will.

    Two algebraic methods are in your syllabus: substitution and elimination. Both reduce the pair to a single equation in one variable, which you already know how to solve. They always give the same answer — pick whichever is less work.

    3.3.1 Substitution method

    The idea in one line: make one variable the subject in one equation, then put that expression into the other equation. The second equation then contains only one variable.

    1. Look at both equations and pick the one in which a variable is easiest to make alone — best of all, a variable whose coefficient is \(1\) or \(-1\).
    2. Make that variable the subject. Call this Equation (3) and keep it: you need it again at the end.
    3. Substitute the expression into the other equation. Only one variable is left.
    4. Solve that equation.
    5. Put the value back into Equation (3) to get the second variable.
    6. Verify both values in both original equations.

    Substituting into the same equation you rearranged is the single most common mistake in this section. You get \(3=3\) — true but useless — and lose the question. The expression from equation (1) must go into equation (2).

    When you substitute, put the expression in brackets: \(7(3-2y)\), not \(7\times3-2y\).

    NCERT Example 4substitution basics

    Solve by the substitution method:

    $$\begin{aligned} 7x-15y&=2 &&\cdots(1)\\ x+2y&=3 &&\cdots(2) \end{aligned}$$
    Solution

    Step 1. In equation (2) the coefficient of \(x\) is 1, so \(x\) is the easiest to isolate. From (2),

    $$x=3-2y\qquad\cdots(3)$$

    Step 2. Substitute this value of \(x\) in equation (1):

    $$\begin{aligned} 7(3-2y)-15y&=2\\ 21-14y-15y&=2\\ -29y&=2-21\\ -29y&=-19\\ y&=\frac{19}{29} \end{aligned}$$

    Step 3. Put \(y=\dfrac{19}{29}\) into equation (3):

    $$\begin{aligned} x&=3-2\left(\frac{19}{29}\right)=3-\frac{38}{29}\\ &=\frac{87-38}{29}=\frac{49}{29} \end{aligned}$$

    Verification. Put both values back into the original equations:

    $$\begin{aligned} 7x-15y&=\frac{343-285}{29}=\frac{58}{29}=2\\ x+2y&=\frac{49+38}{29}=\frac{87}{29}=3 \end{aligned}$$

    Both are satisfied ✓.

    \(x=\dfrac{49}{29},\quad y=\dfrac{19}{29}\)

    Notice how badly a graph would have served here: \(\frac{49}{29}\approx1.69\) and \(\frac{19}{29}\approx0.66\) — you could never read those off graph paper.

    NCERT Example 5age problem

    Aftab says to his daughter: “Seven years ago I was seven times as old as you were then. Three years from now I shall be three times as old as you will be.” Set this up as a pair of linear equations and solve it by substitution.

    Solution

    Let \(s\) = Aftab's present age in years and \(t\) = his daughter's present age in years.

    Seven years ago the daughter was \(t-7\) and Aftab was \(s-7\), and he was seven times her age:

    $$s-7=7(t-7)\quad\Rightarrow\quad s-7t+42=0\qquad\cdots(1)$$

    Three years from now she will be \(t+3\) and he will be \(s+3\), and he will be three times her age:

    $$s+3=3(t+3)\quad\Rightarrow\quad s-3t=6\qquad\cdots(2)$$

    From equation (2), \(s=3t+6\). Substituting this in equation (1):

    $$\begin{aligned} (3t+6)-7t+42&=0\\ -4t+48&=0\\ 4t&=48\\ t&=12 \end{aligned}$$

    Then \(s=3(12)+6=42\).

    Check against the story. Seven years ago they were 5 and 35, and \(35=7\times5\) ✓. Three years from now they will be 15 and 45, and \(45=3\times15\) ✓.

    Aftab is 42 years old and his daughter is 12 years old.

    Age problems. Always let the present ages be the variables. Then “\(n\) years ago” is \(x-n\) and “\(n\) years hence” is \(x+n\) — for both people. Forgetting to age the second person is the classic slip.

    Write “was/will be seven times” as an equation with the multiplier on the younger person: \(\text{older}=7\times\text{younger}\).

    NCERT Example 6infinitely many

    In a shop, 2 pencils and 3 erasers cost ₹ 9, while 4 pencils and 6 erasers cost ₹ 18. Find the cost of one pencil and of one eraser.

    Solution

    Let \(x\) = the cost of one pencil in rupees and \(y\) = the cost of one eraser in rupees. The two purchases give

    $$\begin{aligned} 2x+3y&=9 &&\cdots(1)\\ 4x+6y&=18 &&\cdots(2) \end{aligned}$$

    From equation (1), express \(x\) in terms of \(y\):

    $$x=\frac{9-3y}{2}\qquad\cdots(3)$$

    Substitute this value of \(x\) in equation (2):

    $$\begin{aligned} \frac{4(9-3y)}{2}+6y&=18\\ 2(9-3y)+6y&=18\\ 18-6y+6y&=18\\ 18&=18 \end{aligned}$$

    Both variables have vanished and what is left, \(18=18\), is true whatever \(y\) is. So no single value of \(y\) is forced on us, and no single value of \(x\) either.

    This has happened because equation (2) is just equation (1) multiplied by 2 — the second sentence of the question repeats the first and adds nothing new. The pair has infinitely many solutions.

    The costs cannot be found: the pair has infinitely many solutions.

    For instance \(y=1,\ x=3\) works (2 pencils + 3 erasers \(=6+3=9\)) and so does \(y=3,\ x=0\). Both fit the data, so the data is not enough. In ratio terms \(\frac{2}{4}=\frac{3}{6}=\frac{-9}{-18}=\frac12\): coincident lines.

    NCERT Example 7no solution

    Two rails are laid along the lines \(x+2y-4=0\) and \(2x+4y-12=0\). Will the two rails ever cross each other?

    Solution

    The rails cross exactly where the two equations hold together, so solve the pair

    $$\begin{aligned} x+2y-4&=0 &&\cdots(1)\\ 2x+4y-12&=0 &&\cdots(2) \end{aligned}$$

    From equation (1), \(x=4-2y\). Substituting in equation (2):

    $$\begin{aligned} 2(4-2y)+4y-12&=0\\ 8-4y+4y-12&=0\\ -4&=0 \end{aligned}$$

    This is a false statement — it cannot hold for any \(y\). So no pair \((x,\,y)\) satisfies both equations, and the pair has no solution.

    No — the two rails are parallel and will never cross.

    Ratios: \(\frac12=\frac24\) but \(\frac{-4}{-12}=\frac13\ne\frac12\), which is exactly the parallel case (inconsistent).

    You reach \(0=0\) (or \(18=18\))

    A true statement with no variable in it.

    Meaning: the two equations are the same line. Infinitely many solutions — the pair is dependent and consistent.

    Write: “The pair has infinitely many solutions.”

    You reach \(-4=0\) (or \(0=9\))

    A false statement with no variable in it.

    Meaning: the two lines are parallel. No solution — the pair is inconsistent.

    Write: “The pair has no solution.”

    If both variables disappear, do not write “\(x=0\)” or “no answer”. Look at what is left. \(0=0\) means infinitely many solutions; \(0=9\) means none. Saying which one, and giving the reason, is what earns the mark.

    Solve by substitution: \(2x+y=7\) and \(3x-y=8\).

    Show answer

    From the first, \(y=7-2x\). Substituting in the second: \(3x-(7-2x)=8\Rightarrow5x-7=8\Rightarrow x=3\). Then \(y=7-6=1\). Check: \(2(3)+1=7\) ✓, \(3(3)-1=8\) ✓. So \(x=3,\ y=1\).

    3.3.2 Elimination method

    Here you do not rearrange anything. Instead you make the coefficients of one variable numerically equal and then add or subtract the two equations, so that variable is wiped out. For most board questions this is faster and tidier than substitution.

    1. Write both equations in the same order: \(ax+by=c\), with the \(x\)-terms above each other and the \(y\)-terms above each other.
    2. Multiply one equation, or both, by suitable non-zero numbers so that the coefficients of one variable become numerically equal.
    3. If those coefficients now have the same sign, subtract one equation from the other; if they have opposite signs, add. That variable disappears.
    4. Solve the one-variable equation that is left. (If instead you are left with a true statement such as \(0=0\), the pair has infinitely many solutions; if it is false, such as \(0=9\), the pair has no solution.)
    5. Substitute the value into the simpler original equation to get the other variable, and check both.

    Same signs → Subtract. Different signs → aDD. And when you subtract, subtract every term, including the right-hand side: \((28x-27x)-(12y-12y)=8000-6000\).

    NCERT Example 8ratio problem

    The incomes of two people are in the ratio \(9:7\) and their monthly expenditures are in the ratio \(4:3\). Each of them saves ₹ 2000 a month. Find the monthly income of each.

    Solution

    Because the incomes are in the ratio 9 : 7, let them be ₹ \(9x\) and ₹ \(7x\). Because the expenditures are in the ratio 4 : 3, let them be ₹ \(4y\) and ₹ \(3y\).

    Saving = income − expenditure, and each saves ₹ 2000:

    $$\begin{aligned} 9x-4y&=2000 &&\cdots(1)\\ 7x-3y&=2000 &&\cdots(2) \end{aligned}$$

    Step 1. Make the coefficients of \(y\) numerically equal. Multiply (1) by 3 and (2) by 4:

    $$\begin{aligned} 27x-12y&=6000 &&\cdots(3)\\ 28x-12y&=8000 &&\cdots(4) \end{aligned}$$

    Step 2. The coefficients of \(y\) are equal and have the same sign, so subtract (3) from (4). The \(y\) terms cancel, because \(-12y-(-12y)=0\):

    $$\begin{aligned} 28x-27x&=8000-6000\\ x&=2000 \end{aligned}$$

    Step 3. Substitute \(x=2000\) in equation (1):

    $$\begin{aligned} 9(2000)-4y&=2000\\ 18000-4y&=2000\\ 4y&=16000\\ y&=4000 \end{aligned}$$

    So the incomes are \(9x=\text{₹}\,18000\) and \(7x=\text{₹}\,14000\); the expenditures are \(4y=\text{₹}\,16000\) and \(3y=\text{₹}\,12000\).

    Verification. \(18000:14000=9:7\) ✓ and \(16000:12000=4:3\) ✓. Savings: \(18000-16000=2000\) ✓ and \(14000-12000=2000\) ✓.

    The monthly incomes are ₹ 18,000 and ₹ 14,000.

    The whole question turns on one modelling step: a ratio \(9:7\) must become \(9x\) and \(7x\) with the same \(x\), and the expenditure ratio needs its own letter \(y\). Using \(x\) for both would force income and expenditure to be in the same ratio, which they are not.

    You may eliminate whichever variable you like. In Example 8 you could instead multiply (1) by 7 and (2) by 9 to knock out \(x\), reaching \(-28y+27y=14000-18000\), that is \(y=4000\). Same answer, a little more arithmetic. Choose the variable whose coefficients have the smaller LCM.

    NCERT Example 9no solution

    Use the elimination method to find all possible solutions of

    $$\begin{aligned} 2x+3y&=8 &&\cdots(1)\\ 4x+6y&=7 &&\cdots(2) \end{aligned}$$
    Solution

    Step 1. Multiply equation (1) by 2 and leave equation (2) as it is, so that the coefficients of \(x\) match:

    $$\begin{aligned} 4x+6y&=16 &&\cdots(3)\\ 4x+6y&=7 &&\cdots(4) \end{aligned}$$

    Step 2. The coefficients of \(x\) have the same sign, so subtract (4) from (3):

    $$\begin{aligned} (4x-4x)+(6y-6y)&=16-7\\ 0&=9 \end{aligned}$$

    This is a false statement. No values of \(x\) and \(y\) can make it true, so the pair has no common solution.

    The pair has no solution.

    Look at the lines: \(\frac{2}{4}=\frac{3}{6}=\frac12\) but \(\frac{-8}{-7}=\frac87\ne\frac12\). The lines are parallel, which is why the algebra ran into a contradiction. The question says “all possible solutions”, so the complete answer is “there are none”.

    NCERT Example 10digits problem

    A two-digit number and the number formed by reversing its digits add up to 66. The two digits differ by 2. Find the number, and say how many such numbers there are.

    Solution

    Let \(x\) be the tens digit and \(y\) the units digit of the number. In expanded form the number is \(10x+y\) — for instance \(56=10(5)+6\).

    Reversing the digits puts \(y\) in the tens place and \(x\) in the units place, so the reversed number is \(10y+x\) — for instance reversing 56 gives \(65=10(6)+5\).

    First condition. The two numbers add up to 66:

    $$\begin{aligned} (10x+y)+(10y+x)&=66\\ 11x+11y&=66\\ 11(x+y)&=66\\ x+y&=6\qquad\cdots(1) \end{aligned}$$

    Second condition. The digits differ by 2. “Differ” does not say which one is bigger, so there are two possibilities:

    $$x-y=2\qquad\cdots(2)\qquad\text{or}\qquad y-x=2\qquad\cdots(3)$$

    Case 1: equations (1) and (2). The coefficients of \(y\) are \(+1\) and \(-1\) — opposite signs, so add:

    $$\begin{aligned} (x+y)+(x-y)&=6+2\\ 2x&=8\Rightarrow x=4 \end{aligned}$$

    Then \(y=6-4=2\), and the number is \(10(4)+2=42\).

    Case 2: equations (1) and (3). Again add:

    $$\begin{aligned} (x+y)+(y-x)&=6+2\\ 2y&=8\Rightarrow y=4 \end{aligned}$$

    Then \(x=6-4=2\), and the number is \(10(2)+4=24\).

    Verification. \(42+24=66\) and \(4-2=2\) ✓. For 24: \(24+42=66\) and \(4-2=2\) ✓.

    There are two such numbers: 42 and 24.

    Whenever a question says two quantities “differ by \(k\)” without saying which is larger, expect two answers and solve both cases. If it says “exceeds by \(k\)” or “is greater by \(k\)”, the larger one is named and there is only one case.

    Digit facts worth memorising: a two-digit number with tens digit \(x\) and units digit \(y\) is \(10x+y\); reversed it is \(10y+x\); their sum is \(11(x+y)\) and their difference is \(9(x-y)\). Both 11 and 9 appear again and again in these questions.

    Prefer substitution when…

    • a variable has coefficient \(1\) or \(-1\) (\(x+2y=3\));
    • one equation already reads \(y=\ldots\) or \(x=\ldots\);
    • the equations come from a word problem where one quantity is described in terms of the other.

    Prefer elimination when…

    • no coefficient is 1, as in \(9x-4y=2000\) and \(7x-3y=2000\);
    • a coefficient already matches, or matches after one small multiplication;
    • the numbers are large — elimination keeps them whole, substitution creates fractions.

    Solve by elimination: \(3x+2y=11\) and \(2x+3y=4\).

    Show answer

    Multiply the first by 3 and the second by 2: \(9x+6y=33\) and \(4x+6y=8\). Same signs, so subtract: \(5x=25\Rightarrow x=5\). Then \(3(5)+2y=11\Rightarrow2y=-4\Rightarrow y=-2\). Check: \(2(5)+3(-2)=10-6=4\) ✓. So \(x=5,\ y=-2\).

    For which value of \(k\) does the pair \(2x+3y=5\) and \(kx+9y=7\) have no solution?

    7 questions · forming a pair of equations, the graphical method, and the ratio test for intersecting, parallel and coincident lines

    Q1

    Form a pair of linear equations for each situation below, and then solve it graphically.

    1. Ten students of Class X took part in a Mathematics quiz. The number of girls in the group was 4 more than the number of boys. Find how many boys and how many girls took part.
    2. Five pencils and seven pens together cost ₹ 50. Seven pencils and five pens together cost ₹ 46. Find the cost of one pencil and the cost of one pen.
    Solution

    Method for every graphical question: name the two unknowns → write the two equations → make a small table of two or three points for each line → plot and join → read off the point where the lines cross → check the answer in the original words of the problem.

    (i)

    Let \(x\) = the number of girls and \(y\) = the number of boys.

    Altogether there are 10 students:

    $$x+y=10 \qquad\ldots(1)$$

    The girls are 4 more than the boys, so \(x=y+4\), that is

    $$x-y=4 \qquad\ldots(2)$$

    Now take two convenient points on each line.

    Line (1): \(x+y=10\), i.e. \(y=10-x\)
    \(x\)010
    \(y\)100
    Line (2): \(x-y=4\), i.e. \(y=x-4\)
    \(x\)410
    \(y\)06

    Plot A(0, 10) and B(10, 0) and join them; plot C(4, 0) and D(10, 6) and join them.

    The lines meet at the single point \(\text{P}(7,\,3)\), so \(x=7\) and \(y=3\).

    7 girls and 3 boys took part in the quiz.

    Check: \(7+3=10\) students ✓ and \(7-3=4\), so the girls are 4 more than the boys ✓

    (ii)

    Let \(x\) = the cost of one pencil (in ₹) and \(y\) = the cost of one pen (in ₹).

    5 pencils and 7 pens cost ₹ 50:

    $$5x+7y=50 \qquad\ldots(1)$$

    7 pencils and 5 pens cost ₹ 46:

    $$7x+5y=46 \qquad\ldots(2)$$

    From (1), \(y=\dfrac{50-5x}{7}\); from (2), \(y=\dfrac{46-7x}{5}\). Choose \(x\) so that \(y\) comes out a whole number.

    Line (1): \(5x+7y=50\)
    \(x\)10−4
    \(y\)010
    Line (2): \(7x+5y=46\)
    \(x\)8−2
    \(y\)−212

    The lines meet at \(\text{P}(3,\,5)\), so \(x=3\) and \(y=5\).

    One pencil costs ₹ 3 and one pen costs ₹ 5.

    Check: \(5(3)+7(5)=15+35=50\) ✓ and \(7(3)+5(5)=21+25=46\) ✓

    The negative numbers in the tables are used only to draw the straight lines accurately. The answer must come from the crossing point, and here it is positive, as a price must be.

    Q2

    For each pair of equations below, compare the ratios \(\dfrac{a_1}{a_2}\), \(\dfrac{b_1}{b_2}\) and \(\dfrac{c_1}{c_2}\) and say whether the two lines intersect at a point, are parallel, or are coincident.

    1. \(5x-4y+8=0\) and \(7x+6y-9=0\)
    2. \(9x+3y+12=0\) and \(18x+6y+24=0\)
    3. \(6x-3y+10=0\) and \(2x-y+9=0\)
    Solution

    Write both equations in the standard form \(a_1x+b_1y+c_1=0\) and \(a_2x+b_2y+c_2=0\), keeping the signs. Then use this test:

    RatiosThe linesSolutions
    \(\dfrac{a_1}{a_2}\ne\dfrac{b_1}{b_2}\)intersect at one pointexactly one (unique)
    \(\dfrac{a_1}{a_2}=\dfrac{b_1}{b_2}\ne\dfrac{c_1}{c_2}\)are parallelnone
    \(\dfrac{a_1}{a_2}=\dfrac{b_1}{b_2}=\dfrac{c_1}{c_2}\)are coincidentinfinitely many
    (i)

    \(5x-4y+8=0\) and \(7x+6y-9=0\)

    Here \(a_1=5,\ b_1=-4,\ c_1=8\) and \(a_2=7,\ b_2=6,\ c_2=-9\).

    $$\frac{a_1}{a_2}=\frac57,\qquad \frac{b_1}{b_2}=\frac{-4}{6}=-\frac23$$

    Since \(\dfrac57\ne-\dfrac23\), we have \(\dfrac{a_1}{a_2}\ne\dfrac{b_1}{b_2}\).

    The lines intersect at a point (one unique solution).

    (ii)

    \(9x+3y+12=0\) and \(18x+6y+24=0\)

    $$\begin{aligned} \frac{a_1}{a_2}&=\frac{9}{18}=\frac12\\ \frac{b_1}{b_2}&=\frac{3}{6}=\frac12\\ \frac{c_1}{c_2}&=\frac{12}{24}=\frac12 \end{aligned}$$

    All three ratios are equal: \(\dfrac{a_1}{a_2}=\dfrac{b_1}{b_2}=\dfrac{c_1}{c_2}\).

    The lines are coincident (infinitely many solutions).

    Dividing the second equation by 2 gives \(9x+3y+12=0\) — exactly the first equation. That is why one line lies on the other.

    (iii)

    \(6x-3y+10=0\) and \(2x-y+9=0\)

    $$\begin{aligned} \frac{a_1}{a_2}&=\frac62=3\\ \frac{b_1}{b_2}&=\frac{-3}{-1}=3\\ \frac{c_1}{c_2}&=\frac{10}{9} \end{aligned}$$

    Here \(\dfrac{a_1}{a_2}=\dfrac{b_1}{b_2}=3\) but \(\dfrac{c_1}{c_2}=\dfrac{10}{9}\ne3\).

    The lines are parallel (no solution).

    Part\(\dfrac{a_1}{a_2}\)\(\dfrac{b_1}{b_2}\)\(\dfrac{c_1}{c_2}\)The lines
    (i)\(\tfrac57\)\(-\tfrac23\)\(-\tfrac89\)intersecting
    (ii)\(\tfrac12\)\(\tfrac12\)\(\tfrac12\)coincident
    (iii)\(3\)\(3\)\(\tfrac{10}{9}\)parallel

    Carry the minus signs into the ratios. In (i) the ratio \(\dfrac{b_1}{b_2}\) is \(\dfrac{-4}{6}\), not \(\dfrac46\). Comparing \(\dfrac57\) with \(\dfrac23\) instead of \(-\dfrac23\) is the most common slip here — although in this part the conclusion happens to be the same.

    Q3

    By comparing the ratios \(\dfrac{a_1}{a_2}\), \(\dfrac{b_1}{b_2}\) and \(\dfrac{c_1}{c_2}\), decide whether each pair of equations below is consistent or inconsistent.

    1. \(3x+2y=5\) and \(2x-3y=7\)
    2. \(2x-3y=8\) and \(4x-6y=9\)
    3. \(\dfrac32x+\dfrac53y=7\) and \(9x-10y=14\)
    4. \(5x-3y=11\) and \(-10x+6y=-22\)
    5. \(\dfrac43x+2y=8\) and \(2x+3y=12\)
    Solution

    Consistent means the pair has at least one solution — the lines either intersect at one point or are coincident. Inconsistent means there is no solution at all — the lines are parallel. So only the parallel case \(\dfrac{a_1}{a_2}=\dfrac{b_1}{b_2}\ne\dfrac{c_1}{c_2}\) is inconsistent; the other two cases are consistent.

    (i)

    \(3x+2y-5=0\) and \(2x-3y-7=0\)

    $$\frac{a_1}{a_2}=\frac32,\qquad \frac{b_1}{b_2}=\frac{2}{-3}=-\frac23$$

    \(\dfrac32\ne-\dfrac23\), so \(\dfrac{a_1}{a_2}\ne\dfrac{b_1}{b_2}\): the lines intersect at one point.

    Consistent — one unique solution.

    (ii)

    \(2x-3y-8=0\) and \(4x-6y-9=0\)

    $$\begin{aligned} \frac{a_1}{a_2}&=\frac24=\frac12\\ \frac{b_1}{b_2}&=\frac{-3}{-6}=\frac12\\ \frac{c_1}{c_2}&=\frac{-8}{-9}=\frac89 \end{aligned}$$

    \(\dfrac{a_1}{a_2}=\dfrac{b_1}{b_2}=\dfrac12\) but \(\dfrac{c_1}{c_2}=\dfrac89\ne\dfrac12\): the lines are parallel.

    Inconsistent — no solution.

    (iii)

    \(\dfrac32x+\dfrac53y-7=0\) and \(9x-10y-14=0\)

    $$\frac{a_1}{a_2}=\frac{3/2}{9}=\frac{3}{2}\times\frac19=\frac16$$ $$\frac{b_1}{b_2}=\frac{5/3}{-10}=\frac53\times\frac{1}{-10}=-\frac16$$

    \(\dfrac16\ne-\dfrac16\), so \(\dfrac{a_1}{a_2}\ne\dfrac{b_1}{b_2}\): the lines intersect at one point.

    Consistent — one unique solution.

    (iv)

    \(5x-3y-11=0\) and \(-10x+6y+22=0\)

    $$\begin{aligned} \frac{a_1}{a_2}&=\frac{5}{-10}=-\frac12\\ \frac{b_1}{b_2}&=\frac{-3}{6}=-\frac12\\ \frac{c_1}{c_2}&=\frac{-11}{22}=-\frac12 \end{aligned}$$

    All three ratios are equal, so the lines are coincident.

    Consistent (dependent) — infinitely many solutions.

    Multiplying the first equation by \(-2\) gives the second one exactly, so the two equations say the same thing.

    (v)

    \(\dfrac43x+2y-8=0\) and \(2x+3y-12=0\)

    $$\begin{aligned} \frac{a_1}{a_2}&=\frac{4/3}{2}=\frac43\times\frac12=\frac23\\ \frac{b_1}{b_2}&=\frac23\\ \frac{c_1}{c_2}&=\frac{-8}{-12}=\frac23 \end{aligned}$$

    All three ratios are equal, so the lines are coincident.

    Consistent (dependent) — infinitely many solutions.

    Multiplying the first equation by \(\dfrac32\) gives \(2x+3y-12=0\), the second equation.

    Part\(\dfrac{a_1}{a_2}\)\(\dfrac{b_1}{b_2}\)\(\dfrac{c_1}{c_2}\)LinesAnswer
    (i)\(\tfrac32\)\(-\tfrac23\)\(\tfrac57\)intersectingconsistent
    (ii)\(\tfrac12\)\(\tfrac12\)\(\tfrac89\)parallelinconsistent
    (iii)\(\tfrac16\)\(-\tfrac16\)\(\tfrac12\)intersectingconsistent
    (iv)\(-\tfrac12\)\(-\tfrac12\)\(-\tfrac12\)coincidentconsistent
    (v)\(\tfrac23\)\(\tfrac23\)\(\tfrac23\)coincidentconsistent

    Read the question carefully. Question 2 asks how the lines lie (intersecting / parallel / coincident); Question 3 asks whether a solution exists (consistent / inconsistent). The same three ratios answer both — only the word you write at the end changes.

    Q5

    A rectangular garden is 4 m longer than it is wide, and half of its perimeter measures 36 m. Find the length and the width of the garden.

    Solution

    Let \(x\) = the length of the garden in metres and \(y\) = its width in metres.

    The perimeter of a rectangle is \(2(x+y)\), so half the perimeter is \(x+y\). This is 36 m:

    $$x+y=36 \qquad\ldots(1)$$

    The length is 4 m more than the width, so \(x=y+4\), that is

    $$x-y=4 \qquad\ldots(2)$$

    Add (1) and (2) — the \(y\) terms cancel:

    $$\begin{aligned}(x+y)+(x-y)&=36+4\\ 2x&=40\\ x&=20\end{aligned}$$

    Put \(x=20\) in (1): \(20+y=36\), so \(y=16\).

    Length = 20 m and width = 16 m.

    Check: the perimeter is \(2(20+16)=72\) m, and half of it is 36 m ✓ Also \(20-16=4\) m ✓

    Graphically, the lines \(x+y=36\) and \(x-y=4\) meet at the single point \((20,16)\) — but with numbers this large a graph needs a very big sheet, so the algebraic step above is the sensible way to finish.

    Q6

    You are given the equation \(2x+3y-8=0\). Write down a second linear equation in two variables so that the two lines together are:

    1. intersecting lines
    2. parallel lines
    3. coincident lines
    Solution

    Here \(a_1=2,\ b_1=3,\ c_1=-8\). Choose \(a_2,\ b_2,\ c_2\) to make the ratios behave as the question asks. Many answers are possible; one of each is shown.

    (i)

    Intersecting lines need \(\dfrac{a_1}{a_2}\ne\dfrac{b_1}{b_2}\). Simply swap the coefficients of \(x\) and \(y\):

    \(3x+2y-7=0\)

    Check: \(\dfrac{a_1}{a_2}=\dfrac23\) and \(\dfrac{b_1}{b_2}=\dfrac32\). These are different, so the lines meet — in fact at \((1,2)\).

    (ii)

    Parallel lines need \(\dfrac{a_1}{a_2}=\dfrac{b_1}{b_2}\ne\dfrac{c_1}{c_2}\). Keep the same \(x\) and \(y\) parts and change only the constant:

    \(2x+3y-12=0\)

    Check: \(\dfrac{a_1}{a_2}=\dfrac22=1\),  \(\dfrac{b_1}{b_2}=\dfrac33=1\),  \(\dfrac{c_1}{c_2}=\dfrac{-8}{-12}=\dfrac23\). The first two agree, the third does not ✓

    (iii)

    Coincident lines need \(\dfrac{a_1}{a_2}=\dfrac{b_1}{b_2}=\dfrac{c_1}{c_2}\). Multiply the whole equation by any non-zero number, say 2:

    \(4x+6y-16=0\)

    Check: \(\dfrac{a_1}{a_2}=\dfrac24=\dfrac12\),  \(\dfrac{b_1}{b_2}=\dfrac36=\dfrac12\),  \(\dfrac{c_1}{c_2}=\dfrac{-8}{-16}=\dfrac12\). All three are equal ✓

    The safest recipe in an exam: for coincident multiply the given equation by 2 or 3; for parallel copy the \(x\) and \(y\) terms and change the constant; for intersecting swap the coefficients of \(x\) and \(y\). Then show the ratio check — that is where the mark is.

    Q7

    Draw the graphs of \(x-y+1=0\) and \(3x+2y-12=0\). These two lines and the \(x\)-axis enclose a triangle. Write down the coordinates of its three vertices and shade the triangular region.

    Solution

    The triangle has one side on the \(x\)-axis, so two of its vertices are the points where the given lines cut the \(x\)-axis, and the third is the point where the two lines cut each other.

    Line (1): \(x-y+1=0\), i.e. \(y=x+1\)
    \(x\)−104
    \(y\)015
    Line (2): \(3x+2y-12=0\), i.e. \(y=\dfrac{12-3x}{2}\)
    \(x\)024
    \(y\)630

    Where line (1) meets the \(x\)-axis. On the \(x\)-axis \(y=0\), so \(x-0+1=0\), giving \(x=-1\). That vertex is \(\text{A}(-1,\,0)\).

    Where line (2) meets the \(x\)-axis. Put \(y=0\): \(3x+0-12=0\), giving \(x=4\). That vertex is \(\text{B}(4,\,0)\).

    Where the two lines meet. From (1), \(y=x+1\). Substituting in (2):

    $$\begin{aligned}3x+2(x+1)-12&=0\\ 3x+2x+2-12&=0\\ 5x&=10\\ x&=2\end{aligned}$$

    Then \(y=2+1=3\), so that vertex is \(\text{C}(2,\,3)\). The graph shows the same crossing point.

    The vertices of the triangle are \(\text{A}(-1,0)\), \(\text{B}(4,0)\) and \(\text{C}(2,3)\); the region ABC is shaded.

    A useful extra: the base \(\text{AB}=4-(-1)=5\) units and the height of C above the \(x\)-axis is 3 units, so the shaded area is \(\tfrac12\times5\times3=7.5\) square units. The board often adds this as the last part of the question.

    Exercise 3.2

    3 questions · the substitution method, and word problems solved by substitution

    Q1

    Solve each pair of linear equations below by the substitution method.

    1. \(x+y=14\) and \(x-y=4\)
    2. \(s-t=3\) and \(\dfrac s3+\dfrac t2=6\)
    3. \(3x-y=3\) and \(9x-3y=9\)
    4. \(0.2x+0.3y=1.3\) and \(0.4x+0.5y=2.3\)
    5. \(\sqrt2\,x+\sqrt3\,y=0\) and \(\sqrt3\,x-\sqrt8\,y=0\)
    6. \(\dfrac{3x}{2}-\dfrac{5y}{3}=-2\) and \(\dfrac x3+\dfrac y2=\dfrac{13}{6}\)
    Solution

    The substitution method in three steps:

    1. From the easier equation, write one variable in terms of the other.
    2. Put that expression into the other equation. Only one variable is left, so solve it.
    3. Put the value found back into the expression of Step 1 to get the second variable. Then verify in both original equations.
    (i)

    \(x+y=14\ \ldots(1)\qquad x-y=4\ \ldots(2)\)

    Step 1. From (2): \(x=4+y\)  …(3)

    Step 2. Put this value of \(x\) in (1):

    $$\begin{aligned}(4+y)+y&=14\\ 2y&=10\\ y&=5\end{aligned}$$

    Step 3. Put \(y=5\) in (3): \(x=4+5=9\).

    \(x=9,\ y=5\)

    Check: \(9+5=14\) ✓ and \(9-5=4\) ✓

    (ii)

    \(s-t=3\ \ldots(1)\qquad \dfrac s3+\dfrac t2=6\ \ldots(2)\)

    Step 1. From (1): \(s=t+3\)  …(3)

    Step 2. Put this in (2):

    $$\frac{t+3}{3}+\frac t2=6$$

    Multiply every term by 6, the LCM of 3 and 2:

    $$\begin{aligned}2(t+3)+3t&=36\\ 2t+6+3t&=36\\ 5t&=30\\ t&=6\end{aligned}$$

    Step 3. Put \(t=6\) in (3): \(s=6+3=9\).

    \(s=9,\ t=6\)

    Check: \(9-6=3\) ✓ and \(\dfrac93+\dfrac62=3+3=6\) ✓

    (iii)

    \(3x-y=3\ \ldots(1)\qquad 9x-3y=9\ \ldots(2)\)

    Step 1. From (1): \(y=3x-3\)  …(3)

    Step 2. Put this in (2):

    $$\begin{aligned}9x-3(3x-3)&=9\\ 9x-9x+9&=9\\ 9&=9\end{aligned}$$

    Both variables have vanished and what is left is a true statement. So every value of \(x\) works, provided \(y\) is taken as \(3x-3\). The pair has infinitely many solutions.

    The ratio test says the same thing: for \(3x-y-3=0\) and \(9x-3y-9=0\),

    $$\frac{a_1}{a_2}=\frac39=\frac13,\qquad \frac{b_1}{b_2}=\frac{-1}{-3}=\frac13,\qquad \frac{c_1}{c_2}=\frac{-3}{-9}=\frac13$$

    All three are equal, so the two lines are coincident.

    Infinitely many solutions: \(y=3x-3\), where \(x\) may be any real number. For instance \((0,-3),\ (1,0),\ (2,3)\) all satisfy both equations.

    A true statement such as \(9=9\) means infinitely many solutions. A false statement such as \(0=9\) would mean no solution. Never write “\(x=0\)” or “no answer” when the variables cancel — say which of the two cases it is.

    (iv)

    \(0.2x+0.3y=1.3\ \ldots(1)\qquad 0.4x+0.5y=2.3\ \ldots(2)\)

    First clear the decimals — multiply both equations by 10:

    $$2x+3y=13\ \ldots(1'),\qquad 4x+5y=23\ \ldots(2')$$

    Step 1. From \((1')\): \(x=\dfrac{13-3y}{2}\)  …(3)

    Step 2. Put this in \((2')\):

    $$\begin{aligned}4\!\left(\frac{13-3y}{2}\right)+5y&=23\\ 2(13-3y)+5y&=23\\ 26-6y+5y&=23\\ -y&=-3\\ y&=3\end{aligned}$$

    Step 3. Put \(y=3\) in (3): \(x=\dfrac{13-9}{2}=\dfrac42=2\).

    \(x=2,\ y=3\)

    Check: \(0.2(2)+0.3(3)=0.4+0.9=1.3\) ✓ and \(0.4(2)+0.5(3)=0.8+1.5=2.3\) ✓

    (v)

    \(\sqrt2\,x+\sqrt3\,y=0\ \ldots(1)\qquad \sqrt3\,x-\sqrt8\,y=0\ \ldots(2)\)

    Step 1. From (1): \(\sqrt2\,x=-\sqrt3\,y\), so

    $$x=-\frac{\sqrt3}{\sqrt2}\,y \qquad\ldots(3)$$

    Step 2. Note that \(\sqrt8=\sqrt{4\times2}=2\sqrt2\). Put (3) in (2):

    $$\begin{aligned}\sqrt3\left(-\frac{\sqrt3}{\sqrt2}\,y\right)-2\sqrt2\,y&=0\\ -\frac{3}{\sqrt2}\,y-2\sqrt2\,y&=0\end{aligned}$$

    Multiply every term by \(\sqrt2\):

    $$\begin{aligned}-3y-2(2)y&=0\\ -7y&=0\\ y&=0\end{aligned}$$

    Step 3. Put \(y=0\) in (3): \(x=0\).

    \(x=0,\ y=0\)

    Check: \(\sqrt2(0)+\sqrt3(0)=0\) ✓ and \(\sqrt3(0)-\sqrt8(0)=0\) ✓

    Both equations have constant term 0, so both lines pass through the origin. Since \(\dfrac{\sqrt2}{\sqrt3}\ne\dfrac{\sqrt3}{-\sqrt8}\), they are different lines, and two different lines through the origin meet only at the origin.

    (vi)

    \(\dfrac{3x}{2}-\dfrac{5y}{3}=-2\ \ldots(1)\qquad \dfrac x3+\dfrac y2=\dfrac{13}{6}\ \ldots(2)\)

    Clear the fractions first. Multiply (1) by 6 and (2) by 6:

    $$9x-10y=-12\ \ldots(1'),\qquad 2x+3y=13\ \ldots(2')$$

    Step 1. From \((2')\): \(x=\dfrac{13-3y}{2}\)  …(3)

    Step 2. Put this in \((1')\):

    $$\begin{aligned}9\!\left(\frac{13-3y}{2}\right)-10y&=-12\\ 9(13-3y)-20y&=-24\\ 117-27y-20y&=-24\\ -47y&=-141\\ y&=3\end{aligned}$$

    Step 3. Put \(y=3\) in (3): \(x=\dfrac{13-9}{2}=2\).

    \(x=2,\ y=3\)

    Check: \(\dfrac{3(2)}{2}-\dfrac{5(3)}{3}=3-5=-2\) ✓ and \(\dfrac23+\dfrac32=\dfrac46+\dfrac96=\dfrac{13}{6}\) ✓

    Q2

    Solve \(2x+3y=11\) and \(2x-4y=-24\). Using the values you get, find the number \(m\) for which \(y=mx+3\).

    Solution

    \(2x+3y=11\ \ldots(1)\qquad 2x-4y=-24\ \ldots(2)\)

    Step 1. From (1): \(2x=11-3y\), so

    $$x=\frac{11-3y}{2} \qquad\ldots(3)$$

    Step 2. Put this in (2):

    $$\begin{aligned}2\!\left(\frac{11-3y}{2}\right)-4y&=-24\\ 11-3y-4y&=-24\\ -7y&=-35\\ y&=5\end{aligned}$$

    Step 3. Put \(y=5\) in (3): \(x=\dfrac{11-15}{2}=\dfrac{-4}{2}=-2\).

    So the solution of the pair is \(x=-2,\ y=5\).

    Finding \(m\). The point \((-2,\,5)\) must satisfy \(y=mx+3\):

    $$\begin{aligned}5&=m(-2)+3\\ 5-3&=-2m\\ 2&=-2m\\ m&=-1\end{aligned}$$

    \(x=-2,\ y=5\) and \(m=-1\)

    Check: \(2(-2)+3(5)=-4+15=11\) ✓, \(2(-2)-4(5)=-4-20=-24\) ✓, and with \(m=-1\) the line \(y=-x+3\) gives \(y=-(-2)+3=5\) ✓

    Q3

    For each problem below, form a pair of linear equations and solve it by the substitution method.

    1. Two numbers differ by 26, and the bigger one is three times the smaller one. Find the two numbers.
    2. Two angles are supplementary. The bigger angle is 18 degrees more than the smaller one. Find both angles.
    3. A cricket coach buys 7 bats and 6 balls for ₹ 3800. Some days later she buys 3 bats and 5 balls for ₹ 1750. Find the cost of one bat and the cost of one ball.
    4. A taxi fare in a city is made up of a fixed charge plus a charge for every kilometre travelled. A 10 km ride costs ₹ 105 and a 15 km ride costs ₹ 155. Find the fixed charge and the charge per kilometre, and work out the fare for a 25 km ride.
    5. Adding 2 to both the numerator and the denominator of a fraction turns it into \(\dfrac9{11}\). Adding 3 to both instead turns it into \(\dfrac56\). Find the fraction.
    6. In five years' time Jacob will be three times as old as his son. Five years ago Jacob was seven times as old as his son. Find their present ages.
    Solution
    (i)

    Let \(x\) = the larger number and \(y\) = the smaller number.

    Their difference is 26: \(\ x-y=26\ \ldots(1)\)

    The larger is three times the smaller: \(\ x=3y\ \ldots(2)\)

    Put \(x=3y\) from (2) into (1):

    $$\begin{aligned}3y-y&=26\\ 2y&=26\\ y&=13\end{aligned}$$

    Then \(x=3(13)=39\).

    The two numbers are 39 and 13.

    Check: \(39-13=26\) ✓ and \(39=3\times13\) ✓

    (ii)

    Let \(x\) = the larger angle and \(y\) = the smaller angle, both in degrees.

    Supplementary angles add up to \(180^\circ\): \(\ x+y=180\ \ldots(1)\)

    The larger exceeds the smaller by 18: \(\ x-y=18\ \ldots(2)\)

    From (2): \(x=y+18\ \ldots(3)\). Put this in (1):

    $$\begin{aligned}(y+18)+y&=180\\ 2y&=162\\ y&=81\end{aligned}$$

    Then \(x=81+18=99\).

    The two angles are \(99^\circ\) and \(81^\circ\).

    Check: \(99^\circ+81^\circ=180^\circ\) ✓ and \(99^\circ-81^\circ=18^\circ\) ✓

    (iii)

    Let \(x\) = the cost of one bat (in ₹) and \(y\) = the cost of one ball (in ₹).

    First purchase: \(\ 7x+6y=3800\ \ldots(1)\)

    Second purchase: \(\ 3x+5y=1750\ \ldots(2)\)

    From (2): \(x=\dfrac{1750-5y}{3}\ \ldots(3)\). Put this in (1):

    $$\begin{aligned}7\!\left(\frac{1750-5y}{3}\right)+6y&=3800\\ 7(1750-5y)+18y&=11400\\ 12250-35y+18y&=11400\\ -17y&=-850\\ y&=50\end{aligned}$$

    Put \(y=50\) in (3): \(x=\dfrac{1750-250}{3}=\dfrac{1500}{3}=500\).

    One bat costs ₹ 500 and one ball costs ₹ 50.

    Check: \(7(500)+6(50)=3500+300=3800\) ✓ and \(3(500)+5(50)=1500+250=1750\) ✓

    (iv)

    Let \(x\) = the fixed charge (in ₹) and \(y\) = the charge for each kilometre (in ₹).

    For a 10 km ride: \(\ x+10y=105\ \ldots(1)\)

    For a 15 km ride: \(\ x+15y=155\ \ldots(2)\)

    From (1): \(x=105-10y\ \ldots(3)\). Put this in (2):

    $$\begin{aligned}(105-10y)+15y&=155\\ 5y&=50\\ y&=10\end{aligned}$$

    Put \(y=10\) in (3): \(x=105-100=5\).

    Fare for a 25 km ride:

    $$x+25y=5+25(10)=5+250=255$$

    Fixed charge ₹ 5, charge ₹ 10 per km; a 25 km ride costs ₹ 255.

    Check: \(5+10(10)=105\) ✓ and \(5+15(10)=155\) ✓

    (v)

    Let \(x\) = the numerator and \(y\) = the denominator, so the fraction is \(\dfrac xy\).

    Adding 2 to both gives \(\dfrac9{11}\):

    $$\frac{x+2}{y+2}=\frac{9}{11}\ \Rightarrow\ 11(x+2)=9(y+2)\ \Rightarrow\ 11x-9y+4=0\ \ldots(1)$$

    Adding 3 to both gives \(\dfrac56\):

    $$\frac{x+3}{y+3}=\frac{5}{6}\ \Rightarrow\ 6(x+3)=5(y+3)\ \Rightarrow\ 6x-5y+3=0\ \ldots(2)$$

    From (2): \(5y=6x+3\), so \(y=\dfrac{6x+3}{5}\ \ldots(3)\). Put this in (1):

    $$\begin{aligned}11x-9\!\left(\frac{6x+3}{5}\right)+4&=0\\ 55x-9(6x+3)+20&=0\\ 55x-54x-27+20&=0\\ x-7&=0\\ x&=7\end{aligned}$$

    Put \(x=7\) in (3): \(y=\dfrac{42+3}{5}=\dfrac{45}{5}=9\).

    The fraction is \(\dfrac79\).

    Check: \(\dfrac{7+2}{9+2}=\dfrac9{11}\) ✓ and \(\dfrac{7+3}{9+3}=\dfrac{10}{12}=\dfrac56\) ✓

    (vi)

    Let \(x\) = Jacob's present age and \(y\) = his son's present age, both in years.

    After 5 years Jacob will be \(x+5\) and his son \(y+5\), and Jacob will be three times as old:

    $$x+5=3(y+5)\ \Rightarrow\ x+5=3y+15\ \Rightarrow\ x-3y=10\ \ldots(1)$$

    Five years ago Jacob was \(x-5\) and his son \(y-5\), and Jacob was seven times as old:

    $$x-5=7(y-5)\ \Rightarrow\ x-5=7y-35\ \Rightarrow\ x-7y=-30\ \ldots(2)$$

    From (1): \(x=3y+10\ \ldots(3)\). Put this in (2):

    $$\begin{aligned}(3y+10)-7y&=-30\\ -4y&=-40\\ y&=10\end{aligned}$$

    Put \(y=10\) in (3): \(x=3(10)+10=40\).

    Jacob is 40 years old and his son is 10 years old.

    Check: in 5 years they will be 45 and 15, and \(45=3\times15\) ✓; 5 years ago they were 35 and 5, and \(35=7\times5\) ✓

    In every word problem the first two marks are for the two lines “Let \(x=\dots\), \(y=\dots\)” and the two equations. Write them even if you can see the answer — and finish with a sentence in words, with the unit (₹, years, degrees), not just a pair of numbers.

    Exercise 3.3

    2 questions · the elimination method — and the same pairs done again by substitution

    Q1

    Solve each pair of linear equations below twice: once by the elimination method and once by the substitution method.

    1. \(x+y=5\) and \(2x-3y=4\)
    2. \(3x+4y=10\) and \(2x-2y=2\)
    3. \(3x-5y-4=0\) and \(9x=2y+7\)
    4. \(\dfrac x2+\dfrac{2y}{3}=-1\) and \(x-\dfrac y3=3\)
    Solution

    The elimination method in four steps:

    1. Multiply one or both equations by suitable numbers so that the coefficients of one variable become numerically equal.
    2. Add the two equations if those coefficients have opposite signs, subtract if they have the same sign. That variable disappears.
    3. Solve the single equation that is left.
    4. Put the value back into either original equation to get the other variable, and verify in both.
    (i)

    \(x+y=5\ \ldots(1)\qquad 2x-3y=4\ \ldots(2)\)

    By elimination. Multiply (1) by 3 so that the \(y\) terms become \(+3y\) and \(-3y\):

    $$3x+3y=15 \qquad\ldots(3)$$

    Add (2) and (3) — the \(y\) terms cancel:

    $$\begin{aligned}(2x+3x)+(-3y+3y)&=4+15\\ 5x&=19\\ x&=\frac{19}{5}\end{aligned}$$

    Put \(x=\dfrac{19}{5}\) in (1):

    $$y=5-\frac{19}{5}=\frac{25-19}{5}=\frac65$$

    By substitution. From (1), \(y=5-x\). Put this in (2):

    $$\begin{aligned}2x-3(5-x)&=4\\ 2x-15+3x&=4\\ 5x&=19\\ x&=\frac{19}{5}\end{aligned}$$

    Then \(y=5-\dfrac{19}{5}=\dfrac65\) — the same answer.

    \(x=\dfrac{19}{5},\ y=\dfrac65\)

    Check: \(\dfrac{19}{5}+\dfrac65=\dfrac{25}{5}=5\) ✓ and \(2\!\left(\dfrac{19}{5}\right)-3\!\left(\dfrac65\right)=\dfrac{38-18}{5}=4\) ✓

    (ii)

    \(3x+4y=10\ \ldots(1)\qquad 2x-2y=2\ \ldots(2)\)

    By elimination. Multiply (2) by 2 so that the \(y\) terms become \(+4y\) and \(-4y\):

    $$4x-4y=4 \qquad\ldots(3)$$

    Add (1) and (3):

    $$\begin{aligned}(3x+4x)+(4y-4y)&=10+4\\ 7x&=14\\ x&=2\end{aligned}$$

    Put \(x=2\) in (2): \(4-2y=2\), so \(2y=2\) and \(y=1\).

    By substitution. Divide (2) by 2: \(x-y=1\), so \(x=y+1\). Put this in (1):

    $$\begin{aligned}3(y+1)+4y&=10\\ 3y+3+4y&=10\\ 7y&=7\\ y&=1\end{aligned}$$

    Then \(x=1+1=2\) — the same answer.

    \(x=2,\ y=1\)

    Check: \(3(2)+4(1)=6+4=10\) ✓ and \(2(2)-2(1)=4-2=2\) ✓

    (iii)

    Write both equations with the variables on the left:

    $$3x-5y=4\ \ldots(1),\qquad 9x-2y=7\ \ldots(2)$$

    By elimination. Multiply (1) by 3 to make both \(x\) terms \(9x\):

    $$9x-15y=12 \qquad\ldots(3)$$

    Both \(x\) terms now have the same sign, so subtract (2) from (3):

    $$\begin{aligned}(9x-9x)+(-15y+2y)&=12-7\\ -13y&=5\\ y&=-\frac{5}{13}\end{aligned}$$

    Put \(y=-\dfrac5{13}\) in (1):

    $$\begin{aligned}3x-5\!\left(-\frac5{13}\right)&=4\\ 3x+\frac{25}{13}&=4\\ 3x&=\frac{52-25}{13}=\frac{27}{13}\\ x&=\frac{9}{13}\end{aligned}$$

    By substitution. From (1), \(x=\dfrac{4+5y}{3}\). Put this in (2):

    $$\begin{aligned}9\!\left(\frac{4+5y}{3}\right)-2y&=7\\ 3(4+5y)-2y&=7\\ 12+15y-2y&=7\\ 13y&=-5\\ y&=-\frac5{13}\end{aligned}$$

    Then \(x=\dfrac{1}{3}\!\left(4-\dfrac{25}{13}\right)=\dfrac13\times\dfrac{27}{13}=\dfrac9{13}\) — the same answer.

    \(x=\dfrac9{13},\ y=-\dfrac5{13}\)

    Check: \(3\!\left(\dfrac9{13}\right)-5\!\left(-\dfrac5{13}\right)=\dfrac{27+25}{13}=4\) ✓ and \(9\!\left(\dfrac9{13}\right)=\dfrac{81}{13}=2\!\left(-\dfrac5{13}\right)+7\) ✓

    (iv)

    \(\dfrac x2+\dfrac{2y}{3}=-1\ \ldots(1)\qquad x-\dfrac y3=3\ \ldots(2)\)

    Clear the fractions: multiply (1) by 6 and (2) by 3.

    $$3x+4y=-6\ \ldots(1'),\qquad 3x-y=9\ \ldots(2')$$

    By elimination. The \(x\) terms are already equal, so subtract \((2')\) from \((1')\):

    $$\begin{aligned}(3x-3x)+(4y+y)&=-6-9\\ 5y&=-15\\ y&=-3\end{aligned}$$

    Put \(y=-3\) in \((2')\): \(3x-(-3)=9\), so \(3x=6\) and \(x=2\).

    By substitution. From \((2')\), \(y=3x-9\). Put this in \((1')\):

    $$\begin{aligned}3x+4(3x-9)&=-6\\ 3x+12x-36&=-6\\ 15x&=30\\ x&=2\end{aligned}$$

    Then \(y=3(2)-9=-3\) — the same answer.

    \(x=2,\ y=-3\)

    Check: \(\dfrac22+\dfrac{2(-3)}{3}=1-2=-1\) ✓ and \(2-\dfrac{-3}{3}=2+1=3\) ✓

    Which method to pick when the question lets you choose? If one variable already has coefficient \(1\) or \(-1\) (as in \(x+y=5\)), substitution is quickest. If the coefficients of one variable are equal or easy multiples (as in \(3x+4y\) and \(3x-y\)), elimination is quickest. Both always give the same answer.

    Add when the signs of the matched coefficients are opposite \((+3y\) and \(-3y)\), subtract when they are the same \((-15y\) and \(-2y)\). And when you subtract, change the sign of every term of the equation being taken away — forgetting the constant is the most common error in this exercise.

    Q2

    Form a pair of linear equations for each problem below and find the solution (if one exists) by the elimination method.

    1. A fraction becomes equal to 1 when 1 is added to its numerator and 1 is subtracted from its denominator. It becomes \(\dfrac12\) when 1 is added only to the denominator. Find the fraction.
    2. Five years ago Nuri was three times as old as Sonu. Ten years from now Nuri will be twice as old as Sonu. Find their present ages.
    3. The two digits of a two-digit number add up to 9. Nine times the number equals twice the number formed by writing the digits in the reverse order. Find the number.
    4. Meena withdrew ₹ 2000 from a bank and asked for ₹ 50 notes and ₹ 100 notes only. She received 25 notes altogether. Find how many notes of each kind she received.
    5. A lending library charges a fixed amount for the first three days and then a further fixed amount for every extra day. Saritha kept a book for seven days and paid ₹ 27; Susy kept a book for five days and paid ₹ 21. Find the fixed charge and the charge for each extra day.
    Solution
    (i)

    Let \(x\) = the numerator and \(y\) = the denominator, so the fraction is \(\dfrac xy\).

    Adding 1 to the numerator and subtracting 1 from the denominator gives 1:

    $$\frac{x+1}{y-1}=1\ \Rightarrow\ x+1=y-1\ \Rightarrow\ x-y=-2\ \ldots(1)$$

    Adding 1 only to the denominator gives \(\dfrac12\):

    $$\frac{x}{y+1}=\frac12\ \Rightarrow\ 2x=y+1\ \Rightarrow\ 2x-y=1\ \ldots(2)$$

    The \(y\) terms are already \(-y\) in both, so subtract (1) from (2):

    $$\begin{aligned}(2x-x)+(-y+y)&=1-(-2)\\ x&=3\end{aligned}$$

    Put \(x=3\) in (1): \(3-y=-2\), so \(y=5\).

    The fraction is \(\dfrac35\).

    Check: \(\dfrac{3+1}{5-1}=\dfrac44=1\) ✓ and \(\dfrac{3}{5+1}=\dfrac36=\dfrac12\) ✓

    (ii)

    Let \(x\) = Nuri's present age and \(y\) = Sonu's present age, both in years.

    Five years ago their ages were \(x-5\) and \(y-5\):

    $$x-5=3(y-5)\ \Rightarrow\ x-5=3y-15\ \Rightarrow\ x-3y=-10\ \ldots(1)$$

    Ten years from now their ages will be \(x+10\) and \(y+10\):

    $$x+10=2(y+10)\ \Rightarrow\ x+10=2y+20\ \Rightarrow\ x-2y=10\ \ldots(2)$$

    Both equations have \(x\) with coefficient 1, so subtract (1) from (2):

    $$\begin{aligned}(x-x)+(-2y+3y)&=10-(-10)\\ y&=20\end{aligned}$$

    Put \(y=20\) in (2): \(x-40=10\), so \(x=50\).

    Nuri is 50 years old and Sonu is 20 years old.

    Check: five years ago they were 45 and 15, and \(45=3\times15\) ✓; ten years from now they will be 60 and 30, and \(60=2\times30\) ✓

    (iii)

    Let \(x\) = the tens digit and \(y\) = the units digit.

    Then the number itself is \(10x+y\), and the number with the digits reversed is \(10y+x\). [for example \(37=10(3)+7\), and reversed \(73=10(7)+3\)]

    The digits add up to 9:

    $$x+y=9 \qquad\ldots(1)$$

    Nine times the number is twice the reversed number:

    $$\begin{aligned}9(10x+y)&=2(10y+x)\\ 90x+9y&=20y+2x\\ 88x-11y&=0\end{aligned}$$

    Divide by 11:

    $$8x-y=0 \qquad\ldots(2)$$

    The \(y\) terms are \(+y\) and \(-y\), so add (1) and (2):

    $$\begin{aligned}(x+8x)+(y-y)&=9+0\\ 9x&=9\\ x&=1\end{aligned}$$

    Put \(x=1\) in (1): \(1+y=9\), so \(y=8\).

    The number is \(10x+y=10(1)+8=18\).

    The number is 18.

    Check: the digits give \(1+8=9\) ✓. Nine times the number is \(9\times18=162\); the reversed number is 81 and twice it is \(2\times81=162\) ✓

    (iv)

    Let \(x\) = the number of ₹ 50 notes and \(y\) = the number of ₹ 100 notes.

    There are 25 notes in all:

    $$x+y=25 \qquad\ldots(1)$$

    Their total value is ₹ 2000:

    $$50x+100y=2000$$

    Divide by 50:

    $$x+2y=40 \qquad\ldots(2)$$

    The \(x\) terms are equal, so subtract (1) from (2):

    $$\begin{aligned}(x-x)+(2y-y)&=40-25\\ y&=15\end{aligned}$$

    Put \(y=15\) in (1): \(x+15=25\), so \(x=10\).

    Meena received 10 notes of ₹ 50 and 15 notes of ₹ 100.

    Check: \(10+15=25\) notes ✓ and \(50(10)+100(15)=500+1500=2000\) ✓

    (v)

    Let \(x\) = the fixed charge for the first three days (in ₹) and \(y\) = the charge for each extra day (in ₹).

    A book kept for \(n\) days \((n\gt3)\) costs \(x+(n-3)y\).

    Saritha kept the book 7 days, so she paid for \(7-3=4\) extra days:

    $$x+4y=27 \qquad\ldots(1)$$

    Susy kept the book 5 days, so she paid for \(5-3=2\) extra days:

    $$x+2y=21 \qquad\ldots(2)$$

    The \(x\) terms are equal, so subtract (2) from (1):

    $$\begin{aligned}(x-x)+(4y-2y)&=27-21\\ 2y&=6\\ y&=3\end{aligned}$$

    Put \(y=3\) in (2): \(x+6=21\), so \(x=15\).

    The fixed charge for the first three days is ₹ 15 and each extra day costs ₹ 3.

    Check: seven days cost \(15+4(3)=15+12=27\) ✓ and five days cost \(15+2(3)=15+6=21\) ✓

    All five parts here have a solution, but the question says “if they exist” for a reason. If the variables cancel and you are left with a true statement such as \(0=0\), write “infinitely many solutions”; if you are left with a false statement such as \(0=6\), write “no solution — the pair is inconsistent”. Both of those are complete, full-mark answers.

    150 adult tickets (and \(y=320-150=170\) child tickets)

    Pair of Linear Equations

    What it is

    • \(a_1x+b_1y+c_1=0\) and \(a_2x+b_2y+c_2=0\)
    • \(a,b\) not both zero in either equation
    • A solution is a point \((x,y)\) lying on both lines

    Graphical method

    • Two points per line — take \(x=0\) and \(y=0\)
    • Intersecting → one solution
    • Coincident → infinitely many · Parallel → none

    The ratio test

    • \(\dfrac{a_1}{a_2}\ne\dfrac{b_1}{b_2}\) → unique, consistent
    • \(\dfrac{a_1}{a_2}=\dfrac{b_1}{b_2}=\dfrac{c_1}{c_2}\) → infinitely many, dependent
    • \(\dfrac{a_1}{a_2}=\dfrac{b_1}{b_2}\ne\dfrac{c_1}{c_2}\) → none, inconsistent

    Substitution

    • Make \(x\) (or \(y\)) the subject of the simpler equation
    • Put it into the other equation
    • Back-substitute for the second variable

    Elimination

    • Multiply to make one pair of coefficients equal
    • Add or subtract to kill that variable
    • True statement like \(18=18\) → infinitely many; false like \(0=9\) → none

    Word problems

    • Name the two unknowns first, with units
    • Two facts → two equations
    • Always check both conditions at the end

    Formulas and facts to remember

    • Standard form\(a_1x+b_1y+c_1=0,\ \ a_2x+b_2y+c_2=0\). Bring every term to the left before comparing ratios.
    • Unique solution\(\dfrac{a_1}{a_2}\ne\dfrac{b_1}{b_2}\) — lines intersect, pair is consistent.
    • Infinitely many\(\dfrac{a_1}{a_2}=\dfrac{b_1}{b_2}=\dfrac{c_1}{c_2}\) — lines coincide, pair is dependent (and consistent).
    • No solution\(\dfrac{a_1}{a_2}=\dfrac{b_1}{b_2}\ne\dfrac{c_1}{c_2}\) — lines are parallel, pair is inconsistent.
    • Two-digit numbernumber \(=10x+y\); reversed \(=10y+x\); their sum \(=11(x+y)\), their difference \(=9(x-y)\).
    • Anglessupplementary: \(x+y=180\); complementary: \(x+y=90\); cyclic quadrilateral: opposite angles add to \(180^\circ\).
    • Fixed + running chargetotal \(=\) fixed charge \(+\) (number of units \(\times\) rate). Count only the chargeable units.
    • Triangle with an axiswith the \(x\)-axis: base \(=\) distance between the two \(x\)-intercepts, height \(=y\) of the meeting point. With the \(y\)-axis it is the other way round. Area \(=\frac12\times\)base\(\times\)height.
    • Two vehicles, distance \(d\)same direction: \(t(u-v)=d\); towards each other: \(t(u+v)=d\).
    • Ratios givenincomes \(7:5\) → take \(7x\) and \(5x\); a second ratio needs a different letter, \(9y\) and \(5y\).

    The three cases at a glance

    Two straight lines in a plane can do only three things — meet once, lie on top of each other, or never meet. That is why a pair of linear equations has either exactly one solution, or infinitely many, or none. It can never have exactly two solutions.

    Which case? — decide in ten seconds

    Sign trap: in \(x+2y-3=0\) the constant is \(c_1=-3\), not 3. Move everything to the left first.

    Which method when

    The question says…Use
    “solve graphically”, “draw the graphs”, “shade the triangle”Graphical method — two points per line, on graph paper
    one variable already has coefficient \(\pm1\) (e.g. \(x-y=1\))Substitution
    both equations have awkward coefficients (e.g. \(4x+6y\), \(3x+5y\))Elimination
    decimals or fractions in the coefficientsMultiply out first, then eliminate
    “find \(k\)”, “for what value of \(a\) and \(b\)”, “how many solutions”Ratio test — never solve the pair

    Mistakes that cost marks

    • Comparing ratios straight from \(2x+3y=7\).First write \(2x+3y-7=0\); then \(c_1=-7\).
    • Stopping at \(\frac{a_1}{a_2}=\frac{b_1}{b_2}\) and writing “no solution”.Equal \(a\) and \(b\) ratios can also mean infinitely many. Always test \(\frac{c_1}{c_2}\) too.
    • Reading the graph as \((y,x)\) — writing \((2,4)\) for the point 4 right and 2 up.The \(x\)-coordinate is always written first: \((4,2)\).
    • In the library / hostel type, using the total number of days.Only the days beyond the fixed period are charged extra: 7 days means 4 extra days when the fixed charge covers 3.
    • Forgetting the \(\frac12\) in \(\frac12\times\)base\(\times\)height, or using the wrong coordinate as the height.For the \(x\)-axis triangle the height is the \(y\)-coordinate of the intersection; for the \(y\)-axis triangle it is the \(x\)-coordinate.
    • Taking only one case when “the digits differ by 3”.Both \(x-y=3\) and \(y-x=3\) must be tried — there are usually two answers.
    • Writing \(x+y=90\) for supplementary angles.Supplementary adds to \(180^\circ\); complementary adds to \(90^\circ\).
    • Ending at “\(x=24,\ y=18\)”.Say what they are: “length 24 m, breadth 18 m”. Units and names carry marks.
