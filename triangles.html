    6.1 Introduction

    In Class IX you studied congruent figures — figures with the same shape and the same size. This chapter drops one of the two conditions. Figures that have the same shape, but not necessarily the same size, are called similar figures.

    A passport photo and its poster-size print, a street map and the layout of the streets it shows, a scale model of a building and the building itself — each pair is similar. One is simply an enlargement of the other.

    Why should you care? Because similarity lets you measure what you cannot reach. Nobody climbed Mount Everest with a measuring tape, and nobody stretched a tape to the Moon. Such heights and distances are found by indirect measurement: measure a small triangle that you can reach, and scale it up to the big triangle of the same shape. You will do this yourself in Example 7 (the length of a shadow), in Question 15 of Exercise 6.3 (the height of a tower) and again in Chapters 8 and 9.

    Four facts from earlier classes are used again and again in this chapter.

    1. Congruence. \(\triangle ABC\cong\triangle PQR\) by SAS, ASA, SSS or RHS; then the corresponding parts are equal (CPCT).

    2. Parallel lines. When a transversal cuts two parallel lines, corresponding angles are equal and alternate interior angles are equal. Conversely, if a pair of corresponding (or alternate) angles is equal, the lines are parallel.

    3. Area. Area of a triangle \(=\frac12\times\text{base}\times\text{height}\). Two triangles on the same base and between the same parallels have equal areas.

    4. Triangles. The three angles add up to \(180^\circ\). Sides opposite equal angles are equal, and angles opposite equal sides are equal.

    True or false? (a) Any two congruent figures are similar. (b) Any two similar figures are congruent.

    Show answer

    (a) True — they have the same shape (and, in addition, the same size). (b) False — a photo and its enlargement are similar, but their sizes differ, so they are not congruent.

    6.2 Similar figures

    “Same shape” is easy to see but has to be said precisely before we can prove anything with it. This section turns the picture into a definition.

    Take any two circles. If their radii are different they are not congruent, yet nobody would say that they have different shapes. The same is true of any two squares, and of any two equilateral triangles.

    All congruent figures are similar, but similar figures need not be congruent. Congruent figures are just similar figures whose sizes also happen to be equal.

    A circle and a square can never be similar, and neither can a triangle and a square — their shapes are different.

    What an enlargement really does

    A photographer prints the same picture in stamp size, passport size and postcard size. Suppose the picture is 35 mm wide on the film and is printed 45 mm wide. Then every line segment of the picture becomes \(\dfrac{45}{35}\) times as long. We say the picture is enlarged in the ratio \(35:45\). Printed 55 mm wide, every segment becomes \(\dfrac{55}{35}\) times as long.

    So an enlargement (or a reduction) does two things: it multiplies all lengths by one number and it keeps all angles unchanged. That is exactly what “same shape” means.

    On the other hand, two photographs of the same size — one of a person at the age of 10 and one of the same person at 40 — are not similar. The sizes agree, but the shapes do not.

    Definition — similar polygons

    Two polygons of the same number of sides are similar if

    (i) their corresponding angles are equal, and

    (ii) their corresponding sides are in the same ratio (that is, in proportion).

    The common ratio of the corresponding sides is called the scale factor (or representative fraction). Maps of the world and blueprints of buildings are drawn with a fixed scale factor.

    An activity with a bulb

    Fix a lighted bulb at a point \(O\) on the ceiling and put a table directly under it. Cut a quadrilateral \(ABCD\) out of cardboard and hold it between the bulb and the table, parallel to the table top. Its shadow \(A'B'C'D'\) falls on the table.

    The shadow has the same shape as the card but is bigger: quadrilateral \(A'B'C'D'\) is similar to quadrilateral \(ABCD\). The vertices correspond in order, \(A'\leftrightarrow A,\ B'\leftrightarrow B,\ C'\leftrightarrow C,\ D'\leftrightarrow D\). If you measure, you will find

    $$\angle A=\angle A',\quad \angle B=\angle B',$$ $$\angle C=\angle C',\quad \angle D=\angle D'$$ $$\text{and}\quad \frac{AB}{A'B'}=\frac{BC}{B'C'}=\frac{CD}{C'D'}=\frac{DA}{D'A'}.$$

    Checking two quadrilaterals

    In the figure the corresponding angles are equal. Now compare the corresponding sides, in the same order:

    $$\frac{AB}{PQ}=\frac{2}{4},\qquad \frac{BC}{QR}=\frac{1.5}{3},$$ $$\frac{CD}{RS}=\frac{2.5}{5},\qquad \frac{DA}{SP}=\frac{3}{6}.$$

    Every ratio equals \(\frac12\). Both conditions hold, so quadrilateral \(ABCD\) is similar to quadrilateral \(PQRS\), with scale factor \(\frac12\).

    Similarity passes along a chain: if one polygon is similar to a second polygon, and the second is similar to a third, then the first polygon is similar to the third.

    One condition alone is not enough

    Only the angles agree

    Every angle of the square and of the rectangle is \(90^\circ\). But \(\dfrac{AB}{PQ}=\dfrac24=\dfrac12\) while \(\dfrac{BC}{QR}=\dfrac22=1\). The sides are not in the same ratio.

    Not similar.

    Only the sides agree

    Each side of the square is 2 cm and each side of the rhombus is 3 cm, so every ratio is \(\dfrac23\). But the angles of the square are \(90^\circ\) and those of the rhombus are \(60^\circ\) and \(120^\circ\).

    Not similar.

    So for polygons in general, both conditions must be checked. Triangles are special — for them one condition forces the other, as you will see in Section 6.4.

    “All rectangles are similar”, “all rhombuses are similar”, “all isosceles triangles are similar” — each of these statements is false. What is true: all circles, all squares and all equilateral triangles are similar (in fact, all regular polygons with the same number of sides are similar).

    This section is tested with one-mark questions: fill in the blank, true or false, “which of these pairs is always similar?”. Two facts answer almost all of them: both conditions are needed for polygons, and only circles, squares and equilateral triangles (regular shapes) are always similar to others of their kind.

    One rectangle is 6 cm by 4 cm. Is it similar to a rectangle 9 cm by 6 cm? To a rectangle 8 cm by 6 cm?

    Show answer

    All angles are \(90^\circ\) in every rectangle, so only the sides decide. First pair: \(\frac69=\frac23\) and \(\frac46=\frac23\) — same ratio, so they are similar. Second pair: \(\frac68=\frac34\) but \(\frac46=\frac23\) — different ratios, so they are not similar.

    A map is drawn to the scale \(1:50\,000\). Two villages are 3 cm apart on the map. How far apart are they on the ground?

    Show answer

    Every length on the ground is \(50\,000\) times the length on the map: \(3\times50\,000=150\,000\) cm \(=1.5\) km.

    6.3 Similarity of triangles

    A triangle is a polygon with three sides, so the same definition applies. The big result of this section — the Basic Proportionality Theorem — is the tool with which everything else in the chapter is proved, and its proof is asked in the board exam.

    Definition — similar triangles

    Two triangles are similar if

    (i) their corresponding angles are equal, and

    (ii) their corresponding sides are in the same ratio (or proportion).

    Two triangles whose corresponding angles are equal are called equiangular triangles. The Greek mathematician Thales (about 640–546 B.C.) discovered an important fact about them: the ratio of any two corresponding sides in two equiangular triangles is always the same. He is believed to have used the result that we now call the Basic Proportionality Theorem (BPT), also known as the Thales theorem.

    Discover it yourself

    Draw any angle \(XAY\). On the arm \(AX\) mark five points \(P,\,Q,\,D,\,R,\,B\) at equal steps, so that \(AP=PQ=QD=DR=RB\). Through \(B\) draw any line to meet the arm \(AY\) at \(C\). Now through \(D\) draw a line parallel to \(BC\); let it meet \(AC\) at \(E\).

    So in \(\triangle ABC\), the line \(DE\) parallel to \(BC\) has cut the two sides \(AB\) and \(AC\) in the same ratio: \(\dfrac{AD}{DB}=\dfrac{AE}{EC}=\dfrac32\). This is not a coincidence. It always happens, and here is why.

    The idea of the proof in three pictures

    The proof needs nothing more than the formula area of a triangle = ½ × base × height. Join \(BE\) and \(CD\), and look at three pairs of triangles.

    The first two ratios have the same numerator, ar\((ADE)\), and — by the third picture — equal denominators. So the two ratios are equal. That is the whole proof; now write it the way the examiner wants it.

    Theorem 6.1Basic Proportionality Theorem (Thales theorem)

    If a line is drawn parallel to one side of a triangle to intersect the other two sides in distinct points, the other two sides are divided in the same ratio.

    Proof (asked in the board exam — learn it)

    Given: A triangle \(ABC\) in which a line parallel to side \(BC\) intersects the other two sides \(AB\) and \(AC\) at \(D\) and \(E\) respectively.

    To prove: \(\dfrac{AD}{DB}=\dfrac{AE}{EC}\)

    Construction: Join \(BE\) and \(CD\). Draw \(DM\perp AC\) and \(EN\perp AB\).

    Proof: We write ar\((ADE)\) for the area of \(\triangle ADE\). Since \(EN\perp AB\), \(EN\) is the height of \(\triangle ADE\) on the base \(AD\), and also the height of \(\triangle BDE\) on the base \(DB\).

    $$\text{ar}(ADE)=\tfrac12\times AD\times EN$$ $$\text{ar}(BDE)=\tfrac12\times DB\times EN$$ $$\def\arraystretch{2.5}\begin{aligned}\therefore\ \ \frac{\text{ar}(ADE)}{\text{ar}(BDE)}&=\frac{\frac12\times AD\times EN}{\frac12\times DB\times EN}\\ &=\frac{AD}{DB}\qquad\ldots(1)\end{aligned}$$

    Similarly, \(DM\perp AC\), so \(DM\) is the height of \(\triangle ADE\) on the base \(AE\) and of \(\triangle DEC\) on the base \(EC\).

    $$\text{ar}(ADE)=\tfrac12\times AE\times DM$$ $$\text{ar}(DEC)=\tfrac12\times EC\times DM$$ $$\def\arraystretch{2.5}\begin{aligned}\therefore\ \ \frac{\text{ar}(ADE)}{\text{ar}(DEC)}&=\frac{\frac12\times AE\times DM}{\frac12\times EC\times DM}\\ &=\frac{AE}{EC}\qquad\ldots(2)\end{aligned}$$

    Now \(\triangle BDE\) and \(\triangle DEC\) are on the same base \(DE\) and between the same parallels \(BC\) and \(DE\).

    $$\therefore\ \ \text{ar}(BDE)=\text{ar}(DEC)\quad\ldots(3)$$

    From (1), (2) and (3),

    $$\frac{AD}{DB}=\frac{AE}{EC}.$$ Proved.

    “State and prove the Basic Proportionality Theorem” is one of the most repeated long-answer questions from this chapter. Marks are given for: the figure with the construction, the lines Given / To prove / Construction, the two area ratios (1) and (2), the reason “same base \(DE\) and between the same parallels” for (3), and the conclusion. Very often the second half of the same question asks you to use the theorem in a numerical problem — so do not spend all your time on the proof.

    Join (\(BE,\ CD\)) → Drop (the perpendiculars \(EN,\ DM\)) → Ratio on \(AB\) (common height \(EN\)) → Ratio on \(AC\) (common height \(DM\)) → Same base, same parallels (equal areas). Say it as “Join, Drop, Ratio, Ratio, Same”.

    NCERT Example 1a second form of BPT

    A line cuts the sides \(AB\) and \(AC\) of \(\triangle ABC\) at \(D\) and \(E\) respectively and is parallel to \(BC\). Prove that \(\dfrac{AD}{AB}=\dfrac{AE}{AC}\).

    Solution

    \(DE\parallel BC\) [given]

    $$\therefore\ \ \frac{AD}{DB}=\frac{AE}{EC}\quad\text{[Theorem 6.1]}$$

    Taking reciprocals on both sides,

    $$\frac{DB}{AD}=\frac{EC}{AE}$$

    Adding 1 to both sides,

    $$\def\arraystretch{2.5}\begin{aligned}\frac{DB}{AD}+1&=\frac{EC}{AE}+1\\ \frac{DB+AD}{AD}&=\frac{EC+AE}{AE}\\ \frac{AB}{AD}&=\frac{AC}{AE}\end{aligned}$$

    Taking reciprocals again,

    $$\frac{AD}{AB}=\frac{AE}{AC}.$$ Hence proved.
    Three forms of the Basic Proportionality Theorem
    $$\def\arraystretch{2.5}\begin{aligned}&\text{upper : lower}&\quad\frac{AD}{DB}&=\frac{AE}{EC}\\ &\text{upper : whole}&\quad\frac{AD}{AB}&=\frac{AE}{AC}\\ &\text{lower : whole}&\quad\frac{DB}{AB}&=\frac{EC}{AC}\end{aligned}$$

    Valid whenever \(DE\parallel BC\) with \(D\) on \(AB\) and \(E\) on \(AC\). Always put the same kind of piece on both sides. The first is the theorem, the second is Example 1, and the third follows by subtracting each side of the second from 1. In an exam you may use the second and third forms directly, giving “BPT” (or “corollary of BPT”) as the reason.

    BPT compares pieces of the two sides only. It says nothing about \(DE\) and \(BC\).

    $$\text{Wrong:}\quad\frac{AD}{DB}=\frac{DE}{BC}$$ $$\text{Right:}\quad\frac{AD}{AB}=\frac{DE}{BC}$$

    The correct relation comes from the similar triangles \(ADE\) and \(ABC\) (Section 6.4), not from BPT.

    Is the converse true?

    Draw an angle \(XAY\) again. On \(AX\) mark \(B_1,B_2,B_3,B_4,B\) at equal steps, and on \(AY\) mark \(C_1,C_2,C_3,C_4,C\) at equal steps (the steps on the two arms need not be equal to each other). Join \(B_1C_1\) and \(BC\).

    $$\frac{AB_1}{B_1B}=\frac{AC_1}{C_1C}=\frac14\quad\text{and}\quad B_1C_1\parallel BC$$ $$\frac{AB_2}{B_2B}=\frac{AC_2}{C_2C}=\frac23\quad\text{and}\quad B_2C_2\parallel BC$$

    and so on. Each time a line divides the two sides in the same ratio, it turns out to be parallel to the third side. This is the converse of Theorem 6.1.

    Theorem 6.2Converse of the Basic Proportionality Theorem

    If a line divides any two sides of a triangle in the same ratio, then the line is parallel to the third side.

    Proof (for understanding — not asked in the exam)

    Given: In \(\triangle ABC\), a line \(DE\) meets \(AB\) at \(D\) and \(AC\) at \(E\) such that \(\dfrac{AD}{DB}=\dfrac{AE}{EC}\). To prove: \(DE\parallel BC\).

    Proof: Suppose, if possible, that \(DE\) is not parallel to \(BC\). Through \(D\) draw the line \(DE'\) parallel to \(BC\), meeting \(AC\) at \(E'\). Since \(DE'\parallel BC\), Theorem 6.1 gives

    $$\frac{AD}{DB}=\frac{AE'}{E'C}.$$

    But it is given that \(\dfrac{AD}{DB}=\dfrac{AE}{EC}\). Therefore

    $$\frac{AE}{EC}=\frac{AE'}{E'C}.$$

    Adding 1 to both sides,

    $$\frac{AE+EC}{EC}=\frac{AE'+E'C}{E'C}$$ $$\text{that is,}\quad \frac{AC}{EC}=\frac{AC}{E'C}.$$

    So \(EC=E'C\).

    \(E\) and \(E'\) are points of the same side \(AC\) at the same distance from \(C\), so they are the same point. Hence the line \(DE\) is the line \(DE'\), and \(DE\parallel BC\). Proved.

    Theorem 6.1 — BPT

    You know: the line is parallel to a side.

    You get: equal ratios, \(\dfrac{AD}{DB}=\dfrac{AE}{EC}\).

    Use it to find lengths or to prove that two ratios are equal.

    Theorem 6.2 — converse of BPT

    You know: the ratios are equal.

    You get: the line is parallel to the third side.

    Use it to prove that two lines are parallel.

    NCERT Example 2BPT in a trapezium

    \(ABCD\) is a trapezium with \(AB\parallel DC\). Points \(E\) and \(F\) lie on the non-parallel sides \(AD\) and \(BC\) respectively, and \(EF\) is parallel to \(AB\). Show that \(\dfrac{AE}{ED}=\dfrac{BF}{FC}\).

    Solution

    BPT works inside a triangle, and a trapezium is not a triangle. So we split it into two triangles with a diagonal.

    Construction: Join \(AC\). Let it meet \(EF\) at \(G\).

    \(AB\parallel DC\) and \(EF\parallel AB\) [given]

    \(\therefore\ EF\parallel DC\) [lines parallel to the same line are parallel to each other]

    In \(\triangle ADC\), \(EG\parallel DC\) [as \(EF\parallel DC\)]

    $$\therefore\ \ \frac{AE}{ED}=\frac{AG}{GC}\quad\text{[BPT]}\quad\ldots(1)$$

    In \(\triangle CAB\), \(GF\parallel AB\) [as \(EF\parallel AB\)]

    $$\therefore\ \ \frac{CG}{AG}=\frac{CF}{BF}\quad\text{[BPT]}$$ $$\text{that is,}\quad \frac{AG}{GC}=\frac{BF}{FC}\quad\ldots(2)$$

    From (1) and (2),

    $$\frac{AE}{ED}=\frac{BF}{FC}.$$ Hence proved.
    NCERT Example 3converse of BPT

    In the figure, \(\dfrac{PS}{SQ}=\dfrac{PT}{TR}\) and \(\angle PST=\angle PRQ\). Prove that \(PQR\) is an isosceles triangle.

    Solution
    $$\frac{PS}{SQ}=\frac{PT}{TR}\quad\text{[given]}$$

    \(\therefore\ ST\parallel QR\) [Theorem 6.2, converse of BPT]

    \(\therefore\ \angle PST=\angle PQR\) [corresponding angles, \(ST\parallel QR\)]  …(1)

    Also \(\angle PST=\angle PRQ\) [given]  …(2)

    From (1) and (2), \(\angle PRQ=\angle PQR\).

    \(\therefore\ PQ=PR\) [sides opposite equal angles of a triangle are equal]

    So \(PQR\) is an isosceles triangle. Hence proved.

    How to attack any BPT question. (1) Find the pair of parallel lines. (2) Name the triangle whose two sides are cut by the parallel line — say it aloud: “In \(\triangle ADC\), \(EG\parallel DC\)”. (3) Write the ratio, with the reason “BPT”. (4) If two triangles share a side or a ratio (like \(\frac{AG}{GC}\) in Example 2), write one ratio from each and link them. (5) If you must prove that lines are parallel, aim for equal ratios and finish with the converse.

    In \(\triangle ABC\), \(DE\parallel BC\) with \(D\) on \(AB\) and \(E\) on \(AC\). If \(AD=2\) cm, \(DB=3\) cm and \(AC=10\) cm, find \(AE\).

    Show answer

    \(AB=AD+DB=5\) cm. By BPT (second form), \(\dfrac{AE}{AC}=\dfrac{AD}{AB}=\dfrac25\), so \(AE=\dfrac25\times10=4\) cm.

    In \(\triangle ABC\), \(DE\parallel BC\). If \(AD=x\), \(DB=x-2\), \(AE=x+2\) and \(EC=x-1\), find \(x\).

    Show answer

    By BPT, \(\dfrac{x}{x-2}=\dfrac{x+2}{x-1}\). Cross-multiplying, \(x(x-1)=(x+2)(x-2)\), that is \(x^2-x=x^2-4\), so \(x=4\). Check: \(\frac42=\frac63=2\). ✓

    6.4 Criteria for similarity of triangles

    By the definition, two triangles are similar when three pairs of angles are equal and three ratios of sides are equal — six conditions in all. For congruence you never needed all six parts; three well-chosen parts were enough. The same is true for similarity, and the three short tests are called AA, SSS and SAS.

    The symbol ∼ and the order of the letters

    In \(\triangle ABC\) and \(\triangle DEF\), suppose

    $$\angle A=\angle D,\quad \angle B=\angle E,\quad \angle C=\angle F$$ $$\text{and}\qquad \frac{AB}{DE}=\frac{BC}{EF}=\frac{CA}{FD}.$$

    Then the triangles are similar and we write \(\triangle ABC\sim\triangle DEF\), read as “triangle \(ABC\) is similar to triangle \(DEF\)”. The symbol \(\sim\) means “is similar to”, just as \(\cong\) means “is congruent to”. Here \(A\) corresponds to \(D\), \(B\) to \(E\) and \(C\) to \(F\).

    The order of the letters is part of the statement. For the triangles above you cannot write \(\triangle ABC\sim\triangle EDF\) or \(\triangle ABC\sim\triangle FED\). You may write \(\triangle BAC\sim\triangle EDF\), because the matching is still \(A\leftrightarrow D,\ B\leftrightarrow E,\ C\leftrightarrow F\). A wrong order gives wrong ratios in the next line, and the marks for that step are lost.

    Reading the ratios straight from the statement. Write the two names in the given order and pick letters from the same positions:

    $$\triangle\, A\,B\,C\ \sim\ \triangle\, D\,E\,F$$ $$\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{AB}{DE}=\frac{BC}{EF}=\frac{AC}{DF}$$

    1st–2nd letters over 1st–2nd letters, 2nd–3rd over 2nd–3rd, 1st–3rd over 1st–3rd. You do not even need the figure.

    Activity — when the angles are equal

    Draw \(BC=3\) cm and \(EF=5\) cm. At \(B\) and \(C\) construct angles of \(60^\circ\) and \(40^\circ\); let the two rays (\(BP\) and \(CQ\)) meet at \(A\). Do the same at \(E\) and \(F\); let the rays (\(ER\) and \(FS\)) meet at \(D\).

    By construction \(\angle B=\angle E=60^\circ\) and \(\angle C=\angle F=40^\circ\), so \(\angle A=\angle D=80^\circ\) as well. The corresponding angles are equal. What about the sides? Clearly

    $$\frac{BC}{EF}=\frac35=0.6.$$

    Now measure \(AB,\ DE,\ CA\) and \(FD\) and work out \(\frac{AB}{DE}\) and \(\frac{CA}{FD}\). Each of them is also 0.6 (or very nearly 0.6, if the measurement has a small error). Equal angles have forced the sides into the same ratio.

    Theorem 6.3AAA (Angle–Angle–Angle) similarity criterion

    If in two triangles, corresponding angles are equal, then their corresponding sides are in the same ratio (or proportion) and hence the two triangles are similar.

    Proof (for understanding — not asked in the exam)

    Given: \(\triangle ABC\) and \(\triangle DEF\) with \(\angle A=\angle D,\ \angle B=\angle E,\ \angle C=\angle F\).

    To prove: \(\dfrac{AB}{DE}=\dfrac{BC}{EF}=\dfrac{AC}{DF}\)

    Construction: (Take \(AB\lt DE\).) Cut off \(DP=AB\) on \(DE\) and \(DQ=AC\) on \(DF\). Join \(PQ\).

    Proof: In \(\triangle ABC\) and \(\triangle DPQ\): \(AB=DP\), \(\angle A=\angle D\), \(AC=DQ\). So \(\triangle ABC\cong\triangle DPQ\) [SAS congruence], and therefore \(\angle B=\angle P\) [CPCT].

    But \(\angle B=\angle E\) [given], so \(\angle P=\angle E\). These are corresponding angles made by the transversal \(DE\) with the lines \(PQ\) and \(EF\). Hence \(PQ\parallel EF\).

    $$\therefore\ \ \frac{DP}{PE}=\frac{DQ}{QF}\quad\text{[Theorem 6.1]}$$

    and so, exactly as in Example 1,

    $$\frac{DP}{DE}=\frac{DQ}{DF},\quad\text{that is,}\quad \frac{AB}{DE}=\frac{AC}{DF}.$$

    Repeat the construction at the vertex \(E\): cut off lengths equal to \(BA\) and \(BC\) along \(ED\) and \(EF\). The same argument gives

    $$\frac{AB}{DE}=\frac{BC}{EF}.$$ $$\text{Therefore}\quad \frac{AB}{DE}=\frac{BC}{EF}=\frac{AC}{DF}.$$

    If \(AB=DE\), the triangles are congruent by ASA and each ratio is 1. If \(AB\gt DE\), interchange the roles of the two triangles. Proved.

    AA similarity criterion — the one you will use most

    If two angles of one triangle are respectively equal to two angles of another triangle, then the two triangles are similar.

    Reason: the angles of a triangle add up to \(180^\circ\). If two pairs of angles are equal, the third pair is automatically equal, and AAA applies. So two angles are enough.

    Activity — when the sides are proportional

    Now the other way round. Draw \(\triangle ABC\) with \(AB=3\) cm, \(BC=6\) cm, \(CA=8\) cm and \(\triangle DEF\) with \(DE=4.5\) cm, \(EF=9\) cm, \(FD=12\) cm.

    $$\def\arraystretch{2.5}\begin{aligned}\frac{AB}{DE}&=\frac{3}{4.5}=\frac23\\ \frac{BC}{EF}&=\frac69=\frac23\\ \frac{CA}{FD}&=\frac{8}{12}=\frac23\end{aligned}$$

    The sides are in the same ratio. Measure the angles with a protractor: you will find \(\angle A=\angle D\), \(\angle B=\angle E\) and \(\angle C=\angle F\). Proportional sides have forced the angles to be equal.

    Theorem 6.4SSS (Side–Side–Side) similarity criterion

    If in two triangles, sides of one triangle are proportional to (i.e., in the same ratio of) the sides of the other triangle, then their corresponding angles are equal and hence the two triangles are similar.

    Proof (for understanding — not asked in the exam)

    Given: \(\triangle ABC\) and \(\triangle DEF\) with

    $$\frac{AB}{DE}=\frac{BC}{EF}=\frac{CA}{FD}\quad(\lt1).$$

    To prove: \(\angle A=\angle D,\ \angle B=\angle E,\ \angle C=\angle F\).

    Construction: Cut off \(DP=AB\) on \(DE\) and \(DQ=AC\) on \(DF\). Join \(PQ\).

    Proof: Put \(DP\) for \(AB\) and \(DQ\) for \(AC\) in the given relation \(\frac{AB}{DE}=\frac{AC}{DF}\):

    $$\frac{DP}{DE}=\frac{DQ}{DF}$$

    Take reciprocals and subtract 1 from both sides:

    $$\frac{DE}{DP}-1=\frac{DF}{DQ}-1$$ $$\text{that is,}\quad \frac{PE}{DP}=\frac{QF}{DQ}$$ $$\text{or}\quad \frac{DP}{PE}=\frac{DQ}{QF}.$$

    So the line \(PQ\) divides \(DE\) and \(DF\) in the same ratio, and therefore \(PQ\parallel EF\) [Theorem 6.2].

    Hence \(\angle P=\angle E\) and \(\angle Q=\angle F\) [corresponding angles]. So \(\triangle DPQ\sim\triangle DEF\) [AA], which gives

    $$\frac{DP}{DE}=\frac{PQ}{EF}.$$

    But \(DP=AB\), and it is given that \(\frac{AB}{DE}=\frac{BC}{EF}\). So

    $$\frac{PQ}{EF}=\frac{BC}{EF},\quad\text{that is,}\quad PQ=BC.$$

    Now \(AB=DP\), \(AC=DQ\) and \(BC=PQ\), so \(\triangle ABC\cong\triangle DPQ\) [SSS congruence]. Therefore \(\angle A=\angle D\), \(\angle B=\angle P=\angle E\) and \(\angle C=\angle Q=\angle F\). Proved.

    For polygons with four or more sides, “equal angles” and “proportional sides” are independent conditions — remember the square with the rectangle and the square with the rhombus. For triangles, each condition implies the other (Theorems 6.3 and 6.4). So for triangles you need to check only one of them.

    Activity — two sides and the angle between them

    SSS similarity looks like SSS congruence. Is there a similarity test that looks like SAS congruence? Draw \(\triangle ABC\) with \(AB=2\) cm, \(\angle A=50^\circ\), \(AC=4\) cm and \(\triangle DEF\) with \(DE=3\) cm, \(\angle D=50^\circ\), \(DF=6\) cm.

    $$\frac{AB}{DE}=\frac23\quad\text{and}\quad \frac{AC}{DF}=\frac46=\frac23$$

    Two pairs of sides are in the same ratio, and the angle included between them is the same: \(\angle A=\angle D=50^\circ\). On measuring you will find \(\angle B=\angle E\) and \(\angle C=\angle F\) too. So \(\triangle ABC\sim\triangle DEF\) by AAA.

    Theorem 6.5SAS (Side–Angle–Side) similarity criterion

    If one angle of a triangle is equal to one angle of the other triangle and the sides including these angles are proportional, then the two triangles are similar.

    Proof (for understanding — not asked in the exam)

    Given: \(\triangle ABC\) and \(\triangle DEF\) with \(\angle A=\angle D\) and

    $$\frac{AB}{DE}=\frac{AC}{DF}\quad(\lt1).$$

    To prove: \(\triangle ABC\sim\triangle DEF\).

    Construction: Cut off \(DP=AB\) on \(DE\) and \(DQ=AC\) on \(DF\). Join \(PQ\).

    Proof: In \(\triangle ABC\) and \(\triangle DPQ\): \(AB=DP\), \(\angle A=\angle D\), \(AC=DQ\). So \(\triangle ABC\cong\triangle DPQ\) [SAS congruence], which gives \(\angle B=\angle P\) and \(\angle C=\angle Q\) [CPCT].

    Also, putting \(DP\) for \(AB\) and \(DQ\) for \(AC\) in the given relation,

    $$\frac{DP}{DE}=\frac{DQ}{DF}$$ $$\text{which gives}\quad \frac{DP}{PE}=\frac{DQ}{QF}$$

    (take reciprocals, subtract 1, take reciprocals again). So \(PQ\parallel EF\) [Theorem 6.2], and hence \(\angle P=\angle E\) and \(\angle Q=\angle F\) [corresponding angles].

    Therefore \(\angle A=\angle D\), \(\angle B=\angle E\) and \(\angle C=\angle F\), and so \(\triangle ABC\sim\triangle DEF\) [AAA]. Proved.

    The three criteria at a glance

    AA (or AAA)

    Two angles of one triangle equal two angles of the other.

    Look for: a common angle, vertically opposite angles, right angles, parallel lines (alternate or corresponding angles).

    SSS

    All three pairs of sides are in the same ratio.

    Look for: questions that give only lengths. Arrange the sides of each triangle in increasing order and compare smallest with smallest, and so on.

    SAS

    One pair of equal angles, and the sides containing those angles in the same ratio.

    Look for: a product such as \(OA\cdot OB=OC\cdot OD\), mid-points, medians.

    Congruence (Class IX)Similarity (Class X)What changes
    SSSSSSsides equal → sides proportional
    SASSAStwo sides equal → two sides proportional; included angle equal in both
    ASA, AASAAno side is needed at all
    RHSRHS (see the note at the end of the chapter)hypotenuse and one side proportional

    In SAS the equal angle must be the one between the two proportional sides. Two proportional sides and some other equal angle prove nothing — there is no “SSA” test, for similarity or for congruence.

    Getting the order right. First decide which angles are equal; equal angles sit at corresponding vertices. If \(\angle A=\angle R\), \(\angle B=\angle Q\) and \(\angle C=\angle P\), then \(A\leftrightarrow R\), \(B\leftrightarrow Q\), \(C\leftrightarrow P\) and the statement is \(\triangle ABC\sim\triangle RQP\). When only sides are given, remember that the smallest side faces the smallest angle: match smallest with smallest and largest with largest, then name the vertices opposite to them.

    NCERT Example 4parallel lines give AAA

    In the figure, \(PQ\parallel RS\). Prove that \(\triangle POQ\sim\triangle SOR\).

    Solution

    \(PQ\parallel RS\) [given]

    \(\therefore\ \angle P=\angle S\) [alternate angles, transversal \(PS\)]

    and \(\angle Q=\angle R\) [alternate angles, transversal \(QR\)]

    Also \(\angle POQ=\angle SOR\) [vertically opposite angles]

    \(\therefore\ \triangle POQ\sim\triangle SOR\) [AAA similarity criterion]

    Hence proved.

    Any two of the three pairs of angles would do (AA). Check the order: \(P\leftrightarrow S\), \(O\leftrightarrow O\), \(Q\leftrightarrow R\).

    NCERT Example 5SSS, then angles

    Look at the two triangles and find \(\angle P\).

    Solution

    Only sides are known in \(\triangle PQR\), so try SSS. Match smallest with smallest: \(3.8\leftrightarrow7.6\), \(3\sqrt3\leftrightarrow6\sqrt3\), \(6\leftrightarrow12\).

    In \(\triangle ABC\) and \(\triangle RQP\),

    $$\def\arraystretch{2.5}\begin{aligned}\frac{AB}{RQ}&=\frac{3.8}{7.6}=\frac12\\ \frac{BC}{QP}&=\frac{6}{12}=\frac12\\ \frac{CA}{PR}&=\frac{3\sqrt3}{6\sqrt3}=\frac12\end{aligned}$$ $$\text{That is,}\quad \frac{AB}{RQ}=\frac{BC}{QP}=\frac{CA}{PR}.$$

    \(\therefore\ \triangle ABC\sim\triangle RQP\) [SSS similarity]

    \(\therefore\ \angle C=\angle P\) [corresponding angles of similar triangles]

    But \(\angle C=180^\circ-\angle A-\angle B=180^\circ-80^\circ-60^\circ=40^\circ\) [angle sum property]

    \(\angle P=40^\circ\)

    The order matters: \(A\leftrightarrow R\), \(B\leftrightarrow Q\), \(C\leftrightarrow P\). Writing \(\triangle ABC\sim\triangle PQR\) here would give the wrong angle.

    The lengths in this example are rounded, so an accurate scale drawing gives angles within a degree or two of \(80^\circ\) and \(60^\circ\). In the exam, always work with the data exactly as given.

    NCERT Example 6a product becomes SAS

    In the figure, \(OA\cdot OB=OC\cdot OD\). Show that \(\angle A=\angle C\) and \(\angle B=\angle D\).

    Solution

    \(OA\cdot OB=OC\cdot OD\) [given]

    Dividing both sides by \(OC\cdot OB\),

    $$\frac{OA}{OC}=\frac{OD}{OB}\quad\ldots(1)$$

    Also \(\angle AOD=\angle COB\) [vertically opposite angles]  …(2)

    From (1) and (2), \(\triangle AOD\sim\triangle COB\) [SAS similarity criterion]

    \(\therefore\ \angle A=\angle C\) and \(\angle D=\angle B\) [corresponding angles of similar triangles]

    Hence shown.

    A product of two lengths equal to another product is a disguised proportion: \(ab=cd\) means \(\frac ac=\frac db\). Choose the form that puts the sides of one triangle on top.

    NCERT Example 7shadow of a girl

    A girl 90 cm tall walks away from the base of a lamp-post at a speed of 1.2 m/s. The lamp is fixed 3.6 m above the ground. How long is her shadow after 4 seconds?

    Solution

    Let \(AB\) be the lamp-post and \(CD\) the girl after walking for 4 seconds. Her shadow is \(DE\). Let \(DE=x\) metres.

    Distance walked in 4 seconds: \(BD=1.2\times4=4.8\) m. Height of the girl: \(CD=90\text{ cm}=0.9\) m.

    In \(\triangle ABE\) and \(\triangle CDE\),

    \(\angle B=\angle D\) [each is \(90^\circ\): the lamp-post and the girl both stand vertical to the ground]

    \(\angle E=\angle E\) [common angle]

    \(\therefore\ \triangle ABE\sim\triangle CDE\) [AA similarity criterion]

    So their corresponding sides are proportional:

    $$\frac{BE}{DE}=\frac{AB}{CD}$$ $$\begin{aligned}\frac{4.8+x}{x}&=\frac{3.6}{0.9}=4\\ 4.8+x&=4x\\ 3x&=4.8\\ x&=1.6\end{aligned}$$

    The shadow of the girl after 4 seconds is 1.6 m long.

    Shadow, pole-and-tower and mirror questions are all the same question: two vertical objects, one sun (or lamp), so two right triangles with a common or equal angle — similar by AA. Convert every length to the same unit first (here 90 cm → 0.9 m), and remember that the big triangle's base is the whole distance \(BE=BD+DE\), not just \(BD\).

    NCERT Example 8medians of similar triangles

    In the figure, \(CM\) and \(RN\) are the medians of \(\triangle ABC\) and \(\triangle PQR\) respectively. If \(\triangle ABC\sim\triangle PQR\), prove that:

    1. \(\triangle AMC\sim\triangle PNR\)
    2. \(\dfrac{CM}{RN}=\dfrac{AB}{PQ}\)
    3. \(\triangle CMB\sim\triangle RNQ\)
    Solution
    (i)

    \(\triangle ABC\sim\triangle PQR\) [given]

    $$\frac{AB}{PQ}=\frac{BC}{QR}=\frac{CA}{RP}\ \ \ldots(1)$$

    and \(\angle A=\angle P,\ \angle B=\angle Q,\ \angle C=\angle R\)  …(2)

    But \(AB=2AM\) and \(PQ=2PN\) [\(CM\) and \(RN\) are medians]

    So from (1), \(\dfrac{2AM}{2PN}=\dfrac{CA}{RP}\), that is,

    $$\frac{AM}{PN}=\frac{CA}{RP}\quad\ldots(3)$$

    Also \(\angle MAC=\angle NPR\) [from (2), \(\angle A=\angle P\)]  …(4)

    From (3) and (4), \(\triangle AMC\sim\triangle PNR\) [SAS similarity]  …(5)

    (ii)

    From (5), \(\dfrac{CM}{RN}=\dfrac{CA}{RP}\) [corresponding sides of similar triangles]  …(6)

    But \(\dfrac{CA}{RP}=\dfrac{AB}{PQ}\) [from (1)]  …(7)

    Therefore \(\dfrac{CM}{RN}=\dfrac{AB}{PQ}\) [from (6) and (7)]  …(8)

    (iii)

    \(\dfrac{AB}{PQ}=\dfrac{BC}{QR}\) [from (1)], so by (8), \(\dfrac{CM}{RN}=\dfrac{BC}{QR}\)  …(9)

    Also \(\dfrac{CM}{RN}=\dfrac{AB}{PQ}=\dfrac{2BM}{2QN}\), that is, \(\dfrac{CM}{RN}=\dfrac{BM}{QN}\)  …(10)

    From (9) and (10),

    $$\frac{CM}{RN}=\frac{BC}{QR}=\frac{BM}{QN}$$

    \(\therefore\ \triangle CMB\sim\triangle RNQ\) [SSS similarity]

    Hence proved.

    Part (iii) can also be done like part (i): \(\dfrac{BM}{QN}=\dfrac{BC}{QR}\) and \(\angle B=\angle Q\), so SAS works too.

    Example 8 (ii) is worth remembering as a result: in similar triangles, corresponding medians are in the same ratio as corresponding sides. The same is true of corresponding angle bisectors and corresponding altitudes — you will prove results of this kind in Exercise 6.3 (Questions 10 and 16).

    How a similarity proof is marked. Name the two triangles (“In \(\triangle ABE\) and \(\triangle CDE\) …”), write each equal pair of angles or each ratio on its own line with the reason in brackets, state the criterion (AA / SSS / SAS), and only then use “corresponding sides of similar triangles are proportional”. A correct result with missing reasons does not earn full marks.

    In \(\triangle ABC\) and \(\triangle DEF\): \(\angle A=50^\circ\), \(\angle B=70^\circ\), \(\angle D=50^\circ\), \(\angle F=60^\circ\). Are the triangles similar? Write the statement in symbols.

    Show answer

    \(\angle C=180^\circ-50^\circ-70^\circ=60^\circ\) and \(\angle E=180^\circ-50^\circ-60^\circ=70^\circ\). So \(\angle A=\angle D\), \(\angle B=\angle E\), \(\angle C=\angle F\). Yes: \(\triangle ABC\sim\triangle DEF\) by AA (AAA).

    The sides of one triangle are 4 cm, 6 cm, 8 cm and those of another are 6 cm, 9 cm, 12 cm. Are they similar? What about 4, 6, 8 and 6, 9, 13?

    Show answer

    \(\frac46=\frac69=\frac8{12}=\frac23\): all three ratios are equal, so the triangles are similar by SSS. For the second pair \(\frac46=\frac69=\frac23\) but \(\frac8{13}\ne\frac23\), so they are not similar.

    A pole 2 m high casts a shadow 1.5 m long. At the same time a building casts a shadow 12 m long. How high is the building?

    Show answer

    The sun's rays make the same angle with the ground for both, and both stand at \(90^\circ\) to the ground, so the two right triangles are similar (AA). \(\dfrac{h}{2}=\dfrac{12}{1.5}=8\), so \(h=16\) m.

    6.5 Summary

    The whole chapter in nine lines. If you can state each of these and draw its figure, you are ready for the exercises.

    1. Two figures with the same shape, but not necessarily the same size, are called similar figures.
    2. All congruent figures are similar, but the converse is not true.
    3. Two polygons of the same number of sides are similar if (i) their corresponding angles are equal and (ii) their corresponding sides are in the same ratio (in proportion).
    4. Basic Proportionality Theorem: a line drawn parallel to one side of a triangle, meeting the other two sides in distinct points, divides those two sides in the same ratio.
    5. Converse: a line that divides any two sides of a triangle in the same ratio is parallel to the third side.
    6. AAA: if the corresponding angles of two triangles are equal, their corresponding sides are in the same ratio and the triangles are similar.
    7. AA: if two angles of one triangle are respectively equal to two angles of another, the triangles are similar.
    8. SSS: if the corresponding sides of two triangles are in the same ratio, their corresponding angles are equal and the triangles are similar.
    9. SAS: if one angle of a triangle equals one angle of another and the sides including these angles are in the same ratio, the triangles are similar.

    If in two right triangles, the hypotenuse and one side of one triangle are proportional to the hypotenuse and one side of the other triangle, then the two triangles are similar. This may be called the RHS similarity criterion. It is not needed in this chapter, but it shortens the proof of Example 2 of Chapter 8.

    3 questions · similar figures, the two conditions for similar polygons

    Q1

    Fill in each blank with the correct word from the brackets.

    1. All circles are ______. (congruent, similar)
    2. All squares are ______. (similar, congruent)
    3. All ______ triangles are similar. (isosceles, equilateral)
    4. Two polygons of the same number of sides are similar, if (a) their corresponding angles are ______ and (b) their corresponding sides are ______. (equal, proportional)
    Solution
    (i)

    Every circle has the same shape. Two circles are congruent only when their radii are equal, but they are always similar.

    All circles are similar.

    (ii)

    In any two squares every angle is \(90^\circ\), and since all four sides of a square are equal, the four ratios of corresponding sides are equal. Both conditions hold. The squares are congruent only if their sides are equal.

    All squares are similar.

    (iii)

    In every equilateral triangle each angle is \(60^\circ\), and the three sides are equal, so the three ratios of corresponding sides are equal. Isosceles triangles can have different angles (for example \(30^\circ,75^\circ,75^\circ\) and \(80^\circ,50^\circ,50^\circ\)), so they need not be similar.

    All equilateral triangles are similar.

    (iv)

    This is the definition of similar polygons.

    (a) their corresponding angles are equal and (b) their corresponding sides are proportional.

    (i) similar   (ii) similar   (iii) equilateral   (iv) equal, proportional

    Q2

    Give two different examples of a pair of

    1. similar figures.
    2. non-similar figures.
    Solution
    (i)

    Similar figures (same shape; the size may differ):

    (a) two equilateral triangles with sides 2 cm and 4 cm — all angles are \(60^\circ\) and every ratio of corresponding sides is \(\frac12\);

    (b) two circles with radii 1 cm and 2 cm.

    Other correct pairs: two squares of sides 3 cm and 5 cm; a photograph and its enlargement.

    (ii)

    Non-similar figures (different shapes):

    (a) a square and a rectangle whose sides are unequal — the angles are equal but the sides are not in the same ratio;

    (b) a square and a rhombus that has no right angle — the sides are in the same ratio but the angles are not equal.

    Other correct pairs: a circle and a triangle; an equilateral triangle and a right-angled triangle.

    Any correct examples earn the marks. For polygons, say which of the two conditions holds or fails.

    Q3

    State whether the two quadrilaterals in the figure are similar or not.

    Solution

    Two polygons are similar only if both conditions hold: corresponding angles equal and corresponding sides in the same ratio.

    Sides. Every side of \(PQRS\) is 1.5 cm and every side of \(ABCD\) is 3 cm, and \(\frac{1.5}{3}=\frac12\). So

    $$\frac{PQ}{AB}=\frac{QR}{BC}=\frac{RS}{CD}=\frac{SP}{DA}=\frac12.$$

    The corresponding sides are in the same ratio. ✓

    Angles. Every angle of \(ABCD\) is \(90^\circ\) (it is a square). The angles of \(PQRS\) are not right angles (it is a rhombus that leans to one side). So the corresponding angles are not equal. ✗

    The second condition fails.

    No, the two quadrilaterals are not similar.

    10 questions · Basic Proportionality Theorem and its converse

    Q1

    In figures (i) and (ii), \(DE\parallel BC\). Find \(EC\) in (i) and \(AD\) in (ii).

    Solution
    (i)

    Given: \(DE\parallel BC\), \(AD=1.5\) cm, \(DB=3\) cm, \(AE=1\) cm.

    In \(\triangle ABC\), \(DE\parallel BC\). So, by the Basic Proportionality Theorem,

    $$\frac{AD}{DB}=\frac{AE}{EC}$$ $$\def\arraystretch{2.5}\begin{aligned}\frac{1.5}{3}&=\frac{1}{EC}\\ EC&=\frac{3\times1}{1.5}=2\end{aligned}$$

    \(EC=2\) cm

    (ii)

    Given: \(DE\parallel BC\), \(DB=7.2\) cm, \(AE=1.8\) cm, \(EC=5.4\) cm.

    In \(\triangle ABC\), \(DE\parallel BC\). So, by the Basic Proportionality Theorem,

    $$\frac{AD}{DB}=\frac{AE}{EC}$$ $$\def\arraystretch{2.5}\begin{aligned}\frac{AD}{7.2}&=\frac{1.8}{5.4}=\frac13\\ AD&=\frac{7.2}{3}=2.4\end{aligned}$$

    \(AD=2.4\) cm

    (i) \(EC=2\) cm   (ii) \(AD=2.4\) cm

    Q2

    Points \(E\) and \(F\) lie on the sides \(PQ\) and \(PR\) respectively of \(\triangle PQR\). In each of the following cases, decide whether \(EF\parallel QR\):

    1. \(PE=3.9\) cm, \(EQ=3\) cm, \(PF=3.6\) cm and \(FR=2.4\) cm
    2. \(PE=4\) cm, \(QE=4.5\) cm, \(PF=8\) cm and \(RF=9\) cm
    3. \(PQ=1.28\) cm, \(PR=2.56\) cm, \(PE=0.18\) cm and \(PF=0.36\) cm
    Solution

    By the converse of BPT, \(EF\parallel QR\) if \(\dfrac{PE}{EQ}=\dfrac{PF}{FR}\). If these ratios are different, \(EF\) cannot be parallel to \(QR\) — for if it were, BPT would make the ratios equal.

    The three cases, drawn to scale. In (i) the dashed line is the true parallel to \(QR\) through \(E\); the red segment \(EF\) is tilted away from it.

    (i)
    $$\frac{PE}{EQ}=\frac{3.9}{3}=1.3$$ $$\frac{PF}{FR}=\frac{3.6}{2.4}=1.5$$

    Since \(\dfrac{PE}{EQ}\ne\dfrac{PF}{FR}\), the line \(EF\) does not divide \(PQ\) and \(PR\) in the same ratio.

    \(EF\) is not parallel to \(QR\).

    (ii)
    $$\frac{PE}{EQ}=\frac{4}{4.5}=\frac{40}{45}=\frac89$$ $$\frac{PF}{FR}=\frac89$$

    Since \(\dfrac{PE}{EQ}=\dfrac{PF}{FR}\), by the converse of BPT,

    \(EF\parallel QR\).

    (iii)

    \(EQ=PQ-PE=1.28-0.18=1.10\) cm and \(FR=PR-PF=2.56-0.36=2.20\) cm.

    $$\frac{PE}{EQ}=\frac{0.18}{1.10}=\frac{18}{110}=\frac{9}{55}$$ $$\frac{PF}{FR}=\frac{0.36}{2.20}=\frac{36}{220}=\frac{9}{55}$$

    Since \(\dfrac{PE}{EQ}=\dfrac{PF}{FR}\), by the converse of BPT,

    \(EF\parallel QR\).

    Shortcut: \(\dfrac{PE}{PQ}=\dfrac{0.18}{1.28}=\dfrac{9}{64}\) and \(\dfrac{PF}{PR}=\dfrac{0.36}{2.56}=\dfrac{9}{64}\). Equal “part : whole” ratios also show that the sides are divided in the same ratio.

    (i) No   (ii) Yes   (iii) Yes

    Q3

    In the figure, \(LM\parallel CB\) and \(LN\parallel CD\). Prove that \(\dfrac{AM}{AB}=\dfrac{AN}{AD}\).

    Solution

    Given: \(LM\parallel CB\) and \(LN\parallel CD\). To prove: \(\dfrac{AM}{AB}=\dfrac{AN}{AD}\). The side \(AC\) is common to \(\triangle ABC\) and \(\triangle ADC\), so both ratios can be compared with a ratio on \(AC\).

    In \(\triangle ABC\), \(LM\parallel CB\) [given]

    $$\therefore\ \ \frac{AM}{MB}=\frac{AL}{LC}\quad\text{[BPT]}\quad\ldots(1)$$

    In \(\triangle ADC\), \(LN\parallel CD\) [given]

    $$\therefore\ \ \frac{AN}{ND}=\frac{AL}{LC}\quad\text{[BPT]}\quad\ldots(2)$$

    From (1) and (2),

    $$\frac{AM}{MB}=\frac{AN}{ND}$$

    Taking reciprocals and adding 1 to both sides,

    $$\def\arraystretch{2.5}\begin{aligned}\frac{MB}{AM}+1&=\frac{ND}{AN}+1\\ \frac{MB+AM}{AM}&=\frac{ND+AN}{AN}\\ \frac{AB}{AM}&=\frac{AD}{AN}\end{aligned}$$

    Taking reciprocals again,

    $$\frac{AM}{AB}=\frac{AN}{AD}.$$ Hence proved.

    Shorter: by the second form of BPT (NCERT Example 1), \(\dfrac{AM}{AB}=\dfrac{AL}{AC}\) in \(\triangle ABC\) and \(\dfrac{AN}{AD}=\dfrac{AL}{AC}\) in \(\triangle ADC\); the result follows at once.

    Q4

    In the figure, \(DE\parallel AC\) and \(DF\parallel AE\). Prove that \(\dfrac{BF}{FE}=\dfrac{BE}{EC}\).

    Solution

    Given: \(DE\parallel AC\) and \(DF\parallel AE\). To prove: \(\dfrac{BF}{FE}=\dfrac{BE}{EC}\). Both parallel lines start from \(D\) on \(AB\), so both ratios can be compared with \(\dfrac{BD}{DA}\).

    In \(\triangle ABC\), \(DE\parallel AC\) [given]

    $$\therefore\ \ \frac{BD}{DA}=\frac{BE}{EC}\quad\text{[BPT]}\quad\ldots(1)$$

    In \(\triangle ABE\), \(DF\parallel AE\) [given]

    $$\therefore\ \ \frac{BD}{DA}=\frac{BF}{FE}\quad\text{[BPT]}\quad\ldots(2)$$

    From (1) and (2),

    $$\frac{BF}{FE}=\frac{BE}{EC}.$$ Hence proved.
    Q5

    In the figure, \(DE\parallel OQ\) and \(DF\parallel OR\). Show that \(EF\parallel QR\).

    Solution

    Given: \(DE\parallel OQ\) and \(DF\parallel OR\). To prove: \(EF\parallel QR\). To prove lines parallel, aim for equal ratios on \(PQ\) and \(PR\) and then use the converse of BPT.

    In \(\triangle POQ\), \(DE\parallel OQ\) [given]

    $$\therefore\ \ \frac{PE}{EQ}=\frac{PD}{DO}\quad\text{[BPT]}\quad\ldots(1)$$

    In \(\triangle POR\), \(DF\parallel OR\) [given]

    $$\therefore\ \ \frac{PF}{FR}=\frac{PD}{DO}\quad\text{[BPT]}\quad\ldots(2)$$

    From (1) and (2),

    $$\frac{PE}{EQ}=\frac{PF}{FR}$$

    So in \(\triangle PQR\), the line \(EF\) divides the sides \(PQ\) and \(PR\) in the same ratio.

    \(\therefore\ EF\parallel QR\) [converse of BPT, Theorem 6.2]

    Hence shown.
    Q6

    In the figure, the points \(A\), \(B\) and \(C\) lie on \(OP\), \(OQ\) and \(OR\) respectively, with \(AB\parallel PQ\) and \(AC\parallel PR\). Show that \(BC\parallel QR\).

    Solution

    Given: \(AB\parallel PQ\) and \(AC\parallel PR\). To prove: \(BC\parallel QR\).

    In \(\triangle OPQ\), \(AB\parallel PQ\) [given]

    $$\therefore\ \ \frac{OA}{AP}=\frac{OB}{BQ}\quad\text{[BPT]}\quad\ldots(1)$$

    In \(\triangle OPR\), \(AC\parallel PR\) [given]

    $$\therefore\ \ \frac{OA}{AP}=\frac{OC}{CR}\quad\text{[BPT]}\quad\ldots(2)$$

    From (1) and (2),

    $$\frac{OB}{BQ}=\frac{OC}{CR}$$

    So in \(\triangle OQR\), the line \(BC\) divides the sides \(OQ\) and \(OR\) in the same ratio.

    \(\therefore\ BC\parallel QR\) [converse of BPT, Theorem 6.2]

    Hence shown.
    Q7

    Using Theorem 6.1, prove that a line drawn through the mid-point of one side of a triangle parallel to another side bisects the third side. (You proved this in Class IX by another method.)

    Solution

    Given: \(\triangle ABC\) in which \(D\) is the mid-point of \(AB\). The line through \(D\) parallel to \(BC\) meets \(AC\) at \(E\).

    To prove: \(E\) is the mid-point of \(AC\), that is, \(AE=EC\).

    Proof: In \(\triangle ABC\), \(DE\parallel BC\) [given]

    $$\therefore\ \ \frac{AD}{DB}=\frac{AE}{EC}\quad\text{[Theorem 6.1]}\ \ldots(1)$$

    But \(D\) is the mid-point of \(AB\), so \(AD=DB\), that is, \(\dfrac{AD}{DB}=1\).

    So from (1), \(\dfrac{AE}{EC}=1\), which gives \(AE=EC\).

    Hence \(E\) is the mid-point of \(AC\): the line bisects the third side. Hence proved.

    Q8

    Using Theorem 6.2, prove that the line joining the mid-points of any two sides of a triangle is parallel to the third side. (You did this in Class IX by another method.)

    Solution

    Given: \(\triangle ABC\) in which \(D\) and \(E\) are the mid-points of \(AB\) and \(AC\) respectively.

    To prove: \(DE\parallel BC\).

    Proof: \(D\) is the mid-point of \(AB\), so \(AD=DB\), that is, \(\dfrac{AD}{DB}=1\)  …(1)

    \(E\) is the mid-point of \(AC\), so \(AE=EC\), that is, \(\dfrac{AE}{EC}=1\)  …(2)

    From (1) and (2),

    $$\frac{AD}{DB}=\frac{AE}{EC}$$

    So the line \(DE\) divides the sides \(AB\) and \(AC\) of \(\triangle ABC\) in the same ratio.

    \(\therefore\ DE\parallel BC\) [Theorem 6.2, converse of BPT]

    Hence proved.
    Q9

    In a trapezium \(ABCD\) with \(AB\parallel DC\), the diagonals intersect each other at the point \(O\). Show that \(\dfrac{AO}{BO}=\dfrac{CO}{DO}\).

    Solution

    Given: Trapezium \(ABCD\) with \(AB\parallel DC\); the diagonals \(AC\) and \(BD\) intersect at \(O\).

    To prove: \(\dfrac{AO}{BO}=\dfrac{CO}{DO}\).

    Construction: Through \(O\), draw a line parallel to \(DC\), meeting \(AD\) at \(E\) and \(BC\) at \(F\).

    Proof: \(EO\parallel DC\) [construction] and \(AB\parallel DC\) [given], so \(EO\parallel AB\) [lines parallel to the same line are parallel to each other].

    In \(\triangle ADC\), \(EO\parallel DC\).

    $$\therefore\ \ \frac{AE}{ED}=\frac{AO}{OC}\quad\text{[BPT]}\quad\ldots(1)$$

    In \(\triangle DAB\), \(EO\parallel AB\).

    $$\therefore\ \ \frac{DE}{EA}=\frac{DO}{OB}\quad\text{[BPT]}$$

    Taking reciprocals,

    $$\frac{AE}{ED}=\frac{BO}{OD}\quad\ldots(2)$$

    From (1) and (2),

    $$\frac{AO}{OC}=\frac{BO}{OD}$$

    Cross-multiplying, \(AO\times OD=BO\times OC\). Dividing both sides by \(BO\times OD\),

    $$\frac{AO}{BO}=\frac{CO}{DO}.$$ Hence shown.

    Only the part \(EO\) of the line is used. The same result is proved with similar triangles (\(\triangle AOB\sim\triangle COD\), by AA) in Exercise 6.3, Question 3.

    Q10

    In a quadrilateral \(ABCD\), the diagonals intersect each other at the point \(O\) in such a way that \(\dfrac{AO}{BO}=\dfrac{CO}{DO}\). Show that \(ABCD\) is a trapezium.

    Solution

    Given: Quadrilateral \(ABCD\) whose diagonals \(AC\) and \(BD\) intersect at \(O\), with \(\dfrac{AO}{BO}=\dfrac{CO}{DO}\).

    To prove: \(ABCD\) is a trapezium, that is, \(AB\parallel DC\).

    Construction: Through \(O\), draw \(OE\parallel AB\), meeting \(AD\) at \(E\).

    Proof: In \(\triangle DAB\), \(EO\parallel AB\) [construction]

    $$\therefore\ \ \frac{DE}{EA}=\frac{DO}{OB}\quad\text{[BPT]}\quad\ldots(1)$$

    It is given that \(\dfrac{AO}{BO}=\dfrac{CO}{DO}\). Cross-multiplying, \(AO\times DO=BO\times CO\), so

    $$\frac{DO}{OB}=\frac{CO}{OA}\quad\ldots(2)$$

    From (1) and (2),

    $$\frac{DE}{EA}=\frac{CO}{OA}$$

    So in \(\triangle DAC\), the line \(EO\) divides the sides \(DA\) and \(CA\) in the same ratio.

    \(\therefore\ EO\parallel DC\) [converse of BPT, Theorem 6.2]

    Now \(EO\parallel AB\) [construction] and \(EO\parallel DC\) [proved]. Therefore \(AB\parallel DC\).

    A quadrilateral with one pair of opposite sides parallel is a trapezium. So \(ABCD\) is a trapezium. Hence shown.

    This is the converse of Question 9. Together they say: in a quadrilateral \(ABCD\) whose diagonals meet at \(O\), the relation \(\dfrac{AO}{BO}=\dfrac{CO}{DO}\) holds exactly when \(AB\parallel DC\).

    Q1

    Six pairs of triangles are shown below (Fig. 6.34 of the book, re-drawn to scale). State which pairs are similar. For every similar pair, name the similarity criterion you used and write the similarity in symbolic form.

    Solution

    Three tests are available. AAA (or AA): the angles of one triangle are equal to the angles of the other. SSS: the three pairs of sides are in the same ratio. SAS: one pair of angles is equal and the sides that include these angles are in the same ratio. In the symbolic form, the vertices must be written in matching order.

    (i)

    In \(\triangle ABC\) and \(\triangle PQR\):

    \(\angle A=\angle P=60^\circ,\quad \angle B=\angle Q=80^\circ,\quad \angle C=\angle R=40^\circ\)

    All three pairs of corresponding angles are equal.

    \(\therefore\ \triangle ABC\sim\triangle PQR\) [AAA similarity criterion]

    Yes — AAA; \(\triangle ABC\sim\triangle PQR\)

    (ii)

    Only sides are given, so we test SSS. If the triangles are similar, the shortest side of one must match the shortest side of the other, and so on. So write the sides of each triangle in increasing order and compare:

    \(\triangle ABC:\ AB=2,\ BC=2.5,\ CA=3\qquad \triangle PQR:\ QR=4,\ RP=5,\ PQ=6\)

    $$\frac{AB}{QR}=\frac{2}{4}=\frac12,\qquad \frac{BC}{RP}=\frac{2.5}{5}=\frac12,\qquad \frac{CA}{PQ}=\frac{3}{6}=\frac12$$

    So \(\dfrac{AB}{QR}=\dfrac{BC}{RP}=\dfrac{CA}{PQ}.\)

    \(\therefore\ \triangle ABC\sim\triangle QRP\) [SSS similarity criterion]

    Order of the letters: \(AB\leftrightarrow QR\) and \(BC\leftrightarrow RP,\) so \(A\leftrightarrow Q,\ B\leftrightarrow R,\ C\leftrightarrow P.\) Writing \(\triangle ABC\sim\triangle PQR\) here would be wrong.

    Yes — SSS; \(\triangle ABC\sim\triangle QRP\)

    (iii)

    Again only sides are given. In increasing order:

    \(\triangle LMP:\ MP=2,\ LM=2.7,\ LP=3\qquad \triangle DEF:\ DE=4,\ EF=5,\ DF=6\)

    $$\frac{MP}{DE}=\frac{2}{4}=\frac12,\qquad \frac{LM}{EF}=\frac{2.7}{5}=\frac{27}{50},\qquad \frac{LP}{DF}=\frac{3}{6}=\frac12$$

    Since \(\dfrac{27}{50}=0.54\ne\dfrac12,\) the three ratios are not all equal. The sides are not proportional, so the SSS test fails.

    No — the two triangles are not similar.

    (iv)

    In \(\triangle MNL\) and \(\triangle QPR\):

    $$\frac{MN}{QP}=\frac{2.5}{5}=\frac12,\qquad \frac{ML}{QR}=\frac{5}{10}=\frac12\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \frac{MN}{QP}=\frac{ML}{QR}$$

    \(\angle M=\angle Q=70^\circ\)

    \(\angle M\) is the angle included between \(MN\) and \(ML;\) \(\angle Q\) is the angle included between \(QP\) and \(QR.\) So one pair of angles is equal and the sides including them are proportional.

    \(\therefore\ \triangle MNL\sim\triangle QPR\) [SAS similarity criterion]

    Yes — SAS; \(\triangle MNL\sim\triangle QPR\)

    (v)

    Here \(\dfrac{AB}{DF}=\dfrac{2.5}{5}=\dfrac12\) and \(\dfrac{BC}{EF}=\dfrac{3}{6}=\dfrac12,\) so two pairs of sides are proportional. Also \({\angle A=\angle F=80^\circ}.\)

    For SAS the equal angles must be the angles included between the proportional sides.

    In \(\triangle DEF,\) \(\angle F\) is included between \(DF\) and \(EF.\) ✓

    In \(\triangle ABC,\) the angle included between \(AB\) and \(BC\) is \(\angle B\) — but the angle given is \(\angle A,\) and \(\angle B\) is not known. ✗

    So the SAS criterion cannot be applied, and the data do not fit AAA or SSS either.

    No — the two triangles cannot be said to be similar.

    The figure is drawn to scale: with \(AB=2.5,\ BC=3\) and \(\angle A=80^\circ,\) the angle \(B\) comes out to be about \(45^\circ,\) not \(80^\circ.\) So these two triangles really are not similar.

    In \(\triangle PQR\): \(\angle P=180^\circ-80^\circ-30^\circ=70^\circ\)

    So \(\angle D=\angle P=70^\circ,\quad \angle E=\angle Q=80^\circ,\quad \angle F=\angle R=30^\circ.\)

    \(\therefore\ \triangle DEF\sim\triangle PQR\) [AAA similarity criterion; two pairs of equal angles (AA) are already enough]

    Yes — AA (AAA); \(\triangle DEF\sim\triangle PQR\)

    Q3

    ABCD is a trapezium with \(AB\parallel DC.\) Its diagonals AC and BD intersect each other at the point O. Using a similarity criterion for two triangles, show that \(\dfrac{OA}{OC}=\dfrac{OB}{OD}.\)

    Solution

    Given: Trapezium ABCD with \(AB\parallel DC;\) diagonals AC and BD intersect at O. To prove: \(\dfrac{OA}{OC}=\dfrac{OB}{OD}.\)

    Proof: In \(\triangle OAB\) and \(\triangle OCD\):

    \(\angle OAB=\angle OCD\) [alternate angles; \(AB\parallel DC,\) transversal AC]

    \(\angle OBA=\angle ODC\) [alternate angles; \(AB\parallel DC,\) transversal BD]

    \(\angle AOB=\angle COD\) [vertically opposite angles]

    \(\therefore\ \triangle OAB\sim\triangle OCD\) [AAA similarity criterion]

    \(\therefore\ \dfrac{OA}{OC}=\dfrac{OB}{OD}\) [corresponding sides of similar triangles are proportional]

    Hence proved.

    This is the result of Exercise 6.2, Q9 again. There it was proved with the Basic Proportionality Theorem; here the question asks for a similarity criterion, so use AA/AAA. Any two of the three angle pairs are enough.

    Q4

    In the figure (Fig. 6.36 of the book), \(\dfrac{QR}{QS}=\dfrac{QT}{PR}\) and \(\angle 1=\angle 2.\) Show that \(\triangle PQS\sim\triangle TQR.\)

    Solution

    Given: \(\dfrac{QR}{QS}=\dfrac{QT}{PR}\) and \(\angle 1=\angle 2.\) To prove: \(\triangle PQS\sim\triangle TQR.\)

    Plan: the two triangles share \(\angle Q.\) So we try SAS: we need \(\dfrac{QP}{QT}=\dfrac{QS}{QR}.\) The given ratio has \(PR\) where we want \(PQ\) — and \(\angle1=\angle2\) is exactly what makes \(PR=PQ.\)

    Proof: In \(\triangle PQR,\)

    \(\angle PQR=\angle PRQ\) [\(\angle 1=\angle 2,\) given]

    \(\therefore\ PR=PQ\)  …(1) [sides opposite to equal angles of a triangle are equal]

    Now \(\dfrac{QR}{QS}=\dfrac{QT}{PR}\) [given]

    \(\Rightarrow\ \dfrac{QR}{QS}=\dfrac{QT}{PQ}\) [using (1)]

    \(\Rightarrow\ \dfrac{QS}{QR}=\dfrac{QP}{QT}\)  …(2) [taking reciprocals]

    In \(\triangle PQS\) and \(\triangle TQR\):

    \(\dfrac{QP}{QT}=\dfrac{QS}{QR}\) [from (2)]

    \(\angle PQS=\angle TQR\) [common angle, \(\angle 1\)]

    \(\therefore\ \triangle PQS\sim\triangle TQR\) [SAS similarity criterion]

    Hence proved.

    Q5

    In \(\triangle PQR,\) S is a point on side PR and T is a point on side QR such that \(\angle P=\angle RTS.\) Show that \(\triangle RPQ\sim\triangle RTS.\)

    Solution

    Given: \(\triangle PQR;\) S lies on PR, T lies on QR and \(\angle P=\angle RTS.\) To prove: \(\triangle RPQ\sim\triangle RTS.\)

    Proof: In \(\triangle RPQ\) and \(\triangle RTS\):

    \(\angle RPQ=\angle RTS\) [given]

    \(\angle PRQ=\angle TRS\) [common angle, \(\angle R\)]

    \(\therefore\ \triangle RPQ\sim\triangle RTS\) [AA similarity criterion]

    Hence proved.

    Check the order: \(R\leftrightarrow R\) (common angle), \(P\leftrightarrow T\) (the given equal angles), so \(Q\leftrightarrow S.\) That is exactly \(\triangle RPQ\sim\triangle RTS.\)

    Q6

    In the figure (Fig. 6.37 of the book), \(\triangle ABE\cong\triangle ACD.\) Show that \(\triangle ADE\sim\triangle ABC.\)

    Solution

    Given: \(\triangle ABE\cong\triangle ACD.\) To prove: \(\triangle ADE\sim\triangle ABC.\)

    Proof: \(\triangle ABE\cong\triangle ACD\) [given]

    \(\therefore\ AB=AC\)  …(1)   and   \(AD=AE\)  …(2) [CPCT — corresponding parts of congruent triangles]

    Dividing (2) by (1): \(\dfrac{AD}{AB}=\dfrac{AE}{AC}\)  …(3)

    In \(\triangle ADE\) and \(\triangle ABC\):

    \(\dfrac{AD}{AB}=\dfrac{AE}{AC}\) [from (3)]

    \(\angle DAE=\angle BAC\) [common angle, \(\angle A\)]

    \(\therefore\ \triangle ADE\sim\triangle ABC\) [SAS similarity criterion]

    Hence proved.

    Another way to finish: from (3), \(DE\parallel BC\) by the converse of the Basic Proportionality Theorem; then \(\angle ADE=\angle ABC\) (corresponding angles) and \(\angle A\) is common, so the triangles are similar by AA.

    Q7

    In the figure (Fig. 6.38 of the book), AD and CE are altitudes of \(\triangle ABC,\) and they intersect at the point P. Show that:

    1. \(\triangle AEP\sim\triangle CDP\)
    2. \(\triangle ABD\sim\triangle CBE\)
    3. \(\triangle AEP\sim\triangle ADB\)
    4. \(\triangle PDC\sim\triangle BEC\)
    Solution

    Given: In \(\triangle ABC,\) \(AD\perp BC\) and \(CE\perp AB;\) AD and CE intersect at P. So \({\angle ADB=\angle ADC=90^\circ}\) and \({\angle AEC=\angle BEC=90^\circ}.\)

    Plan: every triangle named in the question has a right angle (at D or at E). So each part needs just one more pair of equal angles — then AA does the job.

    (i)

    In \(\triangle AEP\) and \(\triangle CDP\):

    \(\angle AEP=\angle CDP\) [each \(90^\circ;\) \(CE\perp AB,\) \(AD\perp BC\)]

    \(\angle APE=\angle CPD\) [vertically opposite angles]

    \(\therefore\ \triangle AEP\sim\triangle CDP\) [AA similarity criterion]

    (ii)

    In \(\triangle ABD\) and \(\triangle CBE\):

    \(\angle ADB=\angle CEB\) [each \(90^\circ\)]

    \(\angle ABD=\angle CBE\) [common angle, \(\angle B\)]

    \(\therefore\ \triangle ABD\sim\triangle CBE\) [AA similarity criterion]

    (iii)

    In \(\triangle AEP\) and \(\triangle ADB\):

    \(\angle AEP=\angle ADB\) [each \(90^\circ\)]

    \(\angle PAE=\angle BAD\) [common angle — the angle at A between AB and AD]

    \(\therefore\ \triangle AEP\sim\triangle ADB\) [AA similarity criterion]

    (iv)

    In \(\triangle PDC\) and \(\triangle BEC\):

    \(\angle PDC=\angle BEC\) [each \(90^\circ\)]

    \(\angle PCD=\angle BCE\) [common angle — the angle at C between CB and CE]

    \(\therefore\ \triangle PDC\sim\triangle BEC\) [AA similarity criterion]

    Hence proved.

    In every part the vertices are listed in matching order: right-angle vertex with right-angle vertex (E, D), shared or vertically opposite angle with its partner. Check this before you write the \(\sim\) statement.

    Q8

    ABCD is a parallelogram. E is a point on the side AD produced, and BE intersects CD at F. Show that \(\triangle ABE\sim\triangle CFB.\)

    Solution

    Given: Parallelogram ABCD; E lies on AD produced; BE meets CD at F. To prove: \(\triangle ABE\sim\triangle CFB.\)

    Proof: In \(\triangle ABE\) and \(\triangle CFB\):

    \(\angle BAE=\angle FCB\) [opposite angles of a parallelogram are equal: \(\angle A=\angle C\)]

    \(\angle AEB=\angle CBF\) [alternate angles; \(AE\parallel BC\) because \(AD\parallel BC,\) and BE is a transversal]

    \(\therefore\ \triangle ABE\sim\triangle CFB\) [AA similarity criterion]

    Hence proved.

    Order check: \(A\leftrightarrow C\) (parallelogram angles), \(E\leftrightarrow B\) (alternate angles), so \(B\leftrightarrow F.\) Indeed \(\angle ABE=\angle CFB\) too — they are alternate angles for \(AB\parallel DC.\)

    Q9

    In the figure (Fig. 6.39 of the book), ABC and AMP are two right triangles: \(\triangle ABC\) is right-angled at B and \(\triangle AMP\) is right-angled at M. Prove that:

    1. \(\triangle ABC\sim\triangle AMP\)
    2. \(\dfrac{CA}{PA}=\dfrac{BC}{MP}\)
    Solution

    Given: \(\triangle ABC\) with \(\angle ABC=90^\circ\) and \(\triangle AMP\) with \(\angle AMP=90^\circ.\) To prove: (i) \(\triangle ABC\sim\triangle AMP\)  (ii) \(\dfrac{CA}{PA}=\dfrac{BC}{MP}.\)

    (i)

    In \(\triangle ABC\) and \(\triangle AMP\):

    \(\angle ABC=\angle AMP\) [each \(90^\circ,\) given]

    \(\angle BAC=\angle MAP\) [common angle, \(\angle A\)]

    \(\therefore\ \triangle ABC\sim\triangle AMP\) [AA similarity criterion]

    (ii)

    \(\triangle ABC\sim\triangle AMP\) [proved in (i)]

    So their corresponding sides are proportional. With \(A\leftrightarrow A,\ B\leftrightarrow M,\ C\leftrightarrow P\):

    $$\frac{AB}{AM}=\frac{BC}{MP}=\frac{CA}{PA}$$

    Taking the last two ratios, \(\dfrac{CA}{PA}=\dfrac{BC}{MP}.\)

    Hence proved.

    In \(\triangle ABC,\) CD is the bisector of \(\angle ACB\) and meets side AB at D. In \(\triangle EFG,\) GH is the bisector of \(\angle EGF\) and meets side FE at H. If \(\triangle ABC\sim\triangle FEG,\) show that:

    1. \(\dfrac{CD}{GH}=\dfrac{AC}{FG}\)
    2. \(\triangle DCB\sim\triangle HGE\)
    3. \(\triangle DCA\sim\triangle HGF\)
    Solution

    Given: \(\triangle ABC\sim\triangle FEG;\) CD bisects \(\angle ACB\) with D on AB; GH bisects \(\angle EGF\) with H on FE.

    First, the equal angles. Since \(\triangle ABC\sim\triangle FEG,\)

    \(\angle A=\angle F,\quad \angle B=\angle E,\quad \angle ACB=\angle FGE\)  …(1) [corresponding angles of similar triangles]

    Halving the last pair: \(\tfrac12\angle ACB=\tfrac12\angle FGE.\) As CD and GH are the bisectors of these angles,

    \(\angle ACD=\angle FGH\)  and  \(\angle DCB=\angle HGE\)  …(2)

    (i)

    In \(\triangle DCA\) and \(\triangle HGF\):

    \(\angle DAC=\angle HFG\) [\(\angle A=\angle F,\) from (1)]

    \(\angle ACD=\angle FGH\) [from (2)]

    \(\therefore\ \triangle DCA\sim\triangle HGF\) [AA similarity criterion]

    \(\therefore\ \dfrac{CD}{GH}=\dfrac{AC}{FG}\) [corresponding sides of similar triangles are proportional]

    (ii)

    In \(\triangle DCB\) and \(\triangle HGE\):

    \(\angle DBC=\angle HEG\) [\(\angle B=\angle E,\) from (1)]

    \(\angle DCB=\angle HGE\) [from (2)]

    \(\triangle DCA\sim\triangle HGF\) has already been proved in part (i), by AA: \(\angle DAC=\angle HFG\) and \(\angle ACD=\angle FGH.\)

    \(\angle ABD=\angle ECF\) [from (1); they are the same angles as \(\angle ABC\) and \(\angle ACB\)]

    Q12

    AD is a median of \(\triangle ABC\) and PM is a median of \(\triangle PQR\) (see the figure, Fig. 6.41 of the book). The sides AB, BC and the median AD are respectively proportional to the sides PQ, QR and the median PM. Show that \(\triangle ABC\sim\triangle PQR.\)

    Solution

    Given: AD and PM are medians of \(\triangle ABC\) and \(\triangle PQR,\) and \(\dfrac{AB}{PQ}=\dfrac{BC}{QR}=\dfrac{AD}{PM}\)  …(1). To prove: \(\triangle ABC\sim\triangle PQR.\)

    Plan: the three given lengths AB, AD and “half of BC” are the three sides of \(\triangle ABD.\) So first get \(\triangle ABD\sim\triangle PQM\) by SSS; that gives \(\angle B=\angle Q;\) then finish with SAS.

    Proof: AD and PM are medians, so \(BD=\tfrac12BC\) and \(QM=\tfrac12QR.\) Therefore

    $$\frac{BC}{QR}=\frac{2\,BD}{2\,QM}=\frac{BD}{QM}$$

    Putting this in (1): \(\dfrac{AB}{PQ}=\dfrac{BD}{QM}=\dfrac{AD}{PM}\)  …(2)

    In \(\triangle ABD\) and \(\triangle PQM\):

    \(\dfrac{AB}{PQ}=\dfrac{BD}{QM}=\dfrac{AD}{PM}\) [from (2)]

    \(\therefore\ \triangle ABD\sim\triangle PQM\) [SSS similarity criterion]

    \(\therefore\ \angle ABD=\angle PQM,\) that is, \(\angle B=\angle Q\)  …(3) [corresponding angles of similar triangles]

    Now in \(\triangle ABC\) and \(\triangle PQR\):

    \(\dfrac{AB}{PQ}=\dfrac{BC}{QR}\) [given, (1)]

    \(\angle B=\angle Q\) [from (3); it is the angle included between these sides]

    \(\therefore\ \triangle ABC\sim\triangle PQR\) [SAS similarity criterion]

    Hence proved.

    Q13

    In a triangle ABC, D is a point on the side BC such that \(\angle ADC=\angle BAC.\) Show that \(CA^2=CB\cdot CD.\)

    Solution

    Given: \(\triangle ABC;\) D lies on BC and \(\angle ADC=\angle BAC.\) To prove: \(CA^2=CB\cdot CD.\)

    Plan: the lengths CA, CB and CD all start from C, and they are sides of \(\triangle ABC\) and \(\triangle DAC.\) So prove that these two triangles are similar and write the ratios of their sides.

    Proof: In \(\triangle ABC\) and \(\triangle DAC\):

    \(\angle BAC=\angle ADC\) [given]

    \(\angle ACB=\angle DCA\) [common angle, \(\angle C\)]

    \(\therefore\ \triangle ABC\sim\triangle DAC\) [AA similarity criterion]

    Here \(A\leftrightarrow D,\ B\leftrightarrow A,\ C\leftrightarrow C.\) Writing the sides in this order,

    \(\dfrac{AB}{DA}=\dfrac{BC}{AC}=\dfrac{CA}{CD}\) [corresponding sides of similar triangles are proportional]

    From the last two ratios, \(\dfrac{CB}{CA}=\dfrac{CA}{CD}.\)

    Cross-multiplying, \(CA\times CA=CB\times CD,\) that is,

    $$CA^2=CB\cdot CD$$

    Hence proved.

    Whenever a result looks like \(x^2=y\cdot z,\) rewrite it as \(\dfrac{y}{x}=\dfrac{x}{z}\) and look for two similar triangles that have these sides.

    Q14

    AD is a median of \(\triangle ABC\) and PM is a median of another triangle PQR. The sides AB, AC and the median AD are respectively proportional to the sides PQ, PR and the median PM. Show that \(\triangle ABC\sim\triangle PQR.\)

    Solution

    Given: AD and PM are medians of \(\triangle ABC\) and \(\triangle PQR,\) and \(\dfrac{AB}{PQ}=\dfrac{AC}{PR}=\dfrac{AD}{PM}\)  …(1). To prove: \(\triangle ABC\sim\triangle PQR.\)

    Plan: unlike Q12, the three given lengths AB, AC, AD are not the sides of one triangle. Doubling the median fixes this: it builds \(\triangle ACE\) whose sides are AC, CE (= AB) and AE (= 2AD).

    Construction: Produce AD to a point E such that \(AD=DE,\) and produce PM to a point N such that \(PM=MN.\) Join EC and NR.

    Proof. Step 1 — two pairs of congruent triangles. In \(\triangle ABD\) and \(\triangle ECD\):

    \(AD=ED\) [construction]

    \(\angle ADB=\angle EDC\) [vertically opposite angles]

    \(BD=CD\) [AD is a median, so D is the mid-point of BC]

    \(\therefore\ \triangle ABD\cong\triangle ECD\) [SAS congruence rule]

    \(\therefore\ AB=EC\)  and  \(\angle BAD=\angle CED\)  …(2) [CPCT]

    In the same way, \(\triangle PQM\cong\triangle NRM\) [SAS: \(PM=NM,\) \(\angle PMQ=\angle NMR,\) \(QM=RM\)]

    \(\therefore\ PQ=NR\)  and  \(\angle QPM=\angle RNM\)  …(3) [CPCT]

    Step 2 — \(\triangle ACE\sim\triangle PRN.\) By construction \(AE=2AD\) and \(PN=2PM.\) Using (2) and (3) in (1):

    $$\frac{EC}{NR}=\frac{AC}{PR}=\frac{AD}{PM}=\frac{2AD}{2PM}=\frac{AE}{PN}$$

    So in \(\triangle ACE\) and \(\triangle PRN\): \(\dfrac{AC}{PR}=\dfrac{CE}{RN}=\dfrac{AE}{PN}\)

    \(\therefore\ \triangle ACE\sim\triangle PRN\) [SSS similarity criterion]

    \(\therefore\ \angle CAE=\angle RPN\)  and  \(\angle CEA=\angle RNP\) [corresponding angles of similar triangles]

    that is, \(\angle CAD=\angle RPM\)  …(4)  and  \(\angle CED=\angle RNM\)  …(5)

    Step 3 — \(\angle BAC=\angle QPR.\)

    \(\angle BAD=\angle CED\) [from (2)] \(=\angle RNM\) [from (5)] \(=\angle QPM\) [from (3)]  …(6)

    Adding (4) and (6): \(\angle BAD+\angle CAD=\angle QPM+\angle RPM,\) that is, \(\angle BAC=\angle QPR\)  …(7)

    Step 4 — finish with SAS. In \(\triangle ABC\) and \(\triangle PQR\):

    \(\dfrac{AB}{PQ}=\dfrac{AC}{PR}\) [given, (1)]

    \(\angle BAC=\angle QPR\) [from (7); it is the angle included between these sides]

    \(\therefore\ \triangle ABC\sim\triangle PQR\) [SAS similarity criterion]

    Hence proved.

    The construction is the one suggested in the book's answer key. “Produce the median to double its length” is a standard trick whenever a median appears together with the two sides from the same vertex.

    Q15

    A vertical pole 6 m long casts a shadow 4 m long on the ground. At the same time, the shadow of a tower is 28 m long. Find the height of the tower.

    Solution

    Let AB be the pole and BC its shadow: \(AB=6\text{ m},\ BC=4\text{ m}.\)

    Let PQ be the tower and QR its shadow: \(QR=28\text{ m}.\) Let the height of the tower be \(PQ=h\) metres.

    In \(\triangle ABC\) and \(\triangle PQR\):

    \(\angle ABC=\angle PQR\) [each \(90^\circ;\) the pole and the tower are vertical]

    \(\angle ACB=\angle PRQ\) [at the same time of day, the sun's rays meet the ground at the same angle]

    \(\therefore\ \triangle ABC\sim\triangle PQR\) [AA similarity criterion]

    \(\therefore\ \dfrac{AB}{PQ}=\dfrac{BC}{QR}\) [corresponding sides of similar triangles are proportional]

    \(\Rightarrow\ \dfrac{6}{h}=\dfrac{4}{28}\)

    \(\Rightarrow\ h=\dfrac{6\times28}{4}=42\)

    The height of the tower is 42 m.

    Quick check: the tower's shadow is \(28\div4=7\) times the pole's shadow, so the tower is 7 times the pole: \({7\times6=42}\) m. ✓

    Q16

    \(\triangle ABC\sim\triangle PQR,\) and AD and PM are medians of \(\triangle ABC\) and \(\triangle PQR\) respectively. Prove that \(\dfrac{AB}{PQ}=\dfrac{AD}{PM}.\)

    Solution

    Given: \(\triangle ABC\sim\triangle PQR;\) AD and PM are medians. To prove: \(\dfrac{AB}{PQ}=\dfrac{AD}{PM}.\)

    Proof: \(\triangle ABC\sim\triangle PQR\) [given]

    \(\therefore\ \dfrac{AB}{PQ}=\dfrac{BC}{QR}=\dfrac{CA}{RP}\)  …(1) [corresponding sides of similar triangles are proportional]

    and \(\angle B=\angle Q\)  …(2) [corresponding angles of similar triangles]

    AD and PM are medians, so \(BC=2BD\) and \(QR=2QM.\) From (1),

    $$\frac{AB}{PQ}=\frac{BC}{QR}=\frac{2\,BD}{2\,QM}=\frac{BD}{QM}\qquad\ldots(3)$$

    In \(\triangle ABD\) and \(\triangle PQM\):

    \(\dfrac{AB}{PQ}=\dfrac{BD}{QM}\) [from (3)]

    \(\angle ABD=\angle PQM\) [from (2); it is the angle included between these sides]

    \(\therefore\ \triangle ABD\sim\triangle PQM\) [SAS similarity criterion]

    \(\therefore\ \dfrac{AB}{PQ}=\dfrac{AD}{PM}\) [corresponding sides of similar triangles are proportional]

    Hence proved.

    Remember the result: in two similar triangles, corresponding medians are in the same ratio as corresponding sides. (NCERT Example 8 proves the same thing for the medians CM and RN.)

    Two vertical poles \(AB\) and \(CD\), of heights \(p\) metres and \(q\) metres, stand on level ground at \(B\) and \(D\). A wire joins the top of each pole to the foot of the other; the wires \(AD\) and \(BC\) cross at \(E\). \(EF\) is perpendicular to the ground and \(EF=h\) metres.

    1. Prove that \(\dfrac1p+\dfrac1q=\dfrac1h\).
    2. If the poles are 6 m and 3 m high, find the height at which the wires cross. Does this height change if the poles are moved further apart?
    Answer & solution
    (i)

    In \(\triangle DFE\) and \(\triangle DBA\):

    \(\angle DFE=\angle DBA=90^\circ\) [\(EF\perp BD\) and \(AB\perp BD\)]

    \(\angle FDE=\angle BDA\) [common angle \(D\)]

    \(\therefore\triangle DFE\sim\triangle DBA\) [AA similarity] \(\ \Rightarrow\dfrac{EF}{AB}=\dfrac{DF}{DB}\), that is, \(\dfrac hp=\dfrac{DF}{BD}\) …(1)

    In \(\triangle BFE\) and \(\triangle BDC\):

    \(\angle BFE=\angle BDC=90^\circ\) [\(EF\perp BD\) and \(CD\perp BD\)]

    \(\angle FBE=\angle DBC\) [common angle \(B\)]

    \(\therefore\triangle BFE\sim\triangle BDC\) [AA similarity] \(\ \Rightarrow\dfrac{EF}{CD}=\dfrac{BF}{BD}\), that is, \(\dfrac hq=\dfrac{BF}{BD}\) …(2)

    Adding (1) and (2):

    $$\frac hp+\frac hq=\frac{DF+BF}{BD}=\frac{BD}{BD}=1$$

    Dividing both sides by \(h\): \(\dfrac1p+\dfrac1q=\dfrac1h\). Hence proved.

    (ii)

    \(\dfrac1h=\dfrac16+\dfrac13=\dfrac{1+2}{6}=\dfrac12\Rightarrow h=2\). The wires cross 2 m above the ground.

    The distance \(BD\) does not appear in \(\dfrac1p+\dfrac1q=\dfrac1h\). So the height stays 2 m however far apart the poles are.

    1½ for the first pair of similar triangles and (1) · 1½ for the second pair and (2) · 1 for adding and the result · 1 for part (ii).

    Heights from shadows. On a sunny morning the students of Class X went to the school ground with a measuring tape. At the same time of the day they measured three shadows: Rahul, who is 1.5 m tall, cast a shadow 2 m long; the flagpole cast a shadow 8 m long; and the school building cast a shadow 20 m long. Their teacher told them that the sun's rays reaching the ground are parallel.

    1. The triangle formed by Rahul and his shadow is similar to the triangle formed by the flagpole and its shadow. Name the similarity criterion and give the reason. 1
    2. Find the height of the flagpole. 1
    3. Find the height of the school building. Later in the day Rahul's shadow is only 1 m long. How long is the shadow of the building at that time? 2ORAt the time of the first measurement, a tree 12 m high stands 10 m away from a high wall, and its shadow falls towards the wall. Part of the shadow lies on the ground and the rest climbs up the wall. Find the length of the shadow on the wall.
    Answer & solution
    (i)

    AA similarity. Each triangle has a right angle (Rahul and the pole are vertical), and the sun's rays make the same angle with the ground in both triangles because the rays are parallel.

    (ii)

    Corresponding sides of the similar triangles are in the same ratio:

    $$\frac{\text{height of pole}}{\text{height of Rahul}}=\frac{\text{shadow of pole}}{\text{shadow of Rahul}}$$

    \(\dfrac{h}{1.5}=\dfrac82=4\Rightarrow h=6\) m. 6 m

    (iii)

    Building: \(\dfrac{H}{1.5}=\dfrac{20}{2}=10\Rightarrow H=15\) m.

    Later, for Rahul, shadow : height \(=1:1.5=2:3\). The same ratio holds for the building at that time, so its shadow \(=\dfrac23\times15=10\) m. 15 m; 10 m

    OR: In the morning, shadow : height \(=2:1.5=4:3\). On open ground the tree's shadow would be \(\dfrac43\times12=16\) m long. The wall is only 10 m away, so the last \(16-10=6\) m of the shadow is cut off by the wall.

    The small right triangle behind the wall (base 6 m, height \(y\)) and the big triangle (base 16 m, height 12 m) have a right angle each and share the angle at the tip of the full shadow, so they are similar (AA):

    \(\dfrac{y}{12}=\dfrac{6}{16}\Rightarrow y=4.5\) m. 4.5 m of the shadow is on the wall.

    Triangles

    Similar figures

    • Same shape, not necessarily the same size
    • All circles, all squares, all equilateral triangles are similar
    • Congruent figures are similar; similar figures need not be congruent
    • Polygons need both: equal angles and proportional sides

    Basic Proportionality Theorem

    • \(DE\parallel BC\Rightarrow\dfrac{AD}{DB}=\dfrac{AE}{EC}\)
    • Same fact: \(\dfrac{AD}{AB}=\dfrac{AE}{AC}\) and \(\dfrac{DB}{AB}=\dfrac{EC}{AC}\)
    • Proof uses areas: join \(BE, CD\); draw \(EN\perp AB,\ DM\perp AC\)
    • Trapezium, \(EF\parallel AB\parallel DC\): \(\dfrac{AE}{ED}=\dfrac{BF}{FC}\)

    Converse of BPT

    • \(\dfrac{AD}{DB}=\dfrac{AE}{EC}\Rightarrow DE\parallel BC\)
    • To test for parallel lines: work out both ratios and compare
    • Joining mid-points of two sides gives a line parallel to the third side

    Similarity criteria

    • AA (AAA): two angles equal
    • SSS: all three pairs of sides in the same ratio
    • SAS: one angle equal and the sides including it proportional
    • For triangles, one condition forces the other

    Writing \(\triangle ABC\sim\triangle PQR\)

    • Order of letters = correspondence: \(A\leftrightarrow P,\ B\leftrightarrow Q,\ C\leftrightarrow R\)
    • \(\angle A=\angle P,\ \angle B=\angle Q,\ \angle C=\angle R\)
    • \(\dfrac{AB}{PQ}=\dfrac{BC}{QR}=\dfrac{CA}{RP}\)
    • Match sides opposite equal angles

    What similarity gives you

    • Sides, medians, altitudes, angle bisectors and perimeters — all in the same ratio
    • Products: write the ratio, cross-multiply (\(CA^2=CB\times CD\))
    • Unknown lengths and heights without measuring them

    Standard applications

    • Shadows at the same time: AA (right angle + sun's angle)
    • Lamp-post and walker: big base = distance + shadow
    • Mirror on the ground: equal angles at the mirror
    • Trapezium diagonals: \(\dfrac{OA}{OC}=\dfrac{OB}{OD}=\dfrac{AB}{DC}\)

    Formulas and facts to remember

    • Basic Proportionality Theorem (Thales)If a line parallel to one side of a triangle cuts the other two sides in distinct points, it divides them in the same ratio. \({DE\parallel BC}\ \Rightarrow\ {\dfrac{AD}{DB}=\dfrac{AE}{EC}}\)
    • Converse of BPTIf a line divides two sides of a triangle in the same ratio, it is parallel to the third side.
    • The parallel segment\({DE\parallel BC}\Rightarrow{\triangle ADE\sim\triangle ABC}\), so \({\dfrac{DE}{BC}=\dfrac{AD}{AB}=\dfrac{AE}{AC}}\) — the whole side goes in the denominator.
    • AA (AAA) criterionTwo angles of one triangle equal to two angles of another ⇒ similar.
    • SSS criterion\({\dfrac{AB}{PQ}=\dfrac{BC}{QR}=\dfrac{CA}{RP}}\) ⇒ \({\triangle ABC\sim\triangle PQR}\)
    • SAS criterion\({\angle A=\angle P}\) and \({\dfrac{AB}{PQ}=\dfrac{AC}{PR}}\) ⇒ \({\triangle ABC\sim\triangle PQR}\). The angle must lie between the two sides.
    • Similar triangles, ratio \(k\)Corresponding sides, medians, altitudes, angle bisectors and perimeters are all in the ratio \(k\).
    • Trapezium \(ABCD\), \(AB\parallel DC\), diagonals meet at \(O\)\({\triangle OAB\sim\triangle OCD}\), so \({\dfrac{OA}{OC}=\dfrac{OB}{OD}=\dfrac{AB}{DC}}\)
    • Shadows at the same time\({\dfrac{\text{height}_1}{\text{shadow}_1}=\dfrac{\text{height}_2}{\text{shadow}_2}}\) — after converting to the same unit.

    Mistakes that cost marks

    • Writing \(\frac{DE}{BC}=\frac{AD}{DB}\) when \(DE\parallel BC\).\(\frac{DE}{BC}=\frac{AD}{AB}\). BPT (\(\frac{AD}{DB}\)) is only about the parts of the two sides.
    • Writing the similarity with letters in any order, e.g. \(\triangle ABC\sim\triangle PQR\) when \(A\leftrightarrow Q\).Equal angles decide the order. Wrong order ⇒ wrong ratios ⇒ marks lost.
    • Using SAS with an angle that is not between the two proportional sides.The equal angle must be the included angle.
    • Using BPT when the line is not parallel to the third side (only an angle is given equal).Prove the triangles similar by AA and use the correspondence — see Q20.
    • Lamp-post problems: taking the base of the big triangle as the distance walked.Base of the big triangle = distance from the post + length of the shadow.
    • Mixing units (pole in m, shadow in cm).Convert everything to one unit before writing the ratio.
    • Leaving out reasons in a proof.Every step needs its reason: [alternate angles], [common angle], [BPT], [AA similarity] …
    • Squaring the ratio for medians, altitudes or perimeters.All corresponding lengths are in the same plain ratio as the sides.
