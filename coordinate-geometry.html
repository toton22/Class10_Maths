    7.1 The plane you already know

    In Class IX you learnt to pin down a point with two numbers. This chapter turns that skill into measurement. Given the coordinates of two points, you will find how far apart they are, and where exactly a point sits between them. Two formulas — that is the whole chapter.

    Draw two number lines at right angles. The horizontal one is the \(x\)-axis, the vertical one is the \(y\)-axis, and they cut at the origin \(O\). Now every point of the plane has an address.

    For a point \(P\):

    • its distance from the \(y\)-axis is the \(x\)-coordinate, also called the abscissa;
    • its distance from the \(x\)-axis is the \(y\)-coordinate, also called the ordinate.

    We write the address as \((x,\,y)\) — \(x\) first, always. So \((3,2)\) and \((2,3)\) are two different points.

    7.2 Distance formula

    Two towns, two points, two coordinates each — and one right triangle hiding between them. Every distance question in this chapter is Pythagoras in disguise.

    Start with a map

    Town B is 36 km east and 15 km north of town A. How far is B from A, without walking the distance and measuring it?

    Put A at the origin, take 1 km as one unit, and B becomes the point \((36,15)\). The eastward leg and the northward leg meet at a right angle, so

    $$\begin{aligned} AB^2&=36^2+15^2\\ &=1296+225=1521\\[3pt] AB&=\sqrt{1521}=39 \end{aligned}$$

    The towns are 39 km apart. Notice what we actually used: the two differences of coordinates, and Pythagoras. That is the whole formula.

    Warm-up: points on the axes

    If both points lie on the \(x\)-axis, their distance is simply the difference of the \(x\)-coordinates. The same is true on the \(y\)-axis with the \(y\)-coordinates.

    Now any two points

    Take \(P(x_1,y_1)\) and \(Q(x_2,y_2)\) anywhere in the plane. Drop perpendiculars \(PR\) and \(QS\) to the \(x\)-axis, and from \(P\) draw a horizontal line meeting \(QS\) at \(T\).

    1. \(OR=x_1\) and \(OS=x_2\), so the horizontal leg is \(PT=RS=x_2-x_1\).
    2. \(SQ=y_2\) and \(ST=PR=y_1\), so the vertical leg is \(QT=y_2-y_1\).
    3. \(\angle PTQ=90^\circ\), so by the Pythagoras theorem in \(\triangle PTQ\), $$\begin{aligned}PQ^2&=PT^2+QT^2\\&=(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2.\end{aligned}$$
    4. A distance is never negative, so we take only the positive square root.
    Distance formula
    $$PQ=\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2}$$

    The distance between \(P(x_1,y_1)\) and \(Q(x_2,y_2)\). Subtract the \(x\)’s, subtract the \(y\)’s, square both, add, take the positive square root.

    Distance from the origin
    $$OP=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$$

    Put \((x_1,y_1)=(0,0)\) in the formula above. This is the distance of \(P(x,y)\) from \(O(0,0)\).

    Because squaring destroys a minus sign, \((x_2-x_1)^2=(x_1-x_2)^2\). So you may also write

    $$PQ=\sqrt{(x_1-x_2)^2+(y_1-y_2)^2},$$

    and it does not matter which point you call \(P\) and which you call \(Q\). The answer is the same.

    Three slips cost marks every year:

    • writing \((x_2-y_1)\) — you may only subtract \(x\) from \(x\) and \(y\) from \(y\);
    • forgetting the square root and giving \(PQ^2\) as the answer;
    • losing a sign: for \(P(3,2)\) and \(Q(-2,-3)\), \(x_2-x_1=-2-3=-5\), not \(-2+3\). Put brackets around negative coordinates and the sign looks after itself.

    Given three points, find the three distances. Then read the shape off the lengths.

    Work with the squares of the sides as long as you can. Comparing 50, 52 and 2 is far safer than comparing \(5\sqrt2\), \(2\sqrt{13}\) and \(\sqrt2\) — and the Pythagoras check needs the squares anyway. Take square roots only at the last step.

    NCERT Example 1type of triangle

    Do the points \((3,2)\), \((-2,-3)\) and \((2,3)\) form a triangle? If they do, name the type of triangle.

    Solution

    Given: \(P(3,2)\), \(Q(-2,-3)\), \(R(2,3)\). To find: whether they form a triangle, and its type.

    Apply the distance formula to each pair.

    $$\begin{aligned} PQ&=\sqrt{(3+2)^2+(2+3)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{5^2+5^2}\\ &=\sqrt{50}\approx 7.07\\[2pt] QR&=\sqrt{(-2-2)^2+(-3-3)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{(-4)^2+(-6)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{52}\approx 7.21\\[2pt] PR&=\sqrt{(3-2)^2+(2-3)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{1^2+(-1)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{2}\approx 1.41 \end{aligned}$$

    The sum of any two of these lengths is greater than the third \((7.07+1.41\gt 7.21)\), so the three points are not in a straight line and they do form a triangle.

    Now test for a right angle. The longest side is \(QR\):

    $$PQ^2+PR^2=50+2=52=QR^2$$

    By the converse of the Pythagoras theorem, \(\angle P=90^\circ\).

    Yes, they form a triangle, and \(\triangle PQR\) is a right triangle, right-angled at \(P\).

    Use 2 — which quadrilateral is it?

    Four points, taken in the order given, make a quadrilateral \(ABCD\). Find the four sides \(AB,\ BC,\ CD,\ DA\) and the two diagonals \(AC,\ BD\), then use this table.

    SidesDiagonalsThe quadrilateral is a
    opposite sides equalunequalparallelogram
    opposite sides equalequalrectangle
    all four equalunequalrhombus
    all four equalequalsquare
    1. Take them in orderThe sides are \(AB,\ BC,\ CD,\ DA\) — never \(AC\) or \(BD\), those are the diagonals.
    2. Check the sidesAll four equal, or only the opposite pairs equal?
    3. Check the diagonalsEqual diagonals mean a right angle in the corner.
    4. Guard against a trapIf the largest of \(AB,\ BC,\ CD,\ DA\) equals the sum of two others, three of the points are in a line and there is no quadrilateral at all.

    Sides tell you how long, diagonals tell you how square. Equal diagonals \(\Rightarrow\) the corners are \(90^\circ\). So: all sides equal + equal diagonals = square; all sides equal + unequal diagonals = rhombus.

    NCERT Example 2proving a square

    Show that the points \((1,7)\), \((4,2)\), \((-1,-1)\) and \((-4,4)\) are the vertices of a square.

    Solution

    Given: \(A(1,7)\), \(B(4,2)\), \(C(-1,-1)\), \(D(-4,4)\). To prove: \(ABCD\) is a square.

    A quadrilateral is a square when all four sides are equal and the two diagonals are equal. So find six lengths.

    $$\begin{aligned} AB&=\sqrt{(1-4)^2+(7-2)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{9+25}=\sqrt{34}\\[3pt] BC&=\sqrt{(4+1)^2+(2+1)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{25+9}=\sqrt{34}\\[3pt] CD&=\sqrt{(-1+4)^2+(-1-4)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{9+25}=\sqrt{34}\\[3pt] DA&=\sqrt{(1+4)^2+(7-4)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{25+9}=\sqrt{34} \end{aligned}$$

    Now the diagonals.

    $$\begin{aligned} AC&=\sqrt{(1+1)^2+(7+1)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{4+64}=\sqrt{68}\\[3pt] BD&=\sqrt{(4+4)^2+(2-4)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{64+4}=\sqrt{68} \end{aligned}$$

    So \(AB=BC=CD=DA=\sqrt{34}\) and \(AC=BD=\sqrt{68}\).

    All four sides are equal and both diagonals are equal, so \(ABCD\) is a square. Hence proved.

    Second way. Once the four sides are equal, one right angle is enough. Here \(AD^2+DC^2=34+34=68=AC^2\), so \(\angle D=90^\circ\) by the converse of the Pythagoras theorem. A quadrilateral with four equal sides and one right angle is a square.

    Use 3 — are three points in a line?

    Definition

    Three points are collinear if they lie on one straight line. With coordinates, \(A\), \(B\) and \(C\) are collinear exactly when the largest of the three distances equals the sum of the other two — for example \(AB+BC=AC\), which also tells you that \(B\) lies between \(A\) and \(C\).

    Do not stop at “\(AB+BC\) is about equal to \(AC\)”. Keep the surds: \(3\sqrt2+2\sqrt2=5\sqrt2\) is an exact statement, while \(4.24+2.83=7.07\) is only a rounded one. If the numbers do not match exactly, the points are not collinear.

    NCERT Example 3collinearity

    The desks in a classroom are set out in rows and columns. Ashima, Bharti and Camella sit at \(A(3,1)\), \(B(6,4)\) and \(C(8,6)\). Are the three of them seated in a line? Give reasons.

    Solution

    Given: \(A(3,1)\), \(B(6,4)\), \(C(8,6)\). To find: whether \(A\), \(B\), \(C\) are collinear.

    $$\begin{aligned} AB&=\sqrt{(6-3)^2+(4-1)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{9+9}=\sqrt{18}=3\sqrt2\\[3pt] BC&=\sqrt{(8-6)^2+(6-4)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{4+4}=\sqrt{8}=2\sqrt2\\[3pt] AC&=\sqrt{(8-3)^2+(6-1)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{25+25}=\sqrt{50}=5\sqrt2 \end{aligned}$$

    Now compare:

    $$AB+BC=3\sqrt2+2\sqrt2=5\sqrt2=AC$$

    Since the two shorter distances add up exactly to the longest one, the three points lie on one straight line, with \(B\) between \(A\) and \(C\).

    Yes — Ashima, Bharti and Camella are seated in a line.

    Use 4 — equidistant points

    “\(P\) is equidistant from \(A\) and \(B\)” means \(PA=PB\). Square both sides at once — \(PA^2=PB^2\) — and the square roots disappear before they can cause trouble.

    1. Write the unknown point in the right form: \((x,y)\) in general, \((x,0)\) on the \(x\)-axis, \((0,y)\) on the \(y\)-axis.
    2. Write \(PA^2=PB^2\) using the distance formula, with no square-root signs.
    3. Expand both sides. Every \(x^2\) and \(y^2\) cancels, leaving a simple linear equation.
    4. Solve, and check your answer by computing \(PA\) and \(PB\).

    The set of all points equidistant from \(A\) and \(B\) is the perpendicular bisector of \(AB\) — a straight line through the mid-point of \(AB\) at right angles to it. That is why a question of this type ends in a linear equation.

    NCERT Example 4equidistant: a relation

    Find a relation between \(x\) and \(y\) such that the point \((x,y)\) is equidistant from the points \((7,1)\) and \((3,5)\).

    Solution

    Given: \(P(x,y)\) with \(PA=PB\), where \(A(7,1)\) and \(B(3,5)\). To find: a relation between \(x\) and \(y\).

    Since \(PA=PB\), we may square both sides: \(PA^2=PB^2\).

    $$(x-7)^2+(y-1)^2=(x-3)^2+(y-5)^2$$

    Expand each side on its own:

    $$\begin{aligned} \text{LHS}&=x^2-14x+49+y^2-2y+1\\ \text{RHS}&=x^2-6x+9+y^2-10y+25 \end{aligned}$$

    The \(x^2\) and the \(y^2\) cancel from both sides, and what is left is

    $$\begin{aligned} -14x-2y+50&=-6x-10y+34\\ -8x+8y+16&=0\\ x-y&=2 \end{aligned}$$

    \(x-y=2\)

    This is the equation of a straight line — the perpendicular bisector of \(AB\). Its mid-point \((5,3)\) satisfies it, as it must: \(5-3=2\).

    NCERT Example 5equidistant: a point on an axis

    Find the point on the \(y\)-axis that is equidistant from the points \(A(6,5)\) and \(B(-4,3)\).

    Solution

    Given: \(A(6,5)\), \(B(-4,3)\), and \(P\) on the \(y\)-axis with \(PA=PB\). To find: the coordinates of \(P\).

    A point on the \(y\)-axis has abscissa 0, so let \(P(0,y)\). Then \(PA^2=PB^2\) gives

    $$\begin{aligned} (6-0)^2+(5-y)^2&=(-4-0)^2+(3-y)^2\\ 36+25-10y+y^2&=16+9-6y+y^2\\ 61-10y&=25-6y\\ 36&=4y\\ y&=9 \end{aligned}$$

    Check.

    $$\begin{aligned} PA&=\sqrt{(6-0)^2+(5-9)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{36+16}=\sqrt{52}\\[3pt] PB&=\sqrt{(-4-0)^2+(3-9)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{16+36}=\sqrt{52} \end{aligned}$$

    The required point is \((0,9)\).

    It is the point where the \(y\)-axis meets the perpendicular bisector of \(AB\) — and a perpendicular bisector can cut an axis in only one point, so the answer is unique.

    How this section is asked in the board paper: 2 marks — one distance, or “find \(k\) if the distance between two given points is …”. 3 marks — show three points are collinear, or find a point on an axis equidistant from two given points. 5 marks — name the quadrilateral formed by four points, with all six lengths shown. Marks are given for stating the formula, so write it once before you substitute.

    Find the distance of the point \((-6,8)\) from the origin.

    Show answer

    \(OP=\sqrt{(-6)^2+8^2}=\sqrt{36+64}=\sqrt{100}=10\) units.

    7.3 Section formula

    The distance formula tells you how far. The section formula tells you where — the coordinates of the point that cuts a segment in a given ratio. Its most famous special case, the mid-point formula, is probably the single most used result of this chapter.

    Start with a relay tower

    A telephone company wants a relay tower at a point \(P\) somewhere on the straight road from town A to town B, so that the tower is twice as far from B as it is from A. Then \(P\) divides \(AB\) in the ratio \(1:2\).

    Take A as the origin and 1 km as one unit, so that B is the point \((36,15)\) as before. Let the tower be at \(P(x,y)\).

    Because \(AP:PB=1:2\) and the triangles are similar,

    $$\begin{aligned} \frac{AD}{PC}&=\frac{AP}{PB}=\frac12\\[4pt] \frac{PD}{BC}&=\frac{AP}{PB}=\frac12 \end{aligned}$$

    Reading the lengths off the figure, \(AD=x\), \(PC=36-x\), \(PD=y\) and \(BC=15-y\). So

    $$\begin{aligned} \frac{x}{36-x}&=\frac12\\ 2x&=36-x\\ x&=12 \end{aligned} \qquad \begin{aligned} \frac{y}{15-y}&=\frac12\\ 2y&=15-y\\ y&=5 \end{aligned}$$

    The tower stands at \(P(12,5)\). Check: \(AP=\sqrt{144+25}=13\) and \(PB=\sqrt{24^2+10^2}=26\), and indeed \(13:26=1:2\).

    The general result

    Let \(A(x_1,y_1)\) and \(B(x_2,y_2)\) be any two points, and let \(P(x,y)\) lie on the segment \(AB\) with

    $$\frac{PA}{PB}=\frac{m_1}{m_2}.$$

    Draw \(AR\), \(PS\) and \(BT\) perpendicular to the \(x\)-axis, and draw \(AQ\) and \(PC\) parallel to the \(x\)-axis.

    1. From the similarity, $$\frac{PA}{BP}=\frac{AQ}{PC}=\frac{PQ}{BC}. \qquad \ldots(1)$$
    2. Read the four lengths off the figure: $$\begin{aligned} AQ&=RS=OS-OR=x-x_1\\ PC&=ST=OT-OS=x_2-x\\ PQ&=PS-QS=PS-AR=y-y_1\\ BC&=BT-CT=BT-PS=y_2-y \end{aligned}$$
    3. Put these into (1): $$\frac{m_1}{m_2}=\frac{x-x_1}{x_2-x}=\frac{y-y_1}{y_2-y}$$
    4. Take the first two parts and cross-multiply: $$\begin{aligned} m_1(x_2-x)&=m_2(x-x_1)\\ m_1x_2+m_2x_1&=x(m_1+m_2)\\ x&=\frac{m_1x_2+m_2x_1}{m_1+m_2} \end{aligned}$$
    5. The first and third parts give \(y\) in exactly the same way.
    Section formula (internal division)
    $$\begin{aligned} x&=\frac{m_1x_2+m_2x_1}{m_1+m_2}\\[5pt] y&=\frac{m_1y_2+m_2y_1}{m_1+m_2} \end{aligned}$$

    \(P(x,y)\) is the point that divides the join of \(A(x_1,y_1)\) and \(B(x_2,y_2)\) internally in the ratio \(m_1:m_2\), that is, with \(PA:PB=m_1:m_2\). In one line it is written \(P\left(\frac{m_1x_2+m_2x_1}{m_1+m_2},\frac{m_1y_2+m_2y_1}{m_1+m_2}\right)\).

    The \(k:1\) form
    $$P=\left(\frac{kx_2+x_1}{k+1},\ \frac{ky_2+y_1}{k+1}\right)$$

    Any ratio \(m_1:m_2\) can be written as \(\dfrac{m_1}{m_2}:1\), that is \(k:1\). One unknown instead of two — use this whenever the ratio is what you have to find.

    Mid-point formula
    $$M=\left(\frac{x_1+x_2}{2},\ \frac{y_1+y_2}{2}\right)$$

    The mid-point divides the segment in the ratio \(1:1\). Put \(m_1=m_2=1\) in the section formula and this is what is left: just the average of the two \(x\)’s and the average of the two \(y\)’s.

    Order matters. In \(m_1:m_2\), the number \(m_1\) belongs to the part \(PA\) — the piece next to the first point \(A\). If you write the points in the other order you must swap the ratio too. Dividing \(AB\) in \(2:3\) is not the same point as dividing \(AB\) in \(3:2\).

    Only internal division is in your syllabus. If \(P\) lies on the line \(AB\) but outside the segment, we say \(P\) divides \(AB\) externally; you will meet that in higher classes. So in this chapter, a ratio that comes out negative means the point you were given is not between \(A\) and \(B\) — or that you have made a slip.

    You are given the ratio

    Use the \(m_1:m_2\) form straight away. Substitute and simplify — two short calculations, one for \(x\) and one for \(y\).

    You have to find the ratio

    Use the \(k:1\) form. Equate just one coordinate, solve for \(k\), then answer \(k:1\). Check with the other coordinate.

    NCERT Example 6ratio given

    Find the coordinates of the point that divides the line segment joining \((4,-3)\) and \((8,5)\) internally in the ratio \(3:1\).

    Solution

    Given: \(A(4,-3)\), \(B(8,5)\), ratio \(m_1:m_2=3:1\). To find: the point \(P(x,y)\).

    Here \(x_1=4,\ y_1=-3,\ x_2=8,\ y_2=5,\ m_1=3,\ m_2=1\). By the section formula,

    $$\begin{aligned} x&=\frac{m_1x_2+m_2x_1}{m_1+m_2}\\ &=\frac{3(8)+1(4)}{3+1}=\frac{24+4}{4}\\ &=\frac{28}{4}=7\\[5pt] y&=\frac{m_1y_2+m_2y_1}{m_1+m_2}\\ &=\frac{3(5)+1(-3)}{3+1}=\frac{15-3}{4}\\ &=\frac{12}{4}=3 \end{aligned}$$

    The required point is \((7,3)\).

    NCERT Example 7ratio to be found

    In what ratio does the point \((-4,6)\) divide the line segment joining the points \(A(-6,10)\) and \(B(3,-8)\)?

    Solution

    Given: \(A(-6,10)\), \(B(3,-8)\), and \(P(-4,6)\) on \(AB\). To find: the ratio \(AP:PB\).

    Method 1 — with \(m_1:m_2\). Let \(P\) divide \(AB\) in the ratio \(m_1:m_2\). By the section formula,

    $$\begin{aligned} -4&=\frac{3m_1-6m_2}{m_1+m_2}\\[4pt] 6&=\frac{-8m_1+10m_2}{m_1+m_2} \end{aligned}$$

    (Two ordered pairs are equal only when the first parts are equal and the second parts are equal, so each coordinate gives an equation.) Take the first one,

    $$\begin{aligned} -4&=\frac{3m_1-6m_2}{m_1+m_2}\\ -4m_1-4m_2&=3m_1-6m_2\\ 2m_2&=7m_1\\ \frac{m_1}{m_2}&=\frac27 \end{aligned}$$

    Verify with the \(y\)-coordinate. With \(m_1:m_2=2:7\),

    $$\frac{-8(2)+10(7)}{2+7}=\frac{54}{9}=6\ \checkmark$$

    Method 2 — with \(k:1\) (shorter). Let the ratio be \(k:1\). Then

    $$(-4,\,6)=\left(\frac{3k-6}{k+1},\ \frac{-8k+10}{k+1}\right)$$ $$\begin{aligned} -4&=\frac{3k-6}{k+1}\\ -4k-4&=3k-6\\ 7k&=2\\ k&=\frac27 \end{aligned}$$

    so the ratio \(k:1\) is \(\dfrac27:1=2:7\).

    \((-4,6)\) divides \(AB\) in the ratio \(2:7\).

    You could also find this ratio as \(PA:PB\) using the distance formula, but only because you are told that \(A\), \(P\) and \(B\) are collinear.

    NCERT Example 8trisection

    Find the coordinates of the points of trisection of the line segment joining \(A(2,-2)\) and \(B(-7,4)\). (The points of trisection divide the segment into three equal parts.)

    Solution

    Given: \(A(2,-2)\), \(B(-7,4)\), with \(P\) and \(Q\) on \(AB\) such that \(AP=PQ=QB\). To find: \(P\) and \(Q\).

    If the three parts are equal, then \(AP:PB=1:2\) and \(AQ:QB=2:1\). That is the whole trick.

    The point \(P\) (ratio \(1:2\)):

    $$\begin{aligned} x&=\frac{1(-7)+2(2)}{1+2}=\frac{-7+4}{3}=-1\\ y&=\frac{1(4)+2(-2)}{1+2}=\frac{4-4}{3}=0 \end{aligned}$$

    The point \(Q\) (ratio \(2:1\)):

    $$\begin{aligned} x&=\frac{2(-7)+1(2)}{2+1}=\frac{-14+2}{3}=-4\\ y&=\frac{2(4)+1(-2)}{2+1}=\frac{8-2}{3}=2 \end{aligned}$$

    The points of trisection are \((-1,0)\) and \((-4,2)\).

    A neat check: \(Q\) is the mid-point of \(PB\), and \(\left(\dfrac{-1-7}{2},\dfrac{0+4}{2}\right)=(-4,2)\ \checkmark\).

    NCERT Example 9an axis cuts a segment

    Find the ratio in which the \(y\)-axis divides the line segment joining the points \((5,-6)\) and \((-1,-4)\). Also find the point where it cuts the segment.

    Solution

    Given: \(A(5,-6)\) and \(B(-1,-4)\); the \(y\)-axis meets \(AB\) at \(P\). To find: the ratio \(AP:PB\) and the point \(P\).

    Let the \(y\)-axis divide \(AB\) in the ratio \(k:1\). By the section formula the point of division is

    $$\left(\frac{-k+5}{k+1},\ \frac{-4k-6}{k+1}\right)$$

    This point lies on the \(y\)-axis, and on the \(y\)-axis the abscissa is 0. So

    $$\begin{aligned} \frac{-k+5}{k+1}&=0\\ -k+5&=0\\ k&=5 \end{aligned}$$

    The ratio is \(5:1\). Putting \(k=5\) in the \(y\)-coordinate,

    $$\begin{aligned} y&=\frac{-4(5)-6}{5+1}=\frac{-20-6}{6}\\[3pt] &=\frac{-26}{6}=-\frac{13}{3} \end{aligned}$$

    The \(y\)-axis divides the segment in the ratio \(5:1\), at the point \(\left(0,-\dfrac{13}{3}\right)\).

    The same idea with the \(x\)-axis: there the ordinate is 0, so you set the \(y\)-coordinate of the dividing point equal to 0 instead.

    NCERT Example 10mid-point of the diagonals

    The points \(A(6,1)\), \(B(8,2)\), \(C(9,4)\) and \(D(p,3)\), taken in order, are the vertices of a parallelogram. Find the value of \(p\).

    Solution

    Given: \(ABCD\) is a parallelogram with \(A(6,1)\), \(B(8,2)\), \(C(9,4)\), \(D(p,3)\). To find: \(p\).

    The diagonals of a parallelogram bisect each other, so the mid-point of \(AC\) is the same point as the mid-point of \(BD\).

    $$\left(\frac{6+9}{2},\ \frac{1+4}{2}\right)=\left(\frac{8+p}{2},\ \frac{2+3}{2}\right)$$ $$\left(\frac{15}{2},\ \frac52\right)=\left(\frac{8+p}{2},\ \frac52\right)$$

    The \(y\)-coordinates already agree. Comparing the \(x\)-coordinates,

    $$\begin{aligned} \frac{15}{2}&=\frac{8+p}{2}\\ 15&=8+p\\ p&=7 \end{aligned}$$

    \(p=7\)

    Take the vertices in the order given. In \(ABCD\) the diagonals are \(AC\) and \(BD\) — pairing \(AB\) with \(CD\) instead would give the wrong equation.

    Almost every section-formula question is one of these three, so decide which it is before you write anything.

    1. Point wantedRatio is given — substitute into \(m_1:m_2\) and finish.
    2. Ratio wantedUse \(k:1\); equate the coordinate you know (often 0, on an axis), solve for \(k\).
    3. Missing vertex wantedUse “the diagonals bisect each other” for a parallelogram, or the mid-point formula for a diameter of a circle.

    Board favourites from this section: trisection points (3 marks), the ratio in which an axis or a given line divides a segment (3 marks), the fourth vertex of a parallelogram (3 marks), and the centre of a circle as the mid-point of a diameter (2 marks). Always state the formula first, then substitute — the formula itself carries a mark.

    Find the mid-point of the segment joining \((-3,7)\) and \((5,-1)\).

    Show answer

    \(M=\left(\dfrac{-3+5}{2},\dfrac{7-1}{2}\right)=(1,3)\).

    7.4 Summary

    The whole chapter, on one screen. Learn these four lines by heart — every question you will ever be set on coordinate geometry in Class X comes out of them.

    1 · Distance between two points
    $$PQ=\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2}$$

    For \(P(x_1,y_1)\) and \(Q(x_2,y_2)\).

    2 · Distance from the origin
    $$OP=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$$

    For \(P(x,y)\) and \(O(0,0)\).

    3 · Internal division in the ratio \(m_1:m_2\)
    $$\left(\frac{m_1x_2+m_2x_1}{m_1+m_2},\ \frac{m_1y_2+m_2y_1}{m_1+m_2}\right)$$

    The point on \(AB\) with \(PA:PB=m_1:m_2\), where \(A(x_1,y_1)\) and \(B(x_2,y_2)\).

    4 · Mid-point
    $$\left(\frac{x_1+x_2}{2},\ \frac{y_1+y_2}{2}\right)$$

    The ratio \(1:1\) case of the section formula.

    Exercise 7.1

    10 questions · the distance formula: plain distances, collinearity, type of triangle and quadrilateral, equidistant points

    One formula runs through this whole exercise:

    $$PQ=\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2}$$

    Write it down before you substitute — it carries a mark on its own. Leave surds in the simplest form \(\left(\sqrt{50}=5\sqrt2\right)\); never round unless the question asks you to.

    Q1

    Find the distance between each of these pairs of points:

    1. \((2,3)\) and \((4,1)\)
    2. \((-5,7)\) and \((-1,3)\)
    3. \((a,b)\) and \((-a,-b)\)
    Solution

    To find: the distance in each pair, using \(PQ=\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2}\).

    (i)

    Here \((x_1,y_1)=(2,3)\) and \((x_2,y_2)=(4,1)\).

    $$\begin{aligned} d&=\sqrt{(4-2)^2+(1-3)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{2^2+(-2)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{4+4}=\sqrt{8}=2\sqrt2 \end{aligned}$$

    \(2\sqrt2\) units

    (ii)

    Here \((x_1,y_1)=(-5,7)\) and \((x_2,y_2)=(-1,3)\).

    $$\begin{aligned} d&=\sqrt{(-1-(-5))^2+(3-7)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{4^2+(-4)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{16+16}=\sqrt{32}=4\sqrt2 \end{aligned}$$

    \(4\sqrt2\) units

    (iii)

    Here \((x_1,y_1)=(a,b)\) and \((x_2,y_2)=(-a,-b)\).

    $$\begin{aligned} d&=\sqrt{(-a-a)^2+(-b-b)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{(-2a)^2+(-2b)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{4a^2+4b^2}\\ &=\sqrt{4(a^2+b^2)}\\ &=2\sqrt{a^2+b^2} \end{aligned}$$

    \(2\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\) units

    (i) \(2\sqrt2\)   (ii) \(4\sqrt2\)   (iii) \(2\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\)

    In (iii) the origin is the mid-point of \(AB\), so the distance is twice \(OA=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\) — a quick way to check the answer.

    Q2

    Find the distance between the points \((0,0)\) and \((36,15)\). Can you now find the distance between the two towns A and B discussed in Section 7.2?

    Solution

    Given: \(O(0,0)\) and \(P(36,15)\). To find: \(OP\), and then the distance \(AB\) between the towns.

    Part 1 — the distance between the points. Since one point is the origin, use \(OP=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\).

    $$\begin{aligned} OP&=\sqrt{36^2+15^2}\\ &=\sqrt{1296+225}\\ &=\sqrt{1521}=39 \end{aligned}$$

    Part 2 — the towns. Town B lies 36 km east and 15 km north of town A. Take A as the origin and 1 km as one unit, with east along the positive \(x\)-axis and north along the positive \(y\)-axis. Then A is \((0,0)\) and B is \((36,15)\), exactly the points above.

    \(OP=39\) units; the towns A and B are 39 km apart.

    \(1521=3^2\times169=9\times169\), so \(\sqrt{1521}=3\times13=39\) — no calculator needed.

    Q3

    Determine whether the points \((1,5)\), \((2,3)\) and \((-2,-11)\) are collinear.

    Solution

    Given: \(A(1,5)\), \(B(2,3)\), \(C(-2,-11)\). To find: whether \(A\), \(B\), \(C\) lie on one straight line.

    Three points are collinear only when the longest of the three distances equals the sum of the other two. So find all three.

    $$\begin{aligned} AB&=\sqrt{(2-1)^2+(3-5)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{1+4}=\sqrt5\approx 2.24\\[3pt] BC&=\sqrt{(-2-2)^2+(-11-3)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{16+196}\\ &=\sqrt{212}\\ &=2\sqrt{53}\approx 14.56\\[3pt] AC&=\sqrt{(-2-1)^2+(-11-5)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{9+256}\\ &=\sqrt{265}\approx 16.28 \end{aligned}$$

    The longest distance is \(AC\). Compare:

    $$\begin{aligned} AB+BC&\approx 2.24+14.56=16.80\\ AC&\approx 16.28 \end{aligned}$$

    and \(16.80\ne16.28\).

    The two shorter distances do not add up to the longest one, and no other pairing works either.

    No — the points \((1,5)\), \((2,3)\) and \((-2,-11)\) are not collinear.

    You can also see it without surds: from \(A\) to \(B\) the step is \((+1,-2)\), while from \(B\) to \(C\) it is \((-4,-14)\). If the points were in a line, the second step would be a multiple of the first — and \((-4,-14)\) is not a multiple of \((1,-2)\).

    Q4

    Check whether \((5,-2)\), \((6,4)\) and \((7,-2)\) are the vertices of an isosceles triangle.

    Solution

    Given: \(A(5,-2)\), \(B(6,4)\), \(C(7,-2)\). To check: whether \(\triangle ABC\) is isosceles — that is, whether two of its sides are equal.

    $$\begin{aligned} AB&=\sqrt{(6-5)^2+(4-(-2))^2}\\ &=\sqrt{1^2+6^2}\\ &=\sqrt{1+36}=\sqrt{37}\\[3pt] BC&=\sqrt{(7-6)^2+(-2-4)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{1^2+(-6)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{1+36}=\sqrt{37}\\[3pt] AC&=\sqrt{(7-5)^2+(-2-(-2))^2}\\ &=\sqrt{2^2+0^2}=\sqrt4=2 \end{aligned}$$

    Here \(AB=BC=\sqrt{37}\), while \(AC=2\) is different. Two sides are equal, so the triangle is isosceles. (The three points are not collinear, since \(AB+AC=\sqrt{37}+2\ne\sqrt{37}=BC\); a genuine triangle is formed.)

    Yes — the points are the vertices of an isosceles triangle.

    Q5

    Four friends sit in a classroom at the points A, B, C and D marked on the grid of desks. Champa walks in, looks around and asks Chameli, “Don’t you think \(ABCD\) is a square?” Chameli disagrees. Using the distance formula, decide which of the two is right.

    Solution

    Given: \(A(3,4)\), \(B(6,7)\), \(C(9,4)\), \(D(6,1)\), read off the grid. To find: whether \(ABCD\) is a square.

    Step 1 — the four sides.

    $$\begin{aligned} AB&=\sqrt{(6-3)^2+(7-4)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{9+9}=3\sqrt2\\[3pt] BC&=\sqrt{(9-6)^2+(4-7)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{9+9}=3\sqrt2\\[3pt] CD&=\sqrt{(6-9)^2+(1-4)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{9+9}=3\sqrt2\\[3pt] DA&=\sqrt{(3-6)^2+(4-1)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{9+9}=3\sqrt2 \end{aligned}$$

    Step 2 — the two diagonals.

    $$\begin{aligned} AC&=\sqrt{(9-3)^2+(4-4)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{36+0}=6\\[3pt] BD&=\sqrt{(6-6)^2+(1-7)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{0+36}=6 \end{aligned}$$

    Step 3 — read the table. All four sides are equal \(\left(3\sqrt2\right)\) and the two diagonals are equal \((6)\). A quadrilateral with all sides equal and equal diagonals is a square.

    \(ABCD\) is a square, so Champa is correct.

    A second check: \(AB^2+BC^2=18+18=36=AC^2\), so \(\angle B=90^\circ\) by the converse of the Pythagoras theorem. A rhombus with one right angle is a square.

    Q6

    Name the type of quadrilateral formed, if any, by each set of points below, taken in the order given, and give reasons for your answer:

    1. \((-1,-2),\ (1,0),\ (-1,2),\ (-3,0)\)
    2. \((-3,5),\ (3,1),\ (0,3),\ (-1,-4)\)
    3. \((4,5),\ (7,6),\ (4,3),\ (1,2)\)
    Solution

    Method: call the points \(A\), \(B\), \(C\), \(D\) in the order given. Find the four sides \(AB,\ BC,\ CD,\ DA\) and the two diagonals \(AC,\ BD\), then name the shape.

    (i)

    \(A(-1,-2)\), \(B(1,0)\), \(C(-1,2)\), \(D(-3,0)\).

    $$\begin{aligned} AB&=\sqrt{(1+1)^2+(0+2)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{4+4}=2\sqrt2\\[3pt] BC&=\sqrt{(-1-1)^2+(2-0)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{4+4}=2\sqrt2\\[3pt] CD&=\sqrt{(-3+1)^2+(0-2)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{4+4}=2\sqrt2\\[3pt] DA&=\sqrt{(-1+3)^2+(-2-0)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{4+4}=2\sqrt2 \end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned} AC&=\sqrt{(-1+1)^2+(2+2)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{0+16}=4\\[3pt] BD&=\sqrt{(-3-1)^2+(0-0)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{16+0}=4 \end{aligned}$$

    All four sides are equal and the diagonals are equal.

    \(ABCD\) is a square.

    (ii)

    \(A(-3,5)\), \(B(3,1)\), \(C(0,3)\), \(D(-1,-4)\).

    $$\begin{aligned} AB&=\sqrt{(3+3)^2+(1-5)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{36+16}=2\sqrt{13}\\[3pt] BC&=\sqrt{(0-3)^2+(3-1)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{9+4}=\sqrt{13}\\[3pt] CD&=\sqrt{(-1-0)^2+(-4-3)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{1+49}=5\sqrt2\\[3pt] DA&=\sqrt{(-3+1)^2+(5+4)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{4+81}=\sqrt{85} \end{aligned}$$

    Now look at \(A\), \(C\) and \(B\):

    $$\begin{aligned} AC&=\sqrt{(0+3)^2+(3-5)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{9+4}=\sqrt{13} \end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned}AC+CB&=\sqrt{13}+\sqrt{13}\\&=2\sqrt{13}=AB\end{aligned}$$

    So \(A\), \(C\) and \(B\) are collinear, with \(C\) lying on the segment \(AB\). Three of the four points are on one straight line, so joining \(A\to B\to C\to D\) does not close up into a quadrilateral at all — the “side” \(BC\) folds back along \(AB\).

    No quadrilateral is formed.

    (iii)

    \(A(4,5)\), \(B(7,6)\), \(C(4,3)\), \(D(1,2)\).

    $$\begin{aligned} AB&=\sqrt{(7-4)^2+(6-5)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{9+1}=\sqrt{10}\\[3pt] BC&=\sqrt{(4-7)^2+(3-6)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{9+9}=3\sqrt2\\[3pt] CD&=\sqrt{(1-4)^2+(2-3)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{9+1}=\sqrt{10}\\[3pt] DA&=\sqrt{(4-1)^2+(5-2)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{9+9}=3\sqrt2 \end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned} AC&=\sqrt{(4-4)^2+(3-5)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{0+4}=2\\[3pt] BD&=\sqrt{(1-7)^2+(2-6)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{36+16}=2\sqrt{13} \end{aligned}$$

    Opposite sides are equal in pairs \(\left(AB=CD=\sqrt{10},\ BC=DA=3\sqrt2\right)\), but the diagonals are unequal \(\left(2\ne 2\sqrt{13}\right)\).

    \(ABCD\) is a parallelogram (and not a rectangle).

    Confirm with the diagonals: mid-point of \(AC=\left(\tfrac{4+4}{2},\tfrac{5+3}{2}\right)=(4,4)\) and mid-point of \(BD=\left(\tfrac{7+1}{2},\tfrac{6+2}{2}\right)=(4,4)\). The diagonals bisect each other, which is the definition of a parallelogram.

    (i) square   (ii) no quadrilateral   (iii) parallelogram

    Q7

    Find the point on the \(x\)-axis which is equidistant from \((2,-5)\) and \((-2,9)\).

    Solution

    Given: \(A(2,-5)\) and \(B(-2,9)\), and a point \(P\) on the \(x\)-axis with \(PA=PB\). To find: the coordinates of \(P\).

    Every point of the \(x\)-axis has ordinate 0, so let \(P(x,0)\). Since \(PA=PB\), we may square: \(PA^2=PB^2\).

    $$\begin{aligned} (x-2)^2+(0+5)^2&=(x+2)^2+(0-9)^2\\ x^2-4x+4+25&=x^2+4x+4+81\\ -4x+29&=4x+85\\ -8x&=56\\ x&=-7 \end{aligned}$$

    Check.

    $$\begin{aligned} PA&=\sqrt{(2+7)^2+(-5-0)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{81+25}=\sqrt{106}\\[3pt] PB&=\sqrt{(-2+7)^2+(9-0)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{25+81}=\sqrt{106} \end{aligned}$$

    The required point is \((-7,0)\).

    Q8

    Find the values of \(y\) for which the distance between the points \(P(2,-3)\) and \(Q(10,y)\) is 10 units.

    Solution

    Given: \(P(2,-3)\), \(Q(10,y)\) and \(PQ=10\). To find: the values of \(y\).

    Use the distance formula and square both sides to remove the root.

    $$\begin{aligned} PQ^2&=(10-2)^2+(y+3)^2\\ 10^2&=8^2+(y+3)^2\\ 100&=64+(y+3)^2\\ (y+3)^2&=36\\ y+3&=\pm 6 \end{aligned}$$

    Taking the two signs separately:

    $$\begin{aligned} y+3&=6 &&\Rightarrow\ y=3\\ y+3&=-6 &&\Rightarrow\ y=-9 \end{aligned}$$

    Check. For \(y=3\): \(PQ=\sqrt{8^2+6^2}=\sqrt{100}=10\ \checkmark\). For \(y=-9\): \(PQ=\sqrt{8^2+(-6)^2}=\sqrt{100}=10\ \checkmark\).

    \(y=3\) or \(y=-9\)

    Both answers are genuine — \(Q\) can be 6 units above or 6 units below the level of \(P\). Losing the negative root is the commonest mistake in this question.

    Q9

    \(Q(0,1)\) is equidistant from \(P(5,-3)\) and \(R(x,6)\). Find the values of \(x\). Also find the distances \(QR\) and \(PR\).

    Solution

    Given: \(P(5,-3)\), \(Q(0,1)\), \(R(x,6)\) with \(QP=QR\). To find: \(x\), then \(QR\) and \(PR\).

    Step 1 — find \(x\). Square the condition \(QP=QR\):

    $$\begin{aligned} QP^2&=QR^2\\ (5-0)^2+(-3-1)^2&=(x-0)^2+(6-1)^2\\ 25+16&=x^2+25\\ 41&=x^2+25\\ x^2&=16\\ x&=\pm 4 \end{aligned}$$

    Step 2 — the distance \(QR\). It is the same for both values of \(x\), because \(x^2=16\) either way:

    $$\begin{aligned} QR&=\sqrt{(\pm4-0)^2+(6-1)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{16+25}=\sqrt{41} \end{aligned}$$

    Step 3 — the distance \(PR\). Here the two cases differ, so take them one at a time.

    (a)

    If \(x=4\), then \(R(4,6)\):

    $$\begin{aligned} PR&=\sqrt{(4-5)^2+(6+3)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{1+81}=\sqrt{82} \end{aligned}$$
    (b)

    If \(x=-4\), then \(R(-4,6)\):

    $$\begin{aligned} PR&=\sqrt{(-4-5)^2+(6+3)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{81+81}=\sqrt{162}=9\sqrt2 \end{aligned}$$

    \(x=\pm4\); \(QR=\sqrt{41}\) in both cases; \(PR=\sqrt{82}\) when \(x=4\), and \(PR=9\sqrt2\) when \(x=-4\).

    \(QP=\sqrt{41}=QR\) is the check that the condition of the question really holds.

    Q10

    Find a relation between \(x\) and \(y\) such that the point \((x,y)\) is equidistant from the points \((3,6)\) and \((-3,4)\).

    Solution

    Given: \(P(x,y)\) with \(PA=PB\), where \(A(3,6)\) and \(B(-3,4)\). To find: a relation between \(x\) and \(y\).

    Square the condition first, so that no square roots appear:

    $$\begin{aligned} PA^2&=PB^2\\ (x-3)^2+(y-6)^2&=(x+3)^2+(y-4)^2 \end{aligned}$$

    Expand each side on its own:

    $$\begin{aligned} \text{LHS}&=x^2-6x+9+y^2-12y+36\\ \text{RHS}&=x^2+6x+9+y^2-8y+16 \end{aligned}$$

    Cancel \(x^2\) and \(y^2\) from both sides:

    $$\begin{aligned} -6x-12y+45&=6x-8y+25\\ -12x-4y+20&=0\\ 3x+y-5&=0 \end{aligned}$$

    Check. The mid-point of \(AB\) is \(\left(\dfrac{3-3}{2},\dfrac{6+4}{2}\right)=(0,5)\), and it must satisfy the relation: \(3(0)+5-5=0\ \checkmark\).

    \(3x+y-5=0\)

    The relation is a straight line — the perpendicular bisector of \(AB\). Any point on it is equidistant from \(A\) and \(B\), and no other point is.

    Exercise 7.2

    10 questions · the section formula, the mid-point formula, trisection, and finding the ratio in which a point divides a segment

    If \(P(x,y)\) divides the join of \(A(x_1,y_1)\) and \(B(x_2,y_2)\) internally in the ratio \(m_1:m_2\), then

    $$\begin{aligned} x&=\frac{m_1x_2+m_2x_1}{m_1+m_2}\\[3pt] y&=\frac{m_1y_2+m_2y_1}{m_1+m_2} \end{aligned}$$

    Written as \(k:1\), the same point is \(\left(\dfrac{kx_2+x_1}{k+1},\ \dfrac{ky_2+y_1}{k+1}\right)\) — one unknown instead of two, which is exactly what you want when the ratio is the thing to be found. Putting \(m_1:m_2=1:1\) gives the mid-point \(\left(\dfrac{x_1+x_2}{2},\ \dfrac{y_1+y_2}{2}\right)\).

    Cross over. \(m_1\) is the part next to \(A\), yet it multiplies \(x_2\), the coordinate of the far point \(B\). So the suffixes in the numerator run 1–2, 2–1: \(m_1x_2+m_2x_1\). Write the ratio above the segment before you substitute and you will never swap them.

    Q1

    Find the coordinates of the point that divides the line segment joining \((-1,\,7)\) and \((4,\,-3)\) in the ratio 2 : 3.

    Solution

    Given: \(A(-1,7)\), \(B(4,-3)\) and the ratio \(m_1:m_2=2:3\). To find: the point \(P(x,y)\) on \(AB\) with \(AP:PB=2:3\).

    Here \(x_1=-1,\ y_1=7,\ x_2=4,\ y_2=-3,\ m_1=2,\ m_2=3\). By the section formula,

    $$\begin{aligned} x&=\frac{m_1x_2+m_2x_1}{m_1+m_2}\\[2pt] &=\frac{2(4)+3(-1)}{2+3}\\[2pt] &=\frac{8-3}{5}=\frac55=1 \end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned} y&=\frac{m_1y_2+m_2y_1}{m_1+m_2}\\[2pt] &=\frac{2(-3)+3(7)}{2+3}\\[2pt] &=\frac{-6+21}{5}=\frac{15}{5}=3 \end{aligned}$$

    \(P(1,\,3)\)

    Quick check: \(2\lt 3\), so \(P\) must be closer to \(A\) — and it is, as the figure shows. If your answer comes out closer to \(B\), you have swapped \(m_1\) and \(m_2\).

    Q2

    Find the coordinates of the two points of trisection of the line segment joining \((4,\,-1)\) and \((-2,\,-3)\).

    Solution

    Given: \(A(4,-1)\) and \(B(-2,-3)\). To find: the points \(P\) and \(Q\) that cut \(AB\) into three equal parts, i.e. \(AP=PQ=QB\).

    Trisection means three equal pieces. So \(P\) is one piece from \(A\) and two pieces from \(B\), and \(Q\) is two pieces from \(A\) and one from \(B\):

    $$AP:PB=1:2,\qquad AQ:QB=2:1$$

    The point \(P\) (ratio 1 : 2).

    $$\begin{aligned} x&=\frac{1(-2)+2(4)}{1+2}\\ &=\frac{-2+8}{3}=\frac63=2\\[4pt] y&=\frac{1(-3)+2(-1)}{1+2}\\ &=\frac{-3-2}{3}=-\frac53 \end{aligned}$$

    The point \(Q\) (ratio 2 : 1).

    $$\begin{aligned} x&=\frac{2(-2)+1(4)}{2+1}\\ &=\frac{-4+4}{3}=\frac03=0\\[4pt] y&=\frac{2(-3)+1(-1)}{2+1}\\ &=\frac{-6-1}{3}=-\frac73 \end{aligned}$$

    \(P\left(2,\,-\dfrac53\right)\) and \(Q\left(0,\,-\dfrac73\right)\)

    Check: \(Q\) must be the mid-point of \(PB\). Mid-point of \(P\left(2,-\tfrac53\right)\) and \(B(-2,-3)\) is \(\left(\dfrac{2-2}{2},\ \dfrac{-\frac53-3}{2}\right)=\left(0,\ -\dfrac73\right)\) — it agrees. Once \(P\) is known you can always get \(Q\) this quicker way.

    Q3

    For Sports Day, the school's rectangular ground \(ABCD\) is marked out with chalk powder in straight lines 1 m apart, and 100 flower pots are set 1 m apart from one another all along the side \(AD\), as in the figure. Niharika runs along the 2nd line a distance equal to \(\dfrac14\) of \(AD\) and plants a green flag there. Preet runs along the 8th line a distance equal to \(\dfrac15\) of \(AD\) and plants a red flag.

    1. How far apart are the two flags?
    2. Rashmi wants to plant a blue flag exactly at the middle of the line segment joining the green and the red flag. On which line, and how far along it, should she plant it?
    Solution

    Set up the axes first. Take \(A\) as the origin, \(AB\) along the \(x\)-axis and \(AD\) along the \(y\)-axis, with 1 m as one unit on both axes. Then the \(n\)-th chalk line is the line \(x=n\), and how far a girl runs up that line is simply her \(y\)-coordinate.

    The 100 pots are 1 m apart all along \(AD\), so \(AD=100\) m.

    (i)

    The green flag \(G\). Niharika is on the 2nd line, so \(x=2\); she runs \(\dfrac14\times100=25\) m, so \(y=25\). Hence \(G(2,\,25)\).

    The red flag \(R\). Preet is on the 8th line, so \(x=8\); she runs \(\dfrac15\times100=20\) m, so \(y=20\). Hence \(R(8,\,20)\).

    By the distance formula,

    $$\begin{aligned} GR&=\sqrt{(8-2)^2+(20-25)^2}\\[2pt] &=\sqrt{6^2+(-5)^2}\\[2pt] &=\sqrt{36+25}=\sqrt{61}\ \text{m} \end{aligned}$$

    \(GR=\sqrt{61}\) m ≈ 7.81 m

    (ii)

    The blue flag \(S\) is the mid-point of \(GR\), so use the mid-point formula:

    $$\begin{aligned} S&=\left(\frac{2+8}{2},\ \frac{25+20}{2}\right)\\[2pt] &=\left(\frac{10}{2},\ \frac{45}{2}\right)=(5,\ 22.5) \end{aligned}$$

    An \(x\)-coordinate of 5 means the 5th line; a \(y\)-coordinate of 22.5 means 22.5 m along that line from \(AB\).

    On the 5th line, at a distance of 22.5 m from \(AB\)

    The 100 pots, 1 m apart, are just the question's way of telling you that \(AD\) measures 100 m — that is the reading the official answer uses. Nothing else about the pots is needed.

    In every "playground / field / classroom grid" question, write down your axes in one line before you compute anything: “\(A\) is the origin, \(AB\) is the \(x\)-axis, \(AD\) is the \(y\)-axis, 1 m = 1 unit.” It earns the first mark and it stops you mixing up line numbers with distances.

    Q4

    In what ratio does the point \((-1,\,6)\) divide the line segment joining the points \((-3,\,10)\) and \((6,\,-8)\)?

    Solution

    Given: \(A(-3,10)\), \(B(6,-8)\) and the dividing point \(P(-1,6)\). To find: the ratio \(AP:PB\).

    When the ratio is unknown, take it as \(k:1\) — that leaves one unknown instead of two. So

    $$P=\left(\frac{6k-3}{k+1},\ \frac{-8k+10}{k+1}\right)$$

    and this point is given to be \((-1,\,6)\).

    Two points are equal only when both coordinates match. Compare the \(x\)-coordinates:

    $$\begin{aligned} \frac{6k-3}{k+1}&=-1\\[2pt] 6k-3&=-(k+1)\\[2pt] 6k-3&=-k-1\\[2pt] 7k&=2\\[2pt] k&=\frac27 \end{aligned}$$

    So \(k:1=\dfrac27:1=2:7\).

    Verification with the \(y\)-coordinate. Put \(k=\dfrac27\):

    $$\begin{aligned} y&=\frac{-\frac{16}{7}+10}{\frac27+1}\\[3pt] &=\frac{-\frac{16}{7}+\frac{70}{7}}{\frac27+\frac77}\\[3pt] &=\frac{\ \frac{54}{7}\ }{\frac{9}{7}}=\frac{54}{9}=6\ \checkmark \end{aligned}$$

    The \(y\)-coordinate comes out 6, exactly as given, so \(P\) really does lie on \(AB\) and the ratio is correct.

    \(AP:PB=2:7\)

    Always finish by checking the other coordinate. If the \(y\)-coordinate had not come out 6, it would mean \(P\) does not lie on \(AB\) at all and no ratio exists.

    Q5

    Find the ratio in which the line segment joining \(A(1,\,-5)\) and \(B(-4,\,5)\) is divided by the \(x\)-axis. Also find the coordinates of the point of division.

    Solution

    Given: \(A(1,-5)\), \(B(-4,5)\). To find: the ratio in which the \(x\)-axis cuts \(AB\), and the point where it cuts.

    Let the \(x\)-axis cut \(AB\) at \(P\) in the ratio \(k:1\). By the section formula,

    $$P=\left(\frac{-4k+1}{k+1},\ \frac{5k-5}{k+1}\right)$$

    Every point of the \(x\)-axis has \(y=0\). So the \(y\)-coordinate of \(P\) must be 0:

    $$\begin{aligned} \frac{5k-5}{k+1}&=0\\[2pt] 5k-5&=0\\[2pt] k&=1 \end{aligned}$$

    Hence the ratio is \(k:1=1:1\). Putting \(k=1\) in the \(x\)-coordinate,

    $$x=\frac{-4(1)+1}{1+1}=\frac{-3}{2}$$

    Ratio 1 : 1; the point of division is \(\left(-\dfrac32,\,0\right)\)

    A ratio of 1 : 1 simply means the \(x\)-axis passes through the mid-point of \(AB\) — which had to happen, because \(A\) and \(B\) are the same distance (5 units) from the axis, on opposite sides of it.

    Same trick for the other axis: every point of the \(y\)-axis has \(x=0\), so there you set the \(x\)-coordinate of \(P\) equal to 0 instead.

    Q6

    The points \((1,\,2)\), \((4,\,y)\), \((x,\,6)\) and \((3,\,5)\), taken in that order, are the vertices of a parallelogram. Find \(x\) and \(y\).

    Solution

    Given: \(A(1,2)\), \(B(4,y)\), \(C(x,6)\), \(D(3,5)\) are the vertices of parallelogram \(ABCD\), named in order. To find: \(x\) and \(y\).

    Because the vertices are taken in order, the diagonals are \(AC\) and \(BD\).

    The diagonals of a parallelogram bisect each other, so they have the same mid-point: [property of a parallelogram]

    $$\text{mid-point of }AC=\text{mid-point of }BD$$ $$\left(\frac{1+x}{2},\ \frac{2+6}{2}\right)=\left(\frac{4+3}{2},\ \frac{y+5}{2}\right)$$

    Comparing the two \(x\)-coordinates:

    $$\begin{aligned} \frac{1+x}{2}&=\frac72\\[2pt] 1+x&=7\\[2pt] x&=6 \end{aligned}$$

    Comparing the two \(y\)-coordinates:

    $$\begin{aligned} \frac{2+6}{2}&=\frac{y+5}{2}\\[2pt] 8&=y+5\\[2pt] y&=3 \end{aligned}$$

    \(x=6,\quad y=3\)

    Check with the sides: the vertices are \(A(1,2),B(4,3),C(6,6),D(3,5)\). Going from \(A\) to \(B\) you move 3 right and 1 up, and from \(D\) to \(C\) you also move 3 right and 1 up — so \(AB\parallel DC\) and \(AB=DC\), which is exactly what a parallelogram needs.

    Order matters. "Taken in order" tells you the sides are \(AB,BC,CD,DA\) and the diagonals are \(AC\) and \(BD\). If you pair up \(AB\) and \(CD\) as diagonals by mistake, you get a different (wrong) pair of equations.

    Q7

    \(AB\) is a diameter of a circle whose centre is \((2,\,-3)\), and \(B\) is the point \((1,\,4)\). Find the coordinates of \(A\).

    Solution

    Given: centre \(O(2,-3)\), one end of the diameter \(B(1,4)\). To find: the other end \(A(x,y)\).

    The centre of a circle is the mid-point of every diameter. So \(O\) is the mid-point of \(AB\):

    $$\left(\frac{x+1}{2},\ \frac{y+4}{2}\right)=(2,\ -3)$$

    Comparing the \(x\)-coordinates:

    $$\begin{aligned} \frac{x+1}{2}&=2\\[2pt] x+1&=4\\[2pt] x&=3 \end{aligned}$$

    Comparing the \(y\)-coordinates:

    $$\begin{aligned} \frac{y+4}{2}&=-3\\[2pt] y+4&=-6\\[2pt] y&=-10 \end{aligned}$$

    \(A(3,\,-10)\)

    Check with the radius: \(OB=\sqrt{(2-1)^2+(-3-4)^2}=\sqrt{1+49}=5\sqrt2\) and \(OA=\sqrt{(3-2)^2+(-10+3)^2}=\sqrt{1+49}=5\sqrt2\). Equal, as they must be.

    Q8

    The points \(A\) and \(B\) are \((-2,\,-2)\) and \((2,\,-4)\). The point \(P\) lies on the line segment \(AB\) and \(AP=\dfrac37\,AB\). Find the coordinates of \(P\).

    Solution

    Given: \(A(-2,-2)\), \(B(2,-4)\), \(P\) on \(AB\) with \(AP=\dfrac37AB\). To find: the coordinates of \(P\).

    Step 1 — turn the fraction into a ratio. \(AB\) is made of 7 equal parts and \(AP\) takes 3 of them, so \(PB\) takes the remaining 4:

    $$\begin{aligned} PB&=AB-AP\\[2pt] &=AB-\frac37AB=\frac47AB \end{aligned}$$ $$AP:PB=\frac37AB:\frac47AB=3:4$$

    Step 2 — apply the section formula with \(m_1:m_2=3:4\).

    $$\begin{aligned} x&=\frac{3(2)+4(-2)}{3+4}\\[2pt] &=\frac{6-8}{7}=-\frac27 \end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned} y&=\frac{3(-4)+4(-2)}{3+4}\\[2pt] &=\frac{-12-8}{7}=-\frac{20}{7} \end{aligned}$$

    \(P\left(-\dfrac27,\ -\dfrac{20}{7}\right)\)

    The one mistake everybody makes here. \(AP:AB=3:7\), but the section formula needs \(AP:PB\), which is \(3:4\). Substituting \(3:7\) gives \(\left(-\tfrac45,\,-\tfrac{13}{5}\right)\), which is only \(\tfrac{3}{10}\) of the way along \(AB\) instead of \(\tfrac37\). Whenever a question gives \(AP\) as a fraction of \(AB\), subtract first to get \(PB\).

    Q9

    Find the coordinates of the points that divide the line segment joining \(A(-2,\,2)\) and \(B(2,\,8)\) into four equal parts.

    Solution

    Given: \(A(-2,2)\) and \(B(2,8)\). To find: the three points \(P,\ Q,\ R\) with \(AP=PQ=QR=RB\).

    Four equal parts need three dividing points. Counting the parts from \(A\):

    $$\begin{aligned} AP:PB&=1:3\\[2pt] AQ:QB&=2:2=1:1\\[2pt] AR:RB&=3:1 \end{aligned}$$

    The point \(P\) (ratio 1 : 3).

    $$\begin{aligned} x&=\frac{1(2)+3(-2)}{1+3}=\frac{2-6}{4}=-1\\[3pt] y&=\frac{1(8)+3(2)}{1+3}=\frac{8+6}{4}=\frac72 \end{aligned}$$

    The point \(Q\) (ratio 1 : 1, so it is the mid-point of \(AB\)).

    $$\begin{aligned} x&=\frac{-2+2}{2}=0\\[3pt] y&=\frac{2+8}{2}=5 \end{aligned}$$

    The point \(R\) (ratio 3 : 1).

    $$\begin{aligned} x&=\frac{3(2)+1(-2)}{3+1}=\frac{6-2}{4}=1\\[3pt] y&=\frac{3(8)+1(2)}{3+1}=\frac{24+2}{4}=\frac{13}{2} \end{aligned}$$

    \(P\left(-1,\ \dfrac72\right)\), \(Q(0,\,5)\), \(R\left(1,\ \dfrac{13}{2}\right)\)

    Shorter route: find \(Q\), the mid-point of \(AB\), first. Then \(P\) is the mid-point of \(AQ\) and \(R\) is the mid-point of \(QB\) — three mid-point calculations and no ratios at all. The \(x\)-coordinates \(-2,-1,0,1,2\) and the \(y\)-coordinates \(2,\tfrac72,5,\tfrac{13}{2},8\) both step up evenly, which is the sign that the four parts really are equal.

    Q10

    The points \((3,\,0)\), \((4,\,5)\), \((-1,\,4)\) and \((-2,\,-1)\), taken in order, are the vertices of a rhombus. Find its area.

    Area of a rhombus \(=\dfrac12\times(\text{product of its diagonals})\).

    Solution

    Given: the rhombus \(ABCD\) with \(A(3,0)\), \(B(4,5)\), \(C(-1,4)\), \(D(-2,-1)\). To find: its area.

    Step 1 — confirm it is a rhombus (all four sides equal), using the distance formula.

    $$\begin{aligned} AB&=\sqrt{(4-3)^2+(5-0)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{1+25}=\sqrt{26}\\[4pt] BC&=\sqrt{(-1-4)^2+(4-5)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{25+1}=\sqrt{26} \end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned} CD&=\sqrt{(-2+1)^2+(-1-4)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{1+25}=\sqrt{26}\\[4pt] DA&=\sqrt{(3+2)^2+(0+1)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{25+1}=\sqrt{26} \end{aligned}$$

    All four sides are \(\sqrt{26}\) units, so \(ABCD\) is indeed a rhombus.

    Step 2 — the two diagonals \(AC\) and \(BD\) (the vertices are in order, so these are the diagonals).

    $$\begin{aligned} AC&=\sqrt{(-1-3)^2+(4-0)^2}\\[2pt] &=\sqrt{16+16}=\sqrt{32}=4\sqrt2 \end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned} BD&=\sqrt{(-2-4)^2+(-1-5)^2}\\[2pt] &=\sqrt{36+36}=\sqrt{72}=6\sqrt2 \end{aligned}$$

    Step 3 — the area.

    $$\begin{aligned} \text{Area}&=\frac12\times AC\times BD\\[2pt] &=\frac12\times4\sqrt2\times6\sqrt2\\[2pt] &=\frac12\times24\times2=24 \end{aligned}$$

    Area = 24 square units

    \(\sqrt2\times\sqrt2=2\), so the surds cancel neatly: \(\tfrac12\times4\times6\times2=24\). Do not leave the answer as \(12\sqrt2\cdot\sqrt2\) — simplify.

    Board insight. This is a 3-mark question and the marks usually split as: 1 for the two diagonal lengths, 1 for stating and substituting in \(\tfrac12d_1d_2\), 1 for the answer with the unit "square units". Showing that all four sides are equal costs one extra line and protects you if the examiner asks you to justify the word "rhombus".

    $$\begin{aligned}AB&=\sqrt{(5-1)^2+(4-2)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{16+4}=\sqrt{20},\\ BC&=\sqrt{(3-5)^2+(8-4)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{4+16}=\sqrt{20},\\ CD&=\sqrt{(-1-3)^2+(6-8)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{16+4}=\sqrt{20},\\ DA&=\sqrt{(1+1)^2+(2-6)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{4+16}=\sqrt{20}.\end{aligned}$$

    All four sides are equal: \(AB=BC=CD=DA=2\sqrt5\).

    Step 2 — the two diagonals.

    $$\begin{aligned}AC&=\sqrt{(3-1)^2+(8-2)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{4+36}=\sqrt{40},\\ BD&=\sqrt{(-1-5)^2+(6-4)^2}\\ &=\sqrt{36+4}=\sqrt{40}.\end{aligned}$$

    The diagonals are also equal: \(AC=BD=2\sqrt{10}\).

    Step 3 — conclusion. A quadrilateral with all four sides equal and both diagonals equal is a square. Hence \(ABCD\) is a square.

    Step 4 — area. Area \(=(\text{side})^2=(2\sqrt5)^2=20\).

    \(ABCD\) is a square of area 20 square units

    Second check: \(AB^2+BC^2=20+20=40=AC^2\), so \(\angle B=90^\circ\) — a rhombus with one right angle is a square.

    2 marks for the four sides · 1½ for the two diagonals · ½ for the conclusion · 1 for the area.

    Show that the points \(A(-2,\,3)\), \(B(0,-1)\), \(C(8,\,3)\) and \(D(6,\,7)\), taken in order, are the vertices of a rectangle. Find its area.

    Answer & solution

    The vertices of a triangle are \(A(2,\,1)\), \(B(5,\,4)\) and \(C(-1,\,4)\).

    Coordinate Geometry

    Points and the axes

    • Abscissa \(x\) = distance from the \(y\)-axis
    • Ordinate \(y\) = distance from the \(x\)-axis
    • On the \(x\)-axis: \((x,\,0)\) · on the \(y\)-axis: \((0,\,y)\)

    Distance formula

    • \(PQ=\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2}\)
    • From the origin: \(\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\)
    • Always positive — take the \(+\) root

    Naming a figure

    • All 4 sides equal + equal diagonals → square
    • All 4 sides equal, diagonals unequal → rhombus
    • Opposite sides equal + equal diagonals → rectangle

    Equidistant conditions

    • Write \(PA^2=PB^2\), never \(PA=PB\) with roots
    • On an axis: take \((x,0)\) or \((0,y)\)
    • The relation in \(x,y\) is the perpendicular bisector of \(AB\)

    Section formula

    • \(\left(\dfrac{m_1x_2+m_2x_1}{m_1+m_2},\ \dfrac{m_1y_2+m_2y_1}{m_1+m_2}\right)\)
    • Cross pattern: \(m_1\) goes with \(x_2\)
    • \(k:1\) form when the ratio is unknown

    Mid-point and its uses

    • \(\left(\dfrac{x_1+x_2}{2},\ \dfrac{y_1+y_2}{2}\right)\)
    • Diagonals of a parallelogram bisect each other
    • Centre of a circle = mid-point of a diameter

    Collinearity

    • Find all three distances
    • Collinear ⇔ the two smaller add up to the largest
    • No triangle is formed in that case

    Formulas to remember

    • Distance formula\(PQ=\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2}\)
    • Distance from the origin\(OP=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\)
    • From the axesDistance of \((x,y)\) from the \(x\)-axis is \(|y|\); from the \(y\)-axis it is \(|x|\).
    • Section formula (ratio \(m_1:m_2\))\(\left(\dfrac{m_1x_2+m_2x_1}{m_1+m_2},\ \dfrac{m_1y_2+m_2y_1}{m_1+m_2}\right)\)
    • Section formula (ratio \(k:1\))\(\left(\dfrac{kx_2+x_1}{k+1},\ \dfrac{ky_2+y_1}{k+1}\right)\) — use it when the ratio is unknown.
    • Mid-point\(\left(\dfrac{x_1+x_2}{2},\ \dfrac{y_1+y_2}{2}\right)\)
    • Points of trisectionRatios \(1:2\) and \(2:1\).
    • Fourth vertex of a parallelogram \(ABCD\)Mid-point of \(AC\) = mid-point of \(BD\).
    • Ends of a diameterThe centre is their mid-point: \(x_2=2h-x_1,\ y_2=2k-y_1\).
    • Area of a rhombus\(\dfrac12\times d_1\times d_2\) (its two diagonals).

    Mistakes that cost marks

    • Writing \((x_2-x_1)^2-(y_2-y_1)^2\) or forgetting the square root.The distance formula adds the two squares, and the final step is a square root.
    • Mixing up abscissa and ordinate: “distance from the \(x\)-axis is \(x\)”.Distance from the \(x\)-axis is \(|y|\); a point on the \(x\)-axis is \((x,\,0)\).
    • In the section formula, writing \(\dfrac{m_1x_1+m_2x_2}{m_1+m_2}\).The multiplication is crossed: \(m_1\) goes with \(x_2\) and \(m_2\) with \(x_1\).
    • Answering \(2:1\) when the ratio is \(1:2\).Keep the order: the first number belongs to the first named point.
    • Reading \(AP=\frac25AB\) as \(AP:PB=2:5\).\(AB\) is the whole segment, so \(AP:PB=2:3\).
    • Giving only one value when \(k^2=16\) or \((6-a)^2=9\).A square root gives two values — write both unless the question rules one out.
    • Calling \(ABCD\) a square as soon as the four sides are equal.Check the diagonals too: equal → square, unequal → rhombus.
    • Pairing \(AB\) and \(CD\) as the diagonals of parallelogram \(ABCD\).In \(ABCD\) the diagonals are \(AC\) and \(BD\) — read the order of the letters.
