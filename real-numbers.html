    1.1 Where this chapter fits

    In Class IX you met the real numbers — the rationals and the irrationals together. This chapter goes one level deeper. It asks two sharp questions: what is every whole number made of? and how can we be certain that a number like \(\sqrt 2\) is irrational?

    Both answers come from one powerful fact about multiplication, the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic. Once you own that theorem, three exam skills follow almost for free:

    • Prime factorisation

      Break any composite number into primes — quickly and without mistakes.

    • HCF and LCM

      Read them straight off the prime factors, then use them in word problems.

    • Irrationality proofs

      Prove that \(\sqrt2,\ \sqrt3,\ \sqrt5\) and numbers such as \(3+2\sqrt5\) are irrational.

    Prime number: a natural number greater than 1 with exactly two factors, 1 and itself (2, 3, 5, 7, 11, …). Composite number: a natural number greater than 1 that has more than two factors (4, 6, 8, 9, 10, …). The number 1 is neither prime nor composite, and 2 is the only even prime.

    Coprime numbers: two numbers whose only common factor is 1, for example 8 and 15. Coprime numbers need not be prime themselves.

    1.2 The Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic

    Pick any handful of primes, say 2, 3, 7, 11 and 23. Multiply some of them, repeating a prime as often as you like, and you get a composite number: \(7\times11\times23=1771\), \(2^2\times3\times7\times11\times23=21252\), and so on. Different choices give different numbers.

    Now run the film backwards. Start from a composite number and keep splitting it into factors until only primes are left. A factor tree does exactly this.

    However you draw the tree — start with \(10\times3276\) or with \(8\times4095\) — you end with the same collection of primes. That observation is the theorem.

    Theorem 1.1Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic

    Every composite number can be expressed (factorised) as a product of primes, and this factorisation is unique, apart from the order in which the prime factors occur.

    So we treat \(2\times3\times5\times7\) and \(7\times3\times5\times2\) as the same factorisation. If we agree to write the primes in ascending order and collect equal primes into powers, a composite number \(x\) has exactly one form

    $$x = p_1^{\,a_1}\,p_2^{\,a_2}\cdots p_n^{\,a_n},\qquad p_1 \lt p_2 \lt \cdots \lt p_n \text{ primes}.$$

    The theorem says two things. Existence: a prime factorisation always exists. Uniqueness: there is only one. Most exam questions in this chapter quietly use the uniqueness part — “the only primes in \(4^n\) are 2s, so 5 can never appear”.

    How to factorise fast

    1. Divide by 2 as long as the number is even.
    2. Then try 3 (digit sum divisible by 3), then 5 (ends in 0 or 5), then 7, 11, 13, … in order.
    3. Stop when the quotient is itself prime. You never need to test a prime bigger than the square root of what is left.

    NCERT Example 1uniqueness in action

    Take the numbers \(4^n\), with \(n\) a natural number. Is there any value of \(n\) for which \(4^n\) ends with the digit zero? Justify your answer.

    Solution

    A number that ends with the digit 0 is divisible by 10, so it is divisible by 5. Its prime factorisation must therefore contain the prime 5.

    But \(4^n = (2^2)^n = 2^{2n}\). The only prime in the factorisation of \(4^n\) is 2.

    By the uniqueness part of the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic, \(4^n\) has no other prime factors, so 5 is not a factor of \(4^n\).

    There is no natural number \(n\) for which \(4^n\) ends with the digit zero.

    “Can \(6^n\) / \(12^n\) / \(15^n\) end with the digit 0 (or 5)?” is a board regular. The test is always the same: to end in 0 a number needs both 2 and 5 as prime factors; to end in 5 it needs 5 and must not have 2. Write the prime factorisation of the base, then quote uniqueness.

    HCF and LCM from prime factors

    HCF — Highest Common Factor

    The largest number that divides each of the given numbers.

    Rule: product of the smallest power of each common prime factor.

    LCM — Least Common Multiple

    The smallest number that each of the given numbers divides.

    Rule: product of the greatest power of every prime factor that appears.

    NCERT Example 2prime factorisation method

    Use prime factorisation to find the HCF and the LCM of 6 and 20.

    Solution

    \(6 = 2^1\times3^1\) and \(20 = 2\times2\times5 = 2^2\times5^1\).

    The only common prime is 2, and its smallest power is \(2^1\). So \(\text{HCF}(6,20)=2\).

    All primes that appear are 2, 3 and 5, with greatest powers \(2^2,\ 3^1,\ 5^1\). So \(\text{LCM}(6,20)=2^2\times3\times5=60\).

    HCF = 2, LCM = 60

    Notice that \(\text{HCF}\times\text{LCM}=2\times60=120=6\times20\).

    Product rule for two numbers
    $$\text{HCF}(a,b)\times\text{LCM}(a,b)=a\times b$$

    True for any two positive integers \(a\) and \(b\). So if you know the HCF, then \(\text{LCM}=\dfrac{a\times b}{\text{HCF}}\), and the other way round.

    NCERT Example 3HCF first, then LCM

    Find the HCF of 96 and 404 from their prime factors, and then use it to get their LCM.

    Solution

    \(96 = 2^5\times3\) and \(404 = 2^2\times101\).

    The only common prime is 2 and its smallest power is \(2^2\). Therefore \(\text{HCF}(96,404)=2^2=4\).

    $$\text{LCM}(96,404)=\frac{96\times404}{\text{HCF}(96,404)}=\frac{96\times404}{4}=24\times404=9696$$

    HCF = 4, LCM = 9696

    NCERT Example 4three numbers

    Using prime factorisation, find the HCF and the LCM of the three numbers 6, 72 and 120.

    Solution

    \(6=2\times3,\qquad 72=2^3\times3^2,\qquad 120=2^3\times3\times5.\)

    Common primes are 2 and 3; their smallest powers are \(2^1\) and \(3^1\). So \(\text{HCF}(6,72,120)=2\times3=6\).

    The primes involved are 2, 3 and 5; their greatest powers are \(2^3,\ 3^2,\ 5^1\). So \(\text{LCM}(6,72,120)=2^3\times3^2\times5=360\).

    HCF = 6, LCM = 360

    The product rule works for two numbers only. For three numbers it fails: \(6\times72\times120=51840\) but \(\text{HCF}\times\text{LCM}=6\times360=2160\). Never write \(\text{HCF}\times\text{LCM}=p\times q\times r\).

    For three numbers \(p,\,q,\,r\) the correct relations are

    $$\text{LCM}(p,q,r)=\frac{p\,q\,r\;\text{HCF}(p,q,r)}{\text{HCF}(p,q)\,\text{HCF}(q,r)\,\text{HCF}(p,r)},\qquad \text{HCF}(p,q,r)=\frac{p\,q\,r\;\text{LCM}(p,q,r)}{\text{LCM}(p,q)\,\text{LCM}(q,r)\,\text{LCM}(p,r)}.$$

    Word problems: is it HCF or LCM?

    Most students lose marks here not in the arithmetic but in choosing the wrong tool. Read the question for these signals.

    Use HCF when you are splitting

    • “largest / greatest / maximum” size, length, number of groups
    • equal groups or stacks with nothing left over
    • largest tile, longest tape, maximum number of columns or rows
    • “leaves remainder \(r\)”: subtract the remainders first, then take the HCF

    Use LCM when things repeat

    • “least / smallest / minimum” quantity that works for all
    • bells toll, lights change, runners meet “together again”
    • smallest number divisible by each of …
    • “leaves remainder \(r\) in each case”: take the LCM, then add \(r\)

    Three friends step off together with steps of 40 cm, 42 cm and 45 cm. What minimum distance should each walk so that all cover it in complete steps?

    Show answer

    “Minimum distance … complete steps” means the smallest common multiple. \(40=2^3\times5,\ 42=2\times3\times7,\ 45=3^2\times5\). LCM \(=2^3\times3^2\times5\times7=2520\) cm, that is 25.2 m.

    1.3 Revisiting irrational numbers

    Definition

    A number \(s\) is called irrational if it cannot be written in the form \(\dfrac pq\), where \(p\) and \(q\) are integers and \(q\ne0\). Examples: \(\sqrt2,\ \sqrt3,\ \sqrt{15},\ \pi,\ -\dfrac{\sqrt2}{\sqrt3},\ 0.10110111011110\ldots\)

    In Class IX you located \(\sqrt2\) on the number line and were told it is irrational. Now we prove it. The proof needs one small tool first.

    Theorem 1.2a prime that divides a² also divides a

    Let \(p\) be a prime number. If \(p\) divides \(a^2\), then \(p\) divides \(a\), where \(a\) is a positive integer.

    Proof (for understanding — not asked in the exam)

    Let the prime factorisation of \(a\) be \(a=p_1p_2\cdots p_n\), where the primes need not be distinct. Then

    $$a^2=(p_1p_2\cdots p_n)(p_1p_2\cdots p_n)=p_1^2\,p_2^2\cdots p_n^2.$$

    We are given that \(p\) divides \(a^2\), so \(p\) is one of the prime factors of \(a^2\). By the uniqueness part of the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic, the only prime factors of \(a^2\) are \(p_1,p_2,\ldots,p_n\). So \(p\) is one of \(p_1,p_2,\ldots,p_n\).

    Since \(a=p_1p_2\cdots p_n\), it follows that \(p\) divides \(a\). Proved.

    You may quote Theorem 1.2 in the exam without proving it: “3 divides \(a^2\), therefore 3 divides \(a\)”. The theorem needs \(p\) to be prime — 4 divides \(6^2=36\) but 4 does not divide 6.

    Proof by contradiction

    To prove a statement, assume the opposite is true and follow the logic honestly. If you land on something impossible, the assumption must have been wrong — so the original statement is true.

    Theorem 1.3√2 is irrational

    \(\sqrt2\) is irrational.

    Proof (learn this — it is asked directly)

    Let us assume, to the contrary, that \(\sqrt2\) is rational.

    Then we can find integers \(r\) and \(s\) \((s\ne0)\) such that \(\sqrt2=\dfrac rs\). If \(r\) and \(s\) have a common factor other than 1, we divide by it and get \(\sqrt2=\dfrac ab\), where \(a\) and \(b\) are coprime.

    So \(b\sqrt2=a\). Squaring both sides, \(2b^2=a^2\).

    Therefore 2 divides \(a^2\), and by Theorem 1.2, 2 divides \(a\). So we can write \(a=2c\) for some integer \(c\).

    Substituting, \(2b^2=(2c)^2=4c^2\), that is, \(b^2=2c^2\).

    This means 2 divides \(b^2\), and so 2 divides \(b\) (again by Theorem 1.2 with \(p=2\)).

    Therefore \(a\) and \(b\) have at least 2 as a common factor. But this contradicts the fact that \(a\) and \(b\) are coprime.

    This contradiction has arisen because of our incorrect assumption that \(\sqrt2\) is rational. So we conclude that \(\sqrt2\) is irrational. Proved.

    NCERT Example 5same proof, p = 3

    Prove that \(\sqrt3\) is irrational.

    Solution

    Let us assume, to the contrary, that \(\sqrt3\) is rational. Then we can find integers \(a\) and \(b\) \((b\ne0)\) such that \(\sqrt3=\dfrac ab\). Suppose \(a\) and \(b\) have a common factor other than 1; we divide by it and assume that \(a\) and \(b\) are coprime.

    So \(b\sqrt3=a\). Squaring both sides, \(3b^2=a^2\).

    Therefore \(a^2\) is divisible by 3, and by Theorem 1.2, \(a\) is also divisible by 3. Write \(a=3c\) for some integer \(c\).

    Substituting, \(3b^2=9c^2\), that is, \(b^2=3c^2\). So \(b^2\) is divisible by 3, and hence \(b\) is divisible by 3.

    Thus \(a\) and \(b\) have at least 3 as a common factor, which contradicts the fact that \(a\) and \(b\) are coprime.

    This contradiction has arisen because of our incorrect assumption that \(\sqrt3\) is rational. Hence \(\sqrt3\) is irrational. Hence proved.

    Assume rational → Fraction \(\frac ab\), coprime → Square → Divides \(a\) → Substitute \(a=pc\) → Divides \(b\) → Contradiction. Say it as “A Fox Saw Ducks, Saw Ducks, Cried”. For \(\sqrt5\), \(\sqrt7\), \(\sqrt{11}\) only the prime changes.

    Sums and products with an irrational number

    From Class IX: the sum or difference of a rational and an irrational number is irrational, and the product or quotient of a non-zero rational and an irrational number is irrational. The next two examples show how to prove particular cases. The trick is always to isolate the surd on one side.

    NCERT Example 6rational − irrational

    Show that \(5-\sqrt3\) is irrational.

    Solution

    Let us assume, to the contrary, that \(5-\sqrt3\) is rational. Then we can find coprime integers \(a\) and \(b\) \((b\ne0)\) such that \(5-\sqrt3=\dfrac ab\).

    Rearranging, \(\sqrt3=5-\dfrac ab=\dfrac{5b-a}{b}\).

    Since \(a\) and \(b\) are integers, \(\dfrac{5b-a}{b}\) is rational, and so \(\sqrt3\) is rational.

    But this contradicts the fact that \(\sqrt3\) is irrational. The contradiction has arisen because of our incorrect assumption that \(5-\sqrt3\) is rational.

    So \(5-\sqrt3\) is irrational. Hence proved.

    NCERT Example 7rational × irrational

    Show that \(3\sqrt2\) is irrational.

    Solution

    Let us assume, to the contrary, that \(3\sqrt2\) is rational. Then we can find coprime integers \(a\) and \(b\) \((b\ne0)\) such that \(3\sqrt2=\dfrac ab\).

    Rearranging, \(\sqrt2=\dfrac{a}{3b}\).

    Since 3, \(a\) and \(b\) are integers, \(\dfrac{a}{3b}\) is rational, and so \(\sqrt2\) is rational.

    But this contradicts the fact that \(\sqrt2\) is irrational. So our assumption is wrong, and \(3\sqrt2\) is irrational. Hence proved.

    Read the wording. “Prove that \(3+2\sqrt5\) is irrational, given that \(\sqrt5\) is irrational” is a 2-mark question: isolate \(\sqrt5\) and finish in five lines. If the words “given that” are missing, it is a 3-mark question and you must first prove \(\sqrt5\) is irrational, then do the second part.

    The sum or product of two irrationals can be rational: \((2+\sqrt3)+(2-\sqrt3)=4\) and \(\sqrt2\times\sqrt8=4\). So never claim “irrational + irrational = irrational”.

    7 questions · prime factorisation, HCF and LCM, uniqueness of factorisation

    Q1

    Write each number as a product of prime factors:

    1. 140
    2. 156
    3. 3825
    4. 5005
    5. 7429
    Solution

    Method: divide by the smallest prime that works, again and again, until the quotient is 1 (the ladder method).

    (i)
    $$\begin{array}{r|l} 2 & 140\\ \hline 2 & 70\\ \hline 5 & 35\\ \hline 7 & 7\\ \hline & 1 \end{array}$$

    \(140 = 2\times2\times5\times7 = 2^2\times5\times7\)

    (ii)

    \(156 = 2\times78 = 2\times2\times39 = 2\times2\times3\times13\)

    \(156 = 2^2\times3\times13\)

    (iii)

    Digit sum \(3+8+2+5=18\), so 3 divides 3825. \(3825 = 3\times1275 = 3\times3\times425 = 3\times3\times5\times85 = 3\times3\times5\times5\times17\)

    \(3825 = 3^2\times5^2\times17\)

    (iv)

    \(5005 = 5\times1001 = 5\times7\times143 = 5\times7\times11\times13\)

    \(5005 = 5\times7\times11\times13\)

    (v)

    7429 is not divisible by 2, 3, 5, 7, 11 or 13. Trying 17: \(7429 = 17\times437\), and \(437 = 19\times23\).

    \(7429 = 17\times19\times23\)

    Q2

    For each pair of integers, find the LCM and the HCF. Then check that LCM × HCF equals the product of the two numbers.

    1. 26 and 91
    2. 510 and 92
    3. 336 and 54
    Solution
    (i)

    \(26 = 2\times13,\qquad 91 = 7\times13\)

    HCF = 13 (the only common prime). LCM \(= 2\times7\times13 = 182\).

    Verification: LCM × HCF \(= 182\times13 = 2366\) and \(26\times91 = 2366\). Both are equal. ✓

    (ii)

    \(510 = 2\times3\times5\times17,\qquad 92 = 2^2\times23\)

    HCF = 2. LCM \(= 2^2\times3\times5\times17\times23 = 23460\).

    Verification: LCM × HCF \(= 23460\times2 = 46920\) and \(510\times92 = 46920\). ✓

    (iii)

    \(336 = 2^4\times3\times7,\qquad 54 = 2\times3^3\)

    HCF \(= 2\times3 = 6\). LCM \(= 2^4\times3^3\times7 = 3024\).

    Verification: LCM × HCF \(= 3024\times6 = 18144\) and \(336\times54 = 18144\). ✓

    (i) LCM 182, HCF 13   (ii) LCM 23460, HCF 2   (iii) LCM 3024, HCF 6

    Q3

    Use the prime factorisation method to find the LCM and the HCF of:

    1. 12, 15 and 21
    2. 17, 23 and 29
    3. 8, 9 and 25
    Solution
    (i)

    \(12 = 2^2\times3,\quad 15 = 3\times5,\quad 21 = 3\times7\)

    Only 3 is common to all three, so HCF = 3. LCM \(= 2^2\times3\times5\times7 = 420\).

    (ii)

    17, 23 and 29 are all primes: \(17 = 17,\ 23 = 23,\ 29 = 29\). They have no common prime factor, so HCF = 1.

    LCM \(= 17\times23\times29 = 11339\).

    (iii)

    \(8 = 2^3,\quad 9 = 3^2,\quad 25 = 5^2\). No prime is common, so HCF = 1.

    LCM \(= 2^3\times3^2\times5^2 = 8\times9\times25 = 1800\).

    (i) LCM 420, HCF 3   (ii) LCM 11339, HCF 1   (iii) LCM 1800, HCF 1

    When the numbers are pairwise coprime, as in (ii) and (iii), the HCF is 1 and the LCM is simply their product.

    Q4

    You are told that HCF (306, 657) = 9. Find LCM (306, 657).

    Solution

    For two positive integers, \(\text{HCF}\times\text{LCM} = \text{product of the numbers}\).

    $$\text{LCM}(306,657)=\frac{306\times657}{\text{HCF}(306,657)}=\frac{306\times657}{9}=34\times657=22338$$

    LCM (306, 657) = 22338

    Q5

    Can \(6^n\) end with the digit 0 for some natural number \(n\)? Check.

    Solution

    If \(6^n\) ends with the digit 0 for some \(n\), then \(6^n\) is divisible by 10, and hence by 5. So the prime factorisation of \(6^n\) must contain the prime 5.

    But \(6^n = (2\times3)^n = 2^n\times3^n\). The only primes in the factorisation of \(6^n\) are 2 and 3.

    By the uniqueness of the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic, there are no other primes in the factorisation of \(6^n\). So 5 is not a factor of \(6^n\).

    No. There is no natural number \(n\) for which \(6^n\) ends with the digit 0.

    Q6

    Give reasons why each of these is a composite number: \(7\times11\times13+13\) and \(7\times6\times5\times4\times3\times2\times1+5\).

    Solution

    A composite number has at least one factor other than 1 and itself. So it is enough to write each number as a product of two factors, both greater than 1.

    First number. Take 13 common:

    $$7\times11\times13+13 = 13\,(7\times11+1) = 13\times78 = 13\times2\times3\times13.$$

    It is a product of primes \(2\times3\times13^2\), so it has factors other than 1 and itself. Hence it is composite.

    Second number. Take 5 common:

    $$7\times6\times5\times4\times3\times2\times1+5 = 5\,(7\times6\times4\times3\times2\times1+1) = 5\times(1008+1) = 5\times1009.$$

    It has the factor 5 (and 1009) besides 1 and itself. Hence it is composite.

    Both numbers can be written as a product of two factors greater than 1, so both are composite.

    Q7

    A circular path runs around a sports field. Sonia needs 18 minutes to drive one full round; Ravi needs 12 minutes for the same round. Both set off from the same point, at the same moment and in the same direction. After how many minutes will the two be together at the starting point again?

    Solution

    Sonia is at the starting point after 18, 36, 54, … minutes (multiples of 18). Ravi is there after 12, 24, 36, … minutes (multiples of 12). They are there together for the first time at the least common multiple of 18 and 12.

    \(18 = 2\times3^2,\qquad 12 = 2^2\times3\)

    \(\text{LCM}(18,12) = 2^2\times3^2 = 36\)

    They will meet again at the starting point after 36 minutes.

    In 36 minutes Sonia completes 2 rounds and Ravi completes 3 rounds.

    Exercise 1.2

    3 questions · proofs of irrationality

    Q1

    Prove that \(\sqrt5\) is irrational.

    Solution

    Let us assume, to the contrary, that \(\sqrt5\) is rational.

    Then \(\sqrt5=\dfrac ab\), where \(a\) and \(b\) are integers, \(b\ne0\). Dividing out any common factor, we may take \(a\) and \(b\) to be coprime.

    So \(b\sqrt5=a\). Squaring both sides, \(5b^2=a^2\).  …(1)

    Therefore 5 divides \(a^2\), and so 5 divides \(a\). [If a prime \(p\) divides \(a^2\), then \(p\) divides \(a\)]

    Let \(a=5c\) for some integer \(c\). Substituting in (1): \(5b^2=25c^2\), that is, \(b^2=5c^2\).

    Therefore 5 divides \(b^2\), and so 5 divides \(b\).

    Thus \(a\) and \(b\) have at least 5 as a common factor. This contradicts the fact that \(a\) and \(b\) are coprime.

    The contradiction has arisen because of our incorrect assumption that \(\sqrt5\) is rational. Hence \(\sqrt5\) is irrational. Hence proved.

    Q2

    Prove that \(3+2\sqrt5\) is irrational.

    Solution

    Let us assume, to the contrary, that \(3+2\sqrt5\) is rational.

    Then we can find coprime integers \(a\) and \(b\) \((b\ne0)\) such that \(3+2\sqrt5=\dfrac ab\).

    $$2\sqrt5=\frac ab-3=\frac{a-3b}{b}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sqrt5=\frac{a-3b}{2b}$$

    Since \(a\) and \(b\) are integers, \(\dfrac{a-3b}{2b}\) is rational, and so \(\sqrt5\) is rational.

    But this contradicts the fact that \(\sqrt5\) is irrational (proved in Question 1).

    So our assumption is wrong, and \(3+2\sqrt5\) is irrational. Hence proved.

    In the board exam, if the question does not say “given that \(\sqrt5\) is irrational”, first write the proof of Question 1 and then this part.

    Q3

    Prove that the following are irrationals:

    1. \(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\)
    2. \(7\sqrt5\)
    3. \(6+\sqrt2\)
    Solution
    (i)

    Assume, to the contrary, that \(\dfrac1{\sqrt2}\) is rational. Then \(\dfrac1{\sqrt2}=\dfrac ab\) for some coprime integers \(a,\,b\) with \(b\ne0\). Here \(a\ne0\), because \(\dfrac1{\sqrt2}\ne0\).

    Taking reciprocals, \(\sqrt2=\dfrac ba\). Since \(a\) and \(b\) are integers and \(a\ne0\), \(\dfrac ba\) is rational, so \(\sqrt2\) is rational.

    This contradicts the fact that \(\sqrt2\) is irrational. Hence \(\dfrac1{\sqrt2}\) is irrational.

    (ii)

    Assume, to the contrary, that \(7\sqrt5\) is rational. Then \(7\sqrt5=\dfrac ab\) for some coprime integers \(a,\,b\) with \(b\ne0\).

    So \(\sqrt5=\dfrac{a}{7b}\). Since 7, \(a\) and \(b\) are integers, \(\dfrac a{7b}\) is rational, so \(\sqrt5\) is rational.

    This contradicts the fact that \(\sqrt5\) is irrational. Hence \(7\sqrt5\) is irrational.

    (iii)

    Assume, to the contrary, that \(6+\sqrt2\) is rational. Then \(6+\sqrt2=\dfrac ab\) for some coprime integers \(a,\,b\) with \(b\ne0\).

    So \(\sqrt2=\dfrac ab-6=\dfrac{a-6b}{b}\). Since \(a\) and \(b\) are integers, \(\dfrac{a-6b}b\) is rational, so \(\sqrt2\) is rational.

    This contradicts the fact that \(\sqrt2\) is irrational. Hence \(6+\sqrt2\) is irrational.

    Hence proved.

    Answer & solution

    The required number divides \(280-4=276\), \(396-5=391\) and \(581-6=575\) exactly. Being the greatest such number, it is HCF (276, 391, 575).

    \(276=2^2\times3\times23,\qquad391=17\times23,\qquad575=5^2\times23\).

    The only common prime is 23, so HCF = 23.

    23

    Check: \(280=23\times12+4\), \(396=23\times17+5\), \(581=23\times25+6\). The divisor 23 is larger than every remainder, as it must be.

    Find (i) the LCM of 15, 24 and 36, (ii) the smallest four-digit number divisible by each of them, and (iii) the greatest four-digit number divisible by each of them.

    Answer & solution
    (i)

    \(15=3\times5,\ 24=2^3\times3,\ 36=2^2\times3^2\). LCM \(=2^3\times3^2\times5=360\).

    (ii)

    A number divisible by 15, 24 and 36 is a multiple of 360. The smallest four-digit number is 1000, and \(1000=360\times2+280\). The next multiple is \(360\times3=1080\).

    (iii)

    The greatest four-digit number is 9999, and \(9999=360\times27+279\). So the required number is \(9999-279=9720\).

    (i) 360   (ii) 1080   (iii) 9720

    Three tankers contain 407 litres, 444 litres and 518 litres of diesel respectively. Find the maximum capacity of a container that can measure the diesel of all three tankers an exact number of times. How many times must the container be filled to empty each tanker?

    Answer & solution

    The capacity must divide 407, 444 and 518 exactly, and be as large as possible: capacity = HCF (407, 444, 518).

    \(407=11\times37,\qquad444=2^2\times3\times37,\qquad518=2\times7\times37\).

    HCF = 37. So the container holds 37 litres.

    Number of fillings: \(\dfrac{407}{37}=11,\ \dfrac{444}{37}=12,\ \dfrac{518}{37}=14\).

    37 litres; 11, 12 and 14 times respectively

    Long answer5 marks

    State the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic. Use it to answer the following.

    1. Write 4641 as a product of primes.
    2. Explain why \(3\times7\times13\times17+17\) is a composite number.
    3. Can \(21^n\) end with the digit 0 for some natural number \(n\)?
    Answer & solution

    Statement. Every composite number can be expressed as a product of primes, and this factorisation is unique apart from the order in which the prime factors occur.

    (i)

    \(4641=3\times1547=3\times7\times221=3\times7\times13\times17\).

    (ii)

    \(3\times7\times13\times17+17=17\,(3\times7\times13+1)=17\times274=17\times2\times137\). It has factors other than 1 and itself, so it is composite.

    (iii)

    \(21^n=3^n\times7^n\). To end in 0 a number must have both 2 and 5 as prime factors. By uniqueness of prime factorisation, \(21^n\) has only the primes 3 and 7. So \(21^n\) can never end with the digit 0.

    1 for the statement · 1 for (i) · 1½ for (ii) · 1½ for (iii).

    Stacking the new library books. A school library receives 336 Mathematics books, 240 Science books and 96 English books. The librarian wants to stack them so that every stack has the same number of books, each stack has books of one subject only, and the number of stacks is as small as possible.

    1. Write 336 as a product of its prime factors. 1
    2. How many books will there be in each stack? 1
    3. How many stacks will be formed in all? 2ORIf 144 Hindi books also arrive, will the number of books per stack change? Find the new total number of stacks.
    Answer & solution
    (i)

    \(336=2^4\times3\times7\).

    (ii)

    Fewest stacks means the largest stack size: HCF (336, 240, 96). \(240=2^4\times3\times5,\ 96=2^5\times3\). HCF \(=2^4\times3=48\) books per stack.

    (iii)

    Stacks \(=\dfrac{336}{48}+\dfrac{240}{48}+\dfrac{96}{48}=7+5+2=14\).

    OR: \(144=2^4\times3^2\), and HCF (48, 144) = 48, so the stack size stays 48. Hindi needs \(\frac{144}{48}=3\) stacks; total \(=14+3=17\) stacks.

    Festival lights. Three strings of decorative lights on a school building are programmed to blink every 8 seconds, 12 seconds and 15 seconds. They are switched on together, so all three blink at the same instant at the start.

    1. After how many seconds do strings A and B blink together for the first time? 1
    2. After how many seconds do all three strings blink together for the first time? 1
    3. How many times will all three blink together in the first 10 minutes after the start (do not count the start)? 2ORA fourth string blinks every 20 seconds. After how long will all four blink together? Justify.
    Answer & solution
    (i)

    LCM (8, 12): \(8=2^3,\ 12=2^2\times3\Rightarrow2^3\times3=24\) seconds.

    (ii)

    \(15=3\times5\). LCM (8, 12, 15) \(=2^3\times3\times5=120\) seconds.

    (iii)

    10 minutes = 600 seconds. They blink together every 120 s: at 120, 240, 360, 480 and 600 s. That is \(\frac{600}{120}=5\) times.

    OR: \(20=2^2\times5\). LCM (120, 20) = 120 because 20 already divides 120. So all four blink together after 120 seconds — the fourth string changes nothing.

    The number-card game. Meera writes these numbers on six cards: \(\ \sqrt2,\ \ \sqrt8,\ \ 2+\sqrt3,\ \ 2-\sqrt3,\ \ \pi,\ \ \dfrac{22}{7}\). Her friend Kabir must sort them into “rational” and “irrational” and answer her questions.

    1. Kabir says “\(\pi\) and \(\frac{22}{7}\) are the same number, so both are rational.” Is he right? 1
    2. Find the product of the numbers on the first two cards. Is it rational? 1
    3. Given that \(\sqrt3\) is irrational, prove that \(2+\sqrt3\) is irrational. 2ORShow that both the sum and the product of \(2+\sqrt3\) and \(2-\sqrt3\) are rational.
    Answer & solution
    (i)

    No. \(\frac{22}{7}\) is rational (it is of the form \(\frac pq\)); \(\pi\) is irrational. \(\frac{22}{7}=3.142857\ldots\) is only an approximation of \(\pi=3.14159\ldots\)

    (ii)

    \(\sqrt2\times\sqrt8=\sqrt{16}=4\), which is rational.

    (iii)

    Assume \(2+\sqrt3=\dfrac ab\) with integers \(a, b\), \(b\ne0\). Then \(\sqrt3=\dfrac{a-2b}{b}\), which is rational. This contradicts the irrationality of \(\sqrt3\). Hence \(2+\sqrt3\) is irrational.

    OR: Sum \(=(2+\sqrt3)+(2-\sqrt3)=4\). Product \(=2^2-(\sqrt3)^2=1\). Both 4 and 1 are rational.

    Real Numbers

    Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic

    • Every composite number = product of primes
    • The factorisation is unique (order aside)
    • Standard form: \(x=p_1^{a_1}p_2^{a_2}\cdots p_n^{a_n}\)

    HCF and LCM

    • HCF: smallest powers of common primes
    • LCM: greatest powers of all primes
    • HCF × LCM = \(a\times b\) — two numbers only

    Uniqueness questions

    • Ends in 0 ⇔ has both 2 and 5 as prime factors
    • \(4^n,\ 6^n,\ 12^n\) never end in 0
    • “\(abc+c\) is composite”: take the common factor out

    Irrational numbers

    • Cannot be written as \(\frac pq\), \(q\ne0\)
    • \(p\) prime, \(p\mid a^2\Rightarrow p\mid a\)
    • \(\sqrt2,\ \sqrt3,\ \sqrt5\): proof by contradiction

    Combining with rationals

    • rational ± irrational = irrational
    • non-zero rational × irrational = irrational
    • irrational ± / × irrational: may be either

    Word problems

    • “largest, greatest, maximum” → HCF
    • “smallest, least, together again” → LCM
    • Remainders: subtract first (HCF), add after (LCM)

    Formulas and facts to remember

    • Fundamental Theorem of ArithmeticEvery composite number can be factorised as a product of primes, uniquely, apart from order.
    • HCFProduct of the smallest power of each common prime factor.
    • LCMProduct of the greatest power of each prime factor involved.
    • Product rule (two numbers)\(\text{HCF}(a,b)\times\text{LCM}(a,b)=a\times b\)
    • Coprime numbersHCF = 1, so LCM = \(a\times b\).
    • Theorem 1.2If a prime \(p\) divides \(a^2\), then \(p\) divides \(a\).
    • Ends with digit 0only if the number has both 2 and 5 as prime factors.

    Mistakes that cost marks

    • Using HCF × LCM = product for three numbers.It is valid for two numbers only.
    • Forgetting the word “coprime” in an irrationality proof.Without “\(a, b\) coprime” there is nothing to contradict.
    • Writing “\(\sqrt5\) is irrational” without proof when the question does not give it.Prove it first, unless the question says “given that”.
    • Taking LCM when the question asks for the “largest” or “maximum”.Largest that divides → HCF. Smallest that is divisible → LCM.
    • In remainder problems, taking the HCF of the original numbers.Subtract the remainders first, then take the HCF.
    • Saying 1 is a prime number, or that coprime numbers must be prime.1 is neither prime nor composite; 8 and 15 are coprime.
