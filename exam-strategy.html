Exam Strategy

Knowing the chapters and scoring in the paper are two different skills. This page is the second one: the pattern, the clock, the way an examiner hands out marks, and exactly what to do with the days you have left.

1 Know the paper

You cannot plan three hours you have never looked at. The Mathematics theory paper is 80 marks in 3 hours and has 38 questions, all compulsory, arranged in five sections. There is no choice between sections — the choice is inside a few individual questions.

SectionWhat it containsQuestionsMarks eachTotalInternal choice
A18 MCQs + 2 assertion–reason questions20120None
BVery short answer5210In 2 questions
CShort answer6318In 2 questions
DLong answer4520In 2 questions
ECase studies, sub-parts of 1 + 1 + 2 marks3412In every 2-mark part
Totalall compulsory38—80—

Two rules that apply to the whole paper: use \(\pi=\tfrac{22}{7}\) unless the question itself says otherwise, and calculators are not allowed. So your arithmetic has to be reliable on paper — that is a skill you practise, not a talent you are born with.

Where the 80 marks sit

The syllabus is divided into seven units, and the marks are not spread evenly. Two units, Algebra and Geometry, carry 35 of the 80 marks between them. If you are choosing what to fix first, start there.

Standard or Basic?

Mathematics Standard — 041

Same syllabus, same 38 questions, same three hours. The paper leans harder on application and analysis: roughly 54% of the marks test remembering and understanding, 24% applying, and 22% analysing, evaluating and creating.

Choose it if there is any chance you will want Mathematics in Class XI.

Mathematics Basic — 241

Same syllabus, same pattern, same three hours. The weight shifts to the direct end: about 75% remembering and understanding, 15% applying, 10% analysing, evaluating and creating.

It is not a smaller course. Everything on this site prepares you for both papers.

Decide this with your school early and then stop thinking about it. Students lose more marks worrying about which paper to take than they ever lose to the difference between the two.

The other 20 marks

The board paper is 80 of 100. The remaining 20 marks are internal assessment, awarded in your own school: 10 for pen-paper tests and multiple assessment (5 + 5), 5 for your portfolio and 5 for lab and practical work. These are the cheapest marks in the subject — they ask for attendance, a neat notebook, and taking the class tests seriously. Students who casually throw away six or seven of them spend the board exam trying to win them back the hard way.

CBSE has moved Class X to two board examinations in the same year — a main examination and a second one taken only if you want to improve. Both follow the same syllabus, internal assessment is conducted once, and the better of the two scores is the one that counts. Plan for the first examination as if it is the only one; a second attempt is a safety net, not a strategy. Dates and rules do change, so confirm them on cbse.gov.in and with your school before you build any plan around the second exam.

2 Where the marks are

Every chapter matters, but they do not all behave the same way in a paper. Some are almost entirely one- and two-mark questions; some reliably carry a five-mark question with a figure. This table is the map. Read the last column as “this is the sentence students forget to write”.

Two honest warnings before you use it. First, this is what these chapters are typically asked as — not a prediction, and never an excuse to skip anything. Second, the board takes questions from every chapter, so “low weightage” means “cheap to finish”, not “safe to ignore”.

If your time is short and you must choose, the order that returns the most marks per hour is: Algebra (20 marks, four chapters, and almost all of it is procedure you can drill) → Geometry (15) → Trigonometry (12) → Statistics & Probability (11) → Mensuration (10) → Real Numbers and Coordinate Geometry (6 each, but both are one short chapter, so both are quick wins).

3 How examiners give marks

An examiner does not read your answer and form an opinion. They work down a mark scheme that lists the steps of the expected answer, with a mark or half a mark against each step, and they tick the steps they can see on your page. Marks are given for steps, not for the final number. Everything else on this page follows from that one sentence.

The same question, answered twice

A 3-mark questionquadratic word problem

A shopkeeper buys some books for ₹ 1200. Had he bought 10 more books for the same money, each book would have cost him ₹ 20 less. How many books did he buy?

The full-marks answer — 3 out of 3

Let the number of books he bought be \(x\).

Cost of one book \(=\dfrac{1200}{x}\). For \(x+10\) books bought with the same money, cost of one book \(=\dfrac{1200}{x+10}\), and that is ₹ 20 less. So

$$\frac{1200}{x}-\frac{1200}{x+10}=20$$

[1 mark — the equation is formed from the given condition]

Multiplying both sides by \(x(x+10)\):

$$\begin{aligned} 1200(x+10)-1200x &= 20x(x+10)\\ 12000 &= 20x^2+200x\\ x^2+10x-600 &= 0\\ (x+30)(x-20) &= 0 \end{aligned}$$

\(\therefore\ x=-30\) or \(x=20\).

[1 mark — reduced to a quadratic and solved]

The number of books cannot be negative, so \(x=-30\) is rejected.

[1 mark — impossible root rejected and the answer stated]

He bought 20 books

Check: 20 books at ₹ 60 each; 30 books at ₹ 40 each — exactly ₹ 20 less. ✓

1 for forming the equation · 1 for reducing it to a quadratic and solving · 1 for rejecting the negative root and writing the answer.

The same question, 1½ out of 3

Here is what a student in a hurry writes. Read it as the examiner sees it — four lines on a page, nothing else.

$$\frac{1200}{x}-\frac{1200}{x+10}=20$$ $$x^2+10x-600=0$$ $$x=20,\ -30$$

Answer: \(x=20,\ -30\)

Where the marks went

✓ 1

Line 1 is correct and complete. The condition has been turned into an equation. That mark is earned and no one can take it away.

½

Nothing happens between line 1 and line 2. No multiplying through by \(x(x+10)\), no simplification — the quadratic simply appears. The examiner has no step to tick. The final equation is right, so a little credit is typically given, but the mark for the method is not there to be given.

✕ 0

Both roots are kept. A shopkeeper cannot buy −30 books. The question asked “how many books”, and the answer offered two numbers, one of them impossible. The last mark is for choosing, and no choice was made.

Total: typically 1½ out of 3 — and notice that this student knew how to do the question. Half the marks were lost to writing, not to mathematics.

The three lines that were missing cost 1½ marks: the cross-multiplication, the factorisation, and the sentence “the number of books cannot be negative”.

An arithmetic slip is not a disaster. Suppose you formed the equation correctly, then made a sign error and got \(x^2+10x-500=0\). If you go on to factorise your own equation correctly and reject the impossible root properly, those later steps are still correct steps, done on the page, in front of the examiner. You lose a mark, not the question. This is precisely why “I did it in my head” is the most expensive sentence in mathematics.

The reverse is also true, and it catches good students. A correct final answer with no working is a weak answer. If you can do a 3-mark question in two lines, you are not saving time — you are handing back marks that the mark scheme has already set aside for you.

Twelve presentation rules

None of these need any extra mathematics. All of them need a decision you make before the paper starts.

  1. Question number in the margin, on a fresh line. Never two questions on one line, and never out of the order you have chosen without writing the number clearly.
  2. Formula first, then the numbers. Write “Area of a sector \(=\dfrac{\theta}{360^\circ}\times\pi r^2\)” before you substitute. If your arithmetic then fails, the formula mark is still yours.
  3. One step per line, each starting with \(=\). Do not run working across the page like a sentence.
  4. Draw the figure for every geometry, circle, mensuration and height-and-distance question, even when the paper already prints one — re-draw it and mark what is given on it.
  5. Proofs get four labelled lines: Given, To prove, Construction, Proof. The first three are quick marks that most students skip.
  6. A reason for every geometric step, in brackets: [BPT], [angles in the same segment], [tangent ⟂ radius at the point of contact].
  7. Units on every final answer: cm², cm³, m, km/h, ₹. A bare number is an incomplete answer.
  8. Box or underline the final answer. The examiner should never have to hunt for it on your page.
  9. Rough work in its own column on the right, ruled off with a single line — never in the margin, never between the steps of an answer.
  10. One neat stroke through anything you want cancelled. No scribbling, no whitener, no tearing out pages.
  11. If you attempt both halves of an internal choice, cross out the one you do not want. If you leave both, you have no control over which one gets marked.
  12. Leave two blank lines between answers. Paper is free. A crowded page is not.

Practise this layout now, in every mock test you take, so that on the day you do not have to think about it. Presentation you have to remember costs time; presentation that has become a habit costs nothing and pays every single question.

4 The 3-hour plan

Three hours is 180 minutes for 80 marks — about 2¼ minutes per mark, and that already includes the checking time. Students almost never fail on mathematics in the last hour; they fail on the clock. Decide the plan now, rehearse it in every mock test, and on the day you will simply follow it.

Before the writing starts

You are normally given time to look at the paper before you may begin writing. That time is for deciding, not for solving. Use it in this order:

  1. Read all 20 questions of Section A and put a small tick beside every one you can answer on sight. You now know roughly what Section A is going to cost you.
  2. Find the six questions that carry an internal choice — two in B, two in C, two in D — read both options of each, and circle the one you will attempt.
  3. Read the three case studies of Section E and decide their order: the one whose situation you understand fastest goes first.
  4. Stop there. Do not start planning solutions in your head — you will only convince yourself that a question is harder than it is.

The order to attempt

  1. Section A — 25 minTwenty marks, one per question, in one clean run. Nothing is left blank — there is no negative marking. For an MCQ you cannot see, eliminate two options and choose.
  2. Section B — 20 minTwo-mark questions are short and complete. Four minutes each is plenty, and you finish 30 marks in the first 45 minutes.
  3. Section E — 30 minEach case study is 4 marks built on one situation, and the first two parts are usually one-liners. Doing them while you are still fresh protects 12 marks that panic would otherwise eat.
  4. Section C — 40 minSix questions, roughly 6½ minutes each. These are the ones with three clean steps — write all three.
  5. Section D — 50 minFour questions of 5 marks, about 12 minutes each. Proofs and long word problems. You are warm now, and the whole paper is already in your head.
  6. Check — 15 minNo new question is started here. This time belongs to the answers you have already written.

Why this order and not the printed one? Because Sections A and B hand back marks at nearly twice the rate of Section D, and because a student who has already banked 30 marks in 45 minutes writes the hard questions calmly. You are free to follow the printed order instead — the only rule is that the question number must be written clearly for every answer, whatever order you choose.

Never leave a question half-done meaning to “come back to it”. Half-finished answers are the most common way strong students lose 6 or 7 marks: they run out of time and the unfinished page is worth almost nothing. Either finish it now, or write the formula and the figure, star the question number in the margin, and move on.

When you are stuck

  1. The 90-second rule. If you have not seen the first step of a 1-, 2- or 3-mark question within 90 seconds, stop. On a 5-mark question give it three minutes. Then move.
  2. Write what you do know before you leave it. The formula. The figure, labelled. “Given / To prove”. The value of \(\sin 30^\circ\). Those are real marks, and they take twenty seconds.
  3. Star the question number in the margin so you can find it again in the last 15 minutes without re-reading the paper.
  4. Come back once. Very often you will see the first step immediately — your mind kept working on it while you wrote something else.
  5. Nothing stays blank. In the last block, go to every starred question and write the best line you have. A wrong attempt costs you nothing; a blank costs you everything the mark scheme had set aside.

Using the internal choices

  • Read both, always

    Even when the first option is a topic you like. It takes twenty seconds and it is the only free decision in the paper.

  • Choose on the first step

    Not on the chapter. Pick the option whose first line you can already write. A favourite topic with an unfamiliar set-up is a trap.

  • Once you start, stay

    Switching at the third line wastes the time you have spent and usually the marks too. Decide once, commit.

  • Attempted both? Cross one out

    With one neat stroke. Leaving both standing means you do not choose which one is marked.

The last 15 minutes

  1. Count the answers. Are all 38 question numbers on your sheets, each with something written against it?
  2. Go to the starred questions and fill in whatever you can.
  3. Check that every final answer has a unit and is boxed or underlined.
  4. Check the MCQ letters: did you write (c) when you meant the third option? This is the cheapest mark to lose and the easiest to save.
  5. Check your question numbering against the paper, particularly in the section you attempted out of order.
  6. Do not rub out and redo an answer you merely feel uneasy about. Change something only when you can say exactly what is wrong with it.

5 Mistakes that cost the most marks

These sixteen turn up in every batch of answer sheets, in every chapter, year after year. Not one of them is caused by not knowing the mathematics. Read this list after every mock test you correct — and put a tick against the ones you actually made.

  • Writing the quadratic formula as \(x=\dfrac{b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}\), or dividing only the square root by \(2a\).It is \(x=\dfrac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}\): minus \(b\), and the whole numerator over \(2a\). Write the fraction bar full width before you substitute anything.
  • Giving both roots as the answer to a word problem.Add the line “a speed / length / number of books cannot be negative, so we reject \(x=-30\)”. That sentence is worth a mark on its own.
  • Using \(\pi=3.14\) when the paper says \(\pi=\tfrac{22}{7}\), or the other way round.Use exactly what the question gives. If it says nothing, use \(\tfrac{22}{7}\) — and keep the 7 unmultiplied so it cancels with the radius.
  • Writing cm² for a volume, cm³ for an area, or no unit at all.Length → cm · area → cm² · volume → cm³ · rate → km/h. Read the unit off the formula before you box the answer.
  • Declaring two triangles similar without naming the criterion.Write \(\triangle ABC\sim\triangle PQR\) followed by [AA], [SSS] or [SAS], with the vertices in matching order. Both the criterion and the order carry marks.
  • Putting class marks into the median and mode formulas.Median and mode use the lower boundary \(l\), the class size \(h\), the frequency \(f\) and \(cf\). Class marks \(x_i\) belong to the mean and nowhere else.
  • Finding the median of classes like 10–19, 20–29 without making them continuous.Subtract 0.5 from every lower limit and add 0.5 to every upper limit first: 9.5–19.5, 19.5–29.5, … Then apply the formula.
  • Reading “at least one head” as “exactly one head”.At least 1 = 1 or more · at most 2 = 0, 1 or 2 · “not \(E\)” \(=1-P(E)\). Underline the phrase in the question before you count outcomes.
  • Leaving a height as \(\dfrac{30}{\sqrt3}\) m or a side as \(\dfrac{20}{\sqrt2}\) cm.Rationalise and simplify: \(\dfrac{30}{\sqrt3}=10\sqrt3\) and \(\dfrac{20}{\sqrt2}=10\sqrt2\). The simplified surd is the answer the mark scheme is looking for.
  • Adding the circular faces where a cone or a hemisphere sits on a cylinder.Faces that touch disappear. Sketch the joined solid and add only the surfaces you could actually run your hand over.
  • Using total surface area where the question asked for curved surface area.Underline the words “curved” or “total” in the stem before you write a single line. Same for “volume” against “capacity”.
  • Proving a trigonometric identity by working on both sides at once until they meet in the middle.Start from one side — the messier one — and transform it: \(\text{LHS}=\dots=\text{RHS}\). Finish with “Hence proved”.
  • Reversing a sign the wrong way when moving a term: \(3x-5=7\Rightarrow 3x=7-5\).A term crosses the equals sign with its sign reversed: \(3x=7+5=12\), so \(x=4\). Check one line by substituting back.
  • Mixing up \(a_n\) and \(S_n\) in an AP.“Which term is 78?” needs \(a_n=a+(n-1)d\). “Sum of the first 20 terms” needs \(S_n=\dfrac n2\bigl[2a+(n-1)d\bigr]\). Underline “term” or “sum” in the question.
  • Rounding in the middle of the working — writing 1.73 for \(\sqrt3\) at step two.Carry surds and fractions to the last line, then round once, and only if the question asks you to.
  • Leaving a question blank because you cannot finish it.There is no negative marking. Write the formula, draw the figure, write “Given / To prove”. Part of an answer earns part of the marks; a blank earns exactly nothing.

Keep an error notebook: one page per chapter, and on it one line for every mistake you actually made, in your own handwriting. Most students repeat four or five mistakes forever. Once they are written down in your own hand you stop making them — and that notebook, not the textbook, is what you read on the morning of the exam.

6 Revision plans

Three plans, depending on what you actually have left. Pick the honest one, not the flattering one. All three are built on the same five steps, and all three keep the last stretch for full papers rather than new reading.

  2. 2 · Watch videosFree video lessons picked for that chapter. Use them for the one or two topics that did not click in tab 1 — not as a replacement for it.

  4. 4 · Board practiceFifty-plus original questions per chapter in the exact board pattern — MCQs, assertion–reason, 2-, 3- and 5-mark questions and case studies, each with a full solution and the marking points.
  5. 5 · Quick revisionThe chapter on one screen: mind map, formula list, the mistakes that cost marks, and a checklist. This is the tab you read the night before.

The tick and the star

Beside every question number in tabs 3 and 4 there are two small buttons.

  • ✓ Tick — “done”

    Press it only after you have solved the question on paper and checked it. The progress bar at the top of the tab and the chapter card on the home page both follow your ticks, so the number is only worth something if you are honest with it.

  • ★ Star — “come back”

    Press it the moment you get one wrong, guess an MCQ, or need the solution to start. These are the questions you revise. Nothing else on this site is as useful as your own list of starred questions.

  • Filter chips

    At the top of the Board practice tab: filter by question type (MCQ, 2-mark, 3-mark, 5-mark, case study), or by ★ Starred and Not done yet. Revising is just “Starred”, one chapter at a time.

  • Your ticks stay on this device

    They are stored in the browser you are using. Use the same browser on the same phone or laptop and your progress follows you; a different device starts blank.

The three pages outside the chapters

Formula sheet
Every formula of the course on one page, by chapter. Use it the way a musician uses scales: write the formulas out from memory first, then open this page and check. Reading it is almost useless; writing it out is not.
Mock tests
Full 80-mark papers in the exact board pattern — 38 questions, five sections, three hours. Sit one in a single unbroken sitting, on paper, with a clock in front of you. A mock test done in pieces teaches you nothing about the thing that actually breaks students, which is time.
Board papers
Past papers to practise on. Do these after you can finish a chapter’s Board practice tab without the solutions, not before.

A rhythm that works: chapters on weekdays, one full paper every Sunday morning at exam time, corrected on Sunday evening. Sitting the paper at the same hour as the real one is a small trick with a real effect — by the exam your concentration already knows what 10.30 to 1.30 feels like.

8 If maths scares you

This section is for the student who is behind — who has not opened the book for months, who freezes at a question paper, who has already decided that they are “not a maths person”. Read it carefully, because almost all of that is fixable, and none of it is about talent.

Here is the first thing to understand. Mathematics is the one subject where the paper tells you in advance exactly what it will ask. There are no surprises in a Class X Maths paper — the same twenty or thirty question types appear in a different order with different numbers. Nobody scores in this subject by being clever on the day. They score by having already written that type of question, on paper, ten times.

The quickest marks, in order

If you have very little time, do these chapters first. They are short, they are mechanical, and they return marks fast.

  1. Real Numbers — 6 marks, one short chapter. HCF and LCM from prime factors is arithmetic you already know. Add the \(\sqrt2,\sqrt3,\sqrt5\) irrationality proof, which is the same six lines every time, and this unit is done.
  2. Probability — one formula. \(P(E)=\dfrac{\text{favourable outcomes}}{\text{total outcomes}}\). Count carefully, and you have MCQs plus a 2- or 3-mark question.
  3. Coordinate Geometry — 6 marks, three formulas. Distance, section, midpoint. Nothing to understand, everything to practise.
  4. Arithmetic Progressions — two formulas. \(a_n\) and \(S_n\). The questions are more patterned than in any other algebra chapter.
  5. Trigonometry — the values table. Learn \(\sin,\cos,\tan\) at \(0^\circ,30^\circ,45^\circ,60^\circ,90^\circ\) cold. That table alone answers MCQs and carries the first line of most trigonometry questions.
  6. Statistics — long but completely mechanical. Mean, median and mode from a frequency table is a 5-mark question you can rehearse until it takes you six minutes with no thinking at all.
  7. Areas Related to Circles — two formulas and \(\tfrac{22}{7}\). Sector and segment. Keep 7 as a factor of the radius and the arithmetic stays clean.

Forty-five minutes a day

Not three hours. Forty-five minutes, at the same time every day, at a desk, with paper — no phone on the table.

  1. 10 minutes — writeWrite out the formulas of one chapter from memory. Then open the formula sheet and mark what you missed. Writing, not reading: reading formulas feels like work and teaches nothing.
  2. 25 minutes — solveFive questions from one chapter’s Board practice tab, on paper, timed: two MCQs, two 2-mark, one 3-mark. Solutions stay closed until all five are attempted.
  3. 10 minutes — correctOpen the solutions, correct in red, tick or star each question, and write every mistake as one line in your error notebook.

Six days of that is thirty questions a week. In a month it is about 130 questions solved on paper and nearly twenty hours of writing practice — which is more mathematics than most students do in a year of “studying”. On the seventh day, one full mock test.

Start with the chapter you hate least, not the one that is next. Momentum beats order. Two finished chapters change how you feel about the subject far more than four half-read ones, and how you feel is most of the problem.

If your mind goes blank in the hall: put the pen down. Sit back. Breathe out slowly, ten times, counting them. Then pick up the pen and write the first thing you are certain of — \(\sin 30^\circ=\tfrac12\), or the quadratic formula, anything. A blank is your body, not your memory. It passes in about two minutes, and it passes faster once you are writing.

One honest question before you close this page: what will you do tonight — not this week, tonight?

Dr. PK’s answer

Forty-five minutes. One chapter from the list above. Ten minutes writing its formulas from memory, twenty-five minutes on five questions, ten minutes correcting them and starting your error notebook. Then close the book and sleep.

You do not have to like mathematics to score in it, and you certainly do not have to be a “maths person”. You have to sit at the same desk at the same time and write on paper. That is the entire secret, it is boring, and it works every single time.
