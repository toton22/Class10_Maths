    9.1 Heights and distances

    Trigonometry was invented for exactly this job: measuring things nobody can climb or walk across. Stand on the ground, measure one distance and one angle — and the right triangle hands you the height of the tower.

    Chapter 8 gave you the six ratios of an acute angle in a right triangle. This chapter adds no new mathematics at all. What is new is the picture: you are given a real situation in words, and your first job is to turn it into a right triangle with the right angle at the foot of the vertical object.

    That right angle is free every time. A tower, pole, tree, chimney, building or lamp-post stands perpendicular to level ground, so the triangle formed by the object, the ground and the line of sight is always right-angled at the foot.

    • Heights you cannot climb

      Tower, tree, chimney, building, hill, a balloon or a kite in the sky.

    • Distances you cannot walk

      Width of a river, the gap between two buildings, the distance between two ships at sea.

    • Lengths of slopes

      A ladder against a wall, a slide in a park, the taut string of a kite, a rope tied to a pole.

    • Speed and time

      How far a car or a balloon moved between two observations, and how long it takes to cover the rest.

    Line of sight, angle of elevation, angle of depression

    A student stands on the ground and looks up at the top of a minar. Three things in that picture have names, and every question in this chapter uses them.

    Definition

    The line of sight is the straight line drawn from the eye of an observer to the point on the object that the observer is looking at.

    Definition

    The angle of elevation of a point is the angle made by the line of sight with the horizontal, when the point being viewed is above the horizontal level — the case in which you raise your head to look at the object.

    Now turn the picture upside down. A girl sitting on a balcony looks down at a flower pot on the ground. Her line of sight is now below the horizontal through her eye.

    Definition

    The angle of depression of a point is the angle made by the line of sight with the horizontal, when the point being viewed is below the horizontal level — the case in which you lower your head to look at the object.

    Angle of elevation

    • Observer is low, object is high.
    • Angle is marked at the observer, on the ground.
    • It is an angle of the right triangle itself, so you can use it straight away.

    Angle of depression

    • Observer is high, object is low.
    • Angle is marked at the top, between the horizontal and the line of sight.
    • It lies outside the triangle. Shift it to the bottom using alternate angles first.

    Both angles are measured from the horizontal — never from the vertical wall or the tower. If a figure marks the angle between the line of sight and the vertical, that angle is the complement: subtract it from \(90^\circ\) before you use it.

    And an angle of depression is not marked inside the building. It sits between the dotted horizontal at the top and the line of sight.

    The trick that makes depression easy

    The horizontal line at the top of a building and the horizontal ground are two parallel lines. The line of sight cuts both of them, so it is a transversal — and alternate interior angles are equal.

    The angle of depression of a point O from the top P equals the angle of elevation of P from O. So the moment you see “angle of depression \(=30^\circ\)”, write \(30^\circ\) at the bottom of the triangle and carry on as usual. In a board answer write the reason: [alternate angles, horizontal \(\parallel\) ground].

    The Sun's altitude and shadows

    Many questions talk about the altitude of the Sun. That is simply the angle of elevation of the Sun, measured at the tip of the shadow. A ray of sunlight grazes the top of the pole and lands at the tip of the shadow, so the ray is the line of sight.

    Higher Sun, shorter shadow. Altitude \(60^\circ\) gives a short shadow, altitude \(30^\circ\) a long one. So in any “the shadow is longer by …” question, the longer shadow always belongs to the smaller angle.

    The values you will need

    From Chapter 8: in a right triangle, for an acute angle \(\theta\),

    $$\begin{aligned}\sin\theta &=\frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}}\\[3pt] \cos\theta &=\frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}}\\[3pt] \tan\theta &=\frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}}\end{aligned}$$

    “Opposite” and “adjacent” are always decided with respect to the angle you are using. The hypotenuse is always the side facing the right angle.

    Which ratio do I use?

    Every question involves exactly two sides of the triangle: the one whose length you know and the one you want. Name those two sides with respect to the given angle, and the pair tells you the ratio.

    In practice, \(\tan\) does most of the work: heights and ground distances are the opposite and adjacent sides of the angle, and both are what questions usually give. Reach for \(\sin\) or \(\cos\) only when a slant length appears — a ladder, a slide, a rope, the string of a kite. A slant length is the hypotenuse.

    How to write a full-marks answer

    1. Draw the figure. A horizontal ground line, the vertical object(s) standing on it, the line of sight, and the angle marked at the correct vertex. Name every point with a letter.
    2. Write the given and the required. “AB is the tower, BC = 15 m, \(\angle ACB=30^\circ\); to find AB.”
    3. Choose the triangle that contains both the known side and the unknown side, and say it is right-angled: “In right \(\triangle ABC\), \(\angle B=90^\circ\)”.
    4. Write the ratio, then substitute. Formula first, values next — examiners give a mark for each.
    5. Solve, rationalise, and answer in a sentence with the unit. Leave a surd such as \(15\sqrt3\) as it is unless the question gives you a value for \(\sqrt3\).

    In a 3-mark heights-and-distances question the marks usually split as 1 for a correct labelled figure, 1 for the correct ratio with substitution, 1 for the answer with unit. A student who only writes the arithmetic loses a third of the marks. Always draw.

    The four set-ups you will meet

    • 1 · One triangle

      One angle and one length are given; one length is asked. Pick the ratio and finish in three lines. Examples 1, 2 and 3.

    • 2 · Two points on the ground

      One vertical object seen from two points (shadow grows, a boy walks nearer). Two triangles share the same height — call it \(h\), form two equations and eliminate \(h\). Example 5.

    • 3 · Two heights from one point

      Building + flagstaff, pedestal + statue, tower on a building. Two triangles share the same base — call it \(x\), form two equations and eliminate \(x\). Example 4.

    • 4 · Two verticals facing each other

      Angles of depression of the top and the bottom of one object from the top of another. Draw the horizontal at the top, use alternate angles, and complete the rectangle. Examples 6 and 7.

    Whenever an observer has a height (a 1.5 m girl, a 1.2 m boy), draw the horizontal from the eye to the vertical object. It creates a rectangle, so the piece of the tower below eye level equals the observer's height, and the horizontal equals the ground distance. Find the piece above eye level first, then add the observer's height at the very end.

    The angle of depression of a car from the top of a tower is \(45^\circ\). What is the angle of elevation of the top of the tower from the car, and how do the height of the tower and the distance of the car compare?

    Show answer

    The angle of elevation is also \(45^\circ\) (alternate angles, since the horizontal at the top is parallel to the ground). Because \(\tan45^\circ=1\), the height of the tower equals the distance of the car from its foot.

    The NCERT examples, solved

    Work through all seven with a pencil. Between them they cover every set-up the board asks from this chapter.

    NCERT Example 1one triangle, find the height

    A tower stands straight up on level ground. From a point on the ground 15 m away from the foot of the tower, the top of the tower is seen at an angle of elevation of \(60^\circ\). Find the height of the tower.

    Solution

    Given: AB is the tower, BC = 15 m, \(\angle ACB=60^\circ\). To find: AB.

    The tower is vertical and the ground is horizontal, so \(\angle ABC=90^\circ\). In right \(\triangle ABC\), with respect to \(\angle C\):

    • BC is the adjacent side — and it is known;
    • AB is the opposite side — and it is what we want.

    Opposite and adjacent, so the ratio to use is \(\tan\).

    $$\begin{aligned}\tan 60^\circ &= \frac{AB}{BC}\\[2pt] \sqrt3 &= \frac{AB}{15}\\[2pt] AB &= 15\sqrt3\end{aligned}$$

    The height of the tower is \(15\sqrt3\) m.

    The question gives no value for \(\sqrt3\), so the surd form is the final answer. (If you were asked for a decimal it would be about 25.98 m.)

    A very common slip in Example 1 is writing \(\tan 60^\circ=\dfrac{BC}{AB}\). Always read the ratio off the angle you are standing at: \(\tan\) = opposite over adjacent, and “opposite” means opposite to that angle, not to the right angle.

    NCERT Example 2a slant length — use sin and cot

    An electrician has to repair a fault on an electric pole of height 5 m. She has to reach a point 1.3 m below the top of the pole to do the work. She uses a ladder which, leaning at \(60^\circ\) to the horizontal, just reaches that point. What should the length of the ladder be, and how far from the foot of the pole should she place its foot? (Take \(\sqrt3=1.73\).)

    Solution

    Given: pole AD = 5 m, AB = 1.3 m, ladder BC makes \(60^\circ\) with the ground. To find: BC and DC.

    First get the height she must reach:

    $$\begin{aligned}BD &= AD-AB\\[2pt] &= (5-1.3)\text{ m}=3.7\text{ m}\end{aligned}$$

    In right \(\triangle BDC\), \(\angle BDC=90^\circ\) and \(\angle BCD=60^\circ\). The ladder BC is the hypotenuse and BD is the side opposite \(\angle C\), so use \(\sin\).

    $$\begin{aligned}\sin 60^\circ &= \frac{BD}{BC}\\[2pt] \frac{\sqrt3}{2} &= \frac{3.7}{BC}\\[2pt] BC &= \frac{3.7\times2}{\sqrt3}=\frac{7.4}{1.73}\\[2pt] BC &= 4.28\text{ m (approx.)}\end{aligned}$$

    For DC we need the side adjacent to \(\angle C\) while BD (opposite) is known, so use \(\cot\) (or \(\tan\), it is the same equation upside down).

    $$\begin{aligned}\frac{DC}{BD} &= \cot 60^\circ=\frac{1}{\sqrt3}\\[2pt] DC &= \frac{3.7}{\sqrt3}=\frac{3.7}{1.73}\\[2pt] DC &= 2.14\text{ m (approx.)}\end{aligned}$$

    The ladder should be about 4.28 m long, and its foot should be about 2.14 m from the foot of the pole.

    Notice the two words that decided everything in Example 2: “ladder” (a slant length, therefore the hypotenuse, therefore \(\sin\) or \(\cos\)) and “1.3 m below the top” (so subtract before you start). Reading a question this way — underlining the slant lengths and the words “below”, “above”, “tall” — is half the work.

    NCERT Example 3the observer has a height

    An observer 1.5 m tall is 28.5 m away from a chimney. The angle of elevation of the top of the chimney from her eyes is \(45^\circ\). What is the height of the chimney?

    Solution

    Given: AB is the chimney, CD the observer with CD = 1.5 m, CB = 28.5 m, \(\angle ADE=45^\circ\). To find: AB.

    Construction: draw DE horizontal, meeting the chimney at E. Then DEBC has four right angles, so it is a rectangle and

    $$\begin{aligned}DE &= CB = 28.5\text{ m}\\[2pt] EB &= DC = 1.5\text{ m}\end{aligned}$$

    In right \(\triangle AED\), \(\angle AED=90^\circ\) and \(\angle ADE=45^\circ\). AE is opposite the angle and DE is adjacent to it, so use \(\tan\).

    $$\begin{aligned}\tan 45^\circ &= \frac{AE}{DE}\\[2pt] 1 &= \frac{AE}{28.5}\\[2pt] AE &= 28.5\text{ m}\end{aligned}$$

    The chimney is the part above eye level plus the part below it:

    $$AB = AE+EB = (28.5+1.5)\text{ m}=30\text{ m}$$

    The height of the chimney is 30 m.

    Stopping at \(AE=28.5\) m is the single most common mistake in this chapter. The angle is measured from the eye, so the triangle only reaches down to eye level. Add the observer's height at the end — and if the question asks for the distance the observer walked, do not add anything, because horizontal distances are unaffected.

    NCERT Example 4two heights, one point — share the base

    From a point P on the ground the angle of elevation of the top of a 10 m tall building is \(30^\circ\). A flag is hoisted at the top of the building, and from P the angle of elevation of the top of the flagstaff is \(45^\circ\). Find the length of the flagstaff and the distance of the building from P. (Take \(\sqrt3=1.732\).)

    Solution

    Given: AB = 10 m, \(\angle APB=30^\circ\), \(\angle APD=45^\circ\). To find: DB and PA.

    There are two right triangles here, PAB and PAD, both right-angled at A and both standing on the same base PA. Start with the triangle in which a length is known.

    In right \(\triangle PAB\):

    $$\begin{aligned}\tan 30^\circ &= \frac{AB}{AP}\\[2pt] \frac{1}{\sqrt3} &= \frac{10}{AP}\\[2pt] AP &= 10\sqrt3 = 10\times1.732\\[2pt] AP &= 17.32\text{ m}\end{aligned}$$

    Let \(DB=x\) m. Then \(AD = AB+BD = (10+x)\) m.

    In right \(\triangle PAD\):

    $$\begin{aligned}\tan 45^\circ &= \frac{AD}{AP}\\[2pt] 1 &= \frac{10+x}{10\sqrt3}\\[2pt] 10+x &= 10\sqrt3\\[2pt] x &= 10\left(\sqrt3-1\right)\\[2pt] x &= 10(1.732-1)=7.32\end{aligned}$$

    The flagstaff is 7.32 m long and the building is 17.32 m from P.

    “Building + flagstaff”, “pedestal + statue”, “building + transmission tower”, “tower + cable” — all the same question. The pattern is: the lower triangle gives the base, the upper triangle gives the total, and the difference is the upper object. Solve the triangle whose height is given first.

    NCERT Example 5two shadows — share the height

    The shadow of a tower standing on level ground is found to be 40 m longer when the Sun's altitude is \(30^\circ\) than when it is \(60^\circ\). Find the height of the tower.

    Solution

    Given: AB is the tower; the tip of the shadow is at C when the altitude is \(60^\circ\) and at D when it is \(30^\circ\), with DC = 40 m. To find: AB.

    Let \(AB=h\) m and \(BC=x\) m. The longer shadow belongs to the smaller angle, so

    $$BD = BC+CD = (x+40)\text{ m}.$$

    Both triangles are right-angled at B, and both contain the same height \(h\).

    In right \(\triangle ABC\) — this is equation (1):

    $$\begin{aligned}\tan 60^\circ &=\frac{AB}{BC}\\[2pt] \sqrt3 &=\frac{h}{x}\\[2pt] h &=x\sqrt3\end{aligned}$$

    In right \(\triangle ABD\) — this is equation (2):

    $$\begin{aligned}\tan 30^\circ &=\frac{AB}{BD}\\[2pt] \frac{1}{\sqrt3} &=\frac{h}{x+40}\end{aligned}$$

    Putting the value of \(h\) from (1) into (2):

    $$\begin{aligned}\frac{1}{\sqrt3} &= \frac{x\sqrt3}{x+40}\\[2pt] x+40 &= \left(x\sqrt3\right)\sqrt3 = 3x\\[2pt] 2x &= 40\\[2pt] x &= 20\end{aligned}$$

    From (1), \(h = 20\sqrt3\).

    The height of the tower is \(20\sqrt3\) m.

    Set-up 2 always ends the same way. Take \(\tan\) of the bigger angle first — it gives the shorter, simpler expression \(h=x\sqrt3\) — then substitute into the second equation. If you start with the \(30^\circ\) triangle you end up with \(\sqrt3\) on the wrong side and extra rationalising.

    NCERT Example 6two buildings, angles of depression

    From the top of a multi-storeyed building the angles of depression of the top and the bottom of an 8 m tall building are \(30^\circ\) and \(45^\circ\) respectively. Find the height of the multi-storeyed building and the distance between the two buildings.

    Solution

    Given: PC is the multi-storeyed building, AB the smaller one with AB = 8 m; \(\angle QPB=30^\circ\) and \(\angle QPA=45^\circ\), where PQ is the horizontal through P. To find: PC and AC.

    PQ is horizontal and BD is horizontal, so \(PQ\parallel BD\). PB cuts both, so

    $$\angle PBD = \angle QPB = 30^\circ$$

    [alternate angles, \(PQ\parallel BD\)]

    In the same way \(\angle PAC = \angle QPA = 45^\circ\).

    ABDC has four right angles, so it is a rectangle. Therefore

    $$\begin{aligned}BD &= AC\\[2pt] DC &= AB = 8\text{ m}\end{aligned}$$

    In right \(\triangle PDB\):

    $$\begin{aligned}\frac{PD}{BD} &=\tan 30^\circ=\frac{1}{\sqrt3}\\[4pt] BD &= PD\sqrt3\end{aligned}$$

    In right \(\triangle PCA\):

    $$\begin{aligned}\frac{PC}{AC} &=\tan 45^\circ=1\\[4pt] PC &= AC\end{aligned}$$

    Now \(PC = PD+DC = PD+8\), and \(AC = BD = PD\sqrt3\). Since \(PC=AC\),

    $$\begin{aligned}PD+8 &= PD\sqrt3\\[2pt] 8 &= PD\left(\sqrt3-1\right)\\[2pt] PD &= \frac{8}{\sqrt3-1}\end{aligned}$$

    Rationalise by multiplying above and below by \(\sqrt3+1\):

    $$\begin{aligned}PD &= \frac{8\left(\sqrt3+1\right)}{\left(\sqrt3-1\right)\left(\sqrt3+1\right)}\\[4pt] &= \frac{8\left(\sqrt3+1\right)}{3-1}\\[4pt] &= 4\left(\sqrt3+1\right)\text{ m}\end{aligned}$$

    Therefore

    $$\begin{aligned}PC &= PD+DC\\[2pt] &= 4\left(\sqrt3+1\right)+8\\[2pt] &= 4\sqrt3+12 = 4\left(3+\sqrt3\right)\text{ m}\end{aligned}$$

    and the distance between the buildings is \(AC=PC=4\left(3+\sqrt3\right)\) m.

    Height of the multi-storeyed building \(=4\left(3+\sqrt3\right)\) m \(\approx 18.93\) m; distance between the buildings \(=4\left(3+\sqrt3\right)\) m.

    Why did the height and the distance come out equal? Because the angle of depression of the foot of the other building was \(45^\circ\), and \(\tan 45^\circ=1\) forces the two sides of that triangle to be equal. Whenever you see \(45^\circ\) in a figure, look for two equal sides — it often saves a whole step.

    NCERT Example 7depression on both sides

    From a point on a bridge across a river, the angles of depression of the banks on opposite sides of the river are \(30^\circ\) and \(45^\circ\) respectively. If the bridge is at a height of 3 m from the banks, find the width of the river.

    Solution

    Given: PD = 3 m; the angles of depression of A and B from P are \(30^\circ\) and \(45^\circ\). To find: the width \(AB\).

    The horizontal through P is parallel to AB, so by alternate angles

    $$\angle PAD = 30^\circ,\qquad \angle PBD = 45^\circ.$$

    The width of the river is \(AB = AD+DB\), so find the two pieces separately. Both triangles are right-angled at D.

    In right \(\triangle APD\):

    $$\begin{aligned}\tan 30^\circ &= \frac{PD}{AD}\\[2pt] \frac{1}{\sqrt3} &= \frac{3}{AD}\\[2pt] AD &= 3\sqrt3\text{ m}\end{aligned}$$

    In right \(\triangle BPD\):

    $$\begin{aligned}\tan 45^\circ &= \frac{PD}{BD}\\[2pt] 1 &= \frac{3}{BD}\\[2pt] BD &= 3\text{ m}\end{aligned}$$

    Therefore

    $$\begin{aligned}AB &= BD+AD\\[2pt] &= 3+3\sqrt3 = 3\left(1+\sqrt3\right)\text{ m}\end{aligned}$$

    The width of the river is \(3\left(1+\sqrt3\right)\) m \(\approx 8.20\) m.

    When the two objects are on opposite sides of the observer, the two ground pieces are added (Example 7). When they are on the same side, they are subtracted (Example 5, and “two ships on the same side of a lighthouse”). Read that phrase carefully — it decides whether you write \(AD+DB\) or \(AD-DB\), and it is worth a full mark.

    A tower is \(50\) m high and the angle of elevation of its top from a point on the ground is \(45^\circ\). How far is the point from the foot of the tower? What would the distance be if the angle were \(30^\circ\)?

    Show answer

    At \(45^\circ\): \(\tan45^\circ = \dfrac{50}{d}=1\), so \(d=50\) m. At \(30^\circ\): \(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt3}=\dfrac{50}{d}\), so \(d=50\sqrt3\approx86.6\) m. A smaller angle means you are standing further away — exactly the “longer shadow” idea again.

    From the top of a \(20\) m tall building the angle of depression of a car on the road is \(60^\circ\). How far is the car from the foot of the building?

    Show answer

    The angle of elevation of the top from the car is also \(60^\circ\) (alternate angles). So \(\tan60^\circ=\dfrac{20}{d}\), giving \(d=\dfrac{20}{\sqrt3}=\dfrac{20\sqrt3}{3}\approx11.55\) m.

    9.2 Summary

    Everything this chapter asks of you, on one screen.

    Line of sight
    The line drawn from the eye of an observer to the point on the object being viewed.
    Angle of elevation
    The angle the line of sight makes with the horizontal when the point viewed is above the horizontal level — you raise your head.
    Angle of depression
    The angle the line of sight makes with the horizontal when the point viewed is below the horizontal level — you lower your head.
    What trigonometry does here
    The height or length of an object, and the distance between two distant objects, can be found from a known distance and a known angle using the trigonometric ratios.

    The method, every single time

    1. DrawGround line, vertical objects on it, dashed line of sight, angle marked at the right vertex, every point lettered.
    2. Convert depressionsMove each angle of depression to the bottom of the triangle using alternate angles, and say so.
    3. NameWrite what is given and what is required; call the unknowns \(h\) and \(x\).
    4. ChoosePick the triangle holding the known and the unknown side; decide opposite / adjacent / hypotenuse; pick \(\tan\), \(\sin\) or \(\cos\).
    5. SolveFormula, substitution, working, rationalise, unit — and a one-line answer sentence.

    Reading the words of a question

    What the question saysWhat it means in the figure
    altitude of the Sun is \(\theta\)angle of elevation \(\theta\) at the tip of the shadow
    length of the string of a kitethe hypotenuse (assume no slack)
    length of the ladder / slide / ropethe hypotenuse
    a boy \(1.5\) m tall observes …the angle is at eye level; add \(1.5\) m at the end
    the tree breaks and the top touches the groundbroken part = hypotenuse; height = standing part + broken part
    elevation changes from \(30^\circ\) to \(60^\circ\)two triangles on the same vertical; subtract the two ground distances
    on opposite sides of the tower / riveradd the two ground distances
    on the same sidesubtract the two ground distances

    Mistakes that cost marks

    • Marking the angle of depression inside the building instead of at the horizontal.
    • Forgetting to add the observer's height (Example 3) — or wrongly adding it to a horizontal distance.
    • Writing \(\tan\theta=\dfrac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{opposite}}\) by mistake.
    • Leaving \(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt3}\) unrationalised in the final answer, or dropping the unit.
    • Adding the two ground distances when the objects are on the same side (they must be subtracted).
    • Skipping the figure. It carries a mark of its own in almost every board question from this chapter.

    This chapter is small but it is worth marks every year: usually one 2-mark or 3-mark question, and very often a 5-mark one built on set-up 2 or 4 (two triangles). The 5-mark question is nearly always solved by forming two equations and eliminating the common side — so practise that step until it is automatic.

    Three questions, asked in this order, will start every solution correctly: Where is my right angle? Where is my angle? Which two sides are involved? The ratio follows from the third answer, and the figure follows from the first two.

    A vertical pole casts a shadow whose length is \(\sqrt3\) times the height of the pole. What is the altitude of the Sun at that moment?

    Show answer

    Let the height be \(h\); then the shadow is \(h\sqrt3\). If \(\theta\) is the Sun's altitude, \(\tan\theta=\dfrac{h}{h\sqrt3}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt3}\), so \(\theta=30^\circ\). A long shadow always means a low Sun.

    Exercise 9.1

    Exercise 9.1

    15 questions · heights and distances — poles, towers, buildings, ships, a kite, a balloon and a car

    Every angle in this exercise is \(30^\circ\), \(45^\circ\) or \(60^\circ\). Keep these six values in front of you:

    \(\sin 30^\circ=\dfrac12,\ \ \sin 60^\circ=\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2},\ \ \sin 45^\circ=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\)

    \(\tan 30^\circ=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt3},\ \ \tan 45^\circ=1,\ \ \tan 60^\circ=\sqrt3\)

    The three-step habit. (1) Draw the figure: a horizontal ground line, the vertical object, and the line of sight. (2) Name the right triangle and write “In right \(\triangle ABC\)…”. (3) Pick the ratio that joins the side you have to the side you want — opposite and adjacent give \(\tan\), opposite and hypotenuse give \(\sin\), adjacent and hypotenuse give \(\cos\). Rationalise the denominator before writing the answer, and never forget the unit.

    Angle of depression = angle of elevation. When you look down from the top of a tower at a boat, the horizontal line at the top and the ground are parallel, and the line of sight is a transversal. So the angle of depression at the top equals the angle of elevation at the boat [alternate angles]. Always transfer the depression angle to the bottom of the figure before using it — that is where your right triangle is.

    Q1

    A circus artist is climbing a rope 20 m long. The rope is pulled tight, tied to the top of a vertical pole at one end and fixed to the ground at the other. The rope makes an angle of \(30^\circ\) with the ground. Find the height of the pole.

    Solution

    Given: rope \(AC=20\) m, \(\angle ACB=30^\circ\), and the pole is vertical so \(\angle ABC=90^\circ\). To find: the height \(AB\) of the pole.

    In right \(\triangle ABC\), \(AB\) is the side opposite to \(\angle C\) and \(AC\) is the hypotenuse. Opposite and hypotenuse together mean sine.

    $$\begin{aligned} \sin C&=\frac{AB}{AC}\\ \sin 30^\circ&=\frac{AB}{20}\\ \frac12&=\frac{AB}{20}\\ AB&=\frac{20}{2}=10\ \text{m} \end{aligned}$$

    The pole is 10 m high.

    If you had used \(\tan 30^\circ\) here you would first have to find \(BC\), which the question never gives. Choosing the ratio that uses only the given side saves a whole step.

    Q2

    A storm breaks a tree. The broken part bends over and its top touches the ground, making an angle of \(30^\circ\) with the ground. The distance from the foot of the tree to the point where the top touches the ground is 8 m. Find the height of the tree.

    Solution

    Given: the tree \(AB\) breaks at \(C\); the top comes down to \(D\) on the ground with \(BD=8\) m and \(\angle BDC=30^\circ\). To find: the original height \(BC+CD\).

    Let the standing part be \(BC=x\) m and the broken part be \(CD=y\) m. The broken part has only bent over, it has not changed its length, so the tree was \((x+y)\) m tall.

    Step 1 — find \(x\). In right \(\triangle CBD\), \(BC\) is opposite \(\angle D\) and \(BD\) is adjacent to it, so use \(\tan\).

    $$\begin{aligned} \tan 30^\circ&=\frac{BC}{BD}=\frac{x}{8}\\ \frac{1}{\sqrt3}&=\frac{x}{8}\\ x&=\frac{8}{\sqrt3}=\frac{8\sqrt3}{3}\ \text{m} \end{aligned}$$

    Step 2 — find \(y\). In right \(\triangle CBD\), \(BD\) is adjacent to \(\angle D\) and \(CD\) is the hypotenuse, so use \(\cos\).

    $$\begin{aligned} \cos 30^\circ&=\frac{BD}{CD}=\frac{8}{y}\\ \frac{\sqrt3}{2}&=\frac{8}{y}\\ y&=\frac{16}{\sqrt3}=\frac{16\sqrt3}{3}\ \text{m} \end{aligned}$$

    Step 3 — add the two parts.

    $$\begin{aligned} \text{Height}&=x+y=\frac{8\sqrt3}{3}+\frac{16\sqrt3}{3}\\ &=\frac{24\sqrt3}{3}=8\sqrt3\ \text{m} \end{aligned}$$

    The tree was \(8\sqrt3\) m ≈ 13.86 m tall.

    The answer is not \(\dfrac{8\sqrt3}{3}\) m. That is only the stump left standing. The tree's height is the stump plus the whole broken piece.

    Q3

    A contractor is putting two slides in a park. For children below the age of five the top of the slide is to be 1.5 m above the ground and the slide is to be inclined at \(30^\circ\) to the ground. For older children the top is to be 3 m above the ground and the slide steeper, inclined at \(60^\circ\). How long should each slide be?

    Solution
    (i)

    Slide for the younger children: \(AB=1.5\) m, \(\angle ACB=30^\circ\), \(\angle ABC=90^\circ\). Let the slide be \(AC=x\) m.

    In right \(\triangle ABC\), \(AB\) is opposite \(\angle C\) and \(AC\) is the hypotenuse.

    $$\begin{aligned} \sin 30^\circ&=\frac{AB}{AC}=\frac{1.5}{x}\\ \frac12&=\frac{1.5}{x}\\ x&=1.5\times2=3\ \text{m} \end{aligned}$$

    Length of the first slide = 3 m

    (ii)

    Slide for the elder children: \(PQ=3\) m, \(\angle PRQ=60^\circ\), \(\angle PQR=90^\circ\). Let the slide be \(PR=y\) m.

    In right \(\triangle PQR\), \(PQ\) is opposite \(\angle R\) and \(PR\) is the hypotenuse.

    $$\begin{aligned} \sin 60^\circ&=\frac{PQ}{PR}=\frac{3}{y}\\ \frac{\sqrt3}{2}&=\frac{3}{y}\\ y&=\frac{6}{\sqrt3}=\frac{6\sqrt3}{3}=2\sqrt3\ \text{m} \end{aligned}$$

    Length of the second slide \(=2\sqrt3\) m ≈ 3.46 m

    3 m for the younger children and \(2\sqrt3\) m ≈ 3.46 m for the elder children

    The steeper slide is only a little longer even though it is twice as high — that is what a bigger angle does.

    Q4

    From a point on the ground 30 m away from the foot of a tower, the angle of elevation of the top of the tower is \(30^\circ\). Find the height of the tower.

    Solution

    Given: \(BC=30\) m, angle of elevation \(\angle ACB=30^\circ\), tower \(AB\) vertical so \(\angle ABC=90^\circ\). To find: the height \(AB=h\).

    In right \(\triangle ABC\), \(AB\) is opposite \(\angle C\) and \(BC\) is adjacent to it.

    $$\begin{aligned} \tan C&=\frac{AB}{BC}\\ \tan 30^\circ&=\frac{h}{30}\\ \frac{1}{\sqrt3}&=\frac{h}{30}\\ h&=\frac{30}{\sqrt3}=\frac{30}{\sqrt3}\times\frac{\sqrt3}{\sqrt3}\\ &=\frac{30\sqrt3}{3}=10\sqrt3\ \text{m} \end{aligned}$$

    The tower is \(10\sqrt3\) m ≈ 17.32 m high.

    Multiplying by \(\dfrac{\sqrt3}{\sqrt3}\) is the rationalising step. Leaving the answer as \(\dfrac{30}{\sqrt3}\) costs marks in the board exam.

    Q5

    A kite is flying at a height of 60 m above the ground. The string holding it is tied to a point on the ground and is inclined at \(60^\circ\) to the ground. Find the length of the string, assuming there is no slack in it.

    Solution

    Given: height of the kite \(AB=60\) m, inclination of the string \(\angle ACB=60^\circ\), \(\angle ABC=90^\circ\). To find: the length of the string \(AC=x\).

    In right \(\triangle ABC\), \(AB\) is opposite \(\angle C\) and \(AC\) is the hypotenuse — so use sine.

    $$\begin{aligned} \sin C&=\frac{AB}{AC}\\ \sin 60^\circ&=\frac{60}{x}\\ \frac{\sqrt3}{2}&=\frac{60}{x}\\ x&=\frac{60\times2}{\sqrt3}=\frac{120}{\sqrt3}\\ &=\frac{120\sqrt3}{3}=40\sqrt3\ \text{m} \end{aligned}$$

    The string is \(40\sqrt3\) m ≈ 69.28 m long.

    Check the shape of the answer: the hypotenuse must be longer than the 60 m height, and \(40\sqrt3\approx69.3\) is. A quick sanity check like this catches an upside-down fraction at once.

    Q6

    A boy 1.5 m tall is standing some distance away from a building 30 m tall. He walks towards the building, and the angle of elevation of the top of the building from his eyes increases from \(30^\circ\) to \(60^\circ\). Find the distance he walked towards the building.

    Solution

    Given: building \(AB=30\) m, boy's height \(PE=QF=1.5\) m, \(\angle AEC=30^\circ\), \(\angle AFC=60^\circ\). To find: the distance walked, \(EF\).

    Step 1 — drop the figure to eye level. \(C\) is the point of the building at the boy's eye level, so

    $$AC=AB-BC=30-1.5=28.5\ \text{m}$$

    and \(CE\), \(CF\) are the horizontal distances of his eyes from the building.

    Step 2 — the far position. In right \(\triangle ACE\),

    $$\begin{aligned} \tan 30^\circ&=\frac{AC}{CE}\\ \frac{1}{\sqrt3}&=\frac{28.5}{CE}\\ CE&=28.5\sqrt3\ \text{m} \end{aligned}$$

    Step 3 — the near position. In right \(\triangle ACF\),

    $$\begin{aligned} \tan 60^\circ&=\frac{AC}{CF}\\ \sqrt3&=\frac{28.5}{CF}\\ CF&=\frac{28.5}{\sqrt3}=\frac{28.5\sqrt3}{3}=9.5\sqrt3\ \text{m} \end{aligned}$$

    Step 4 — subtract.

    $$\begin{aligned} EF&=CE-CF\\ &=28.5\sqrt3-9.5\sqrt3\\ &=19\sqrt3\ \text{m} \end{aligned}$$

    He walked \(19\sqrt3\) m ≈ 32.91 m towards the building.

    The 1.5 m is the boy's height, so it must be taken off the building: the triangles stand on the eye-level line \(CE\), not on the ground. Working with \(AB=30\) instead of \(AC=28.5\) is the commonest slip in this question.

    Q7

    A transmission tower is fixed at the top of a building 20 m high. From a point on the ground, the angle of elevation of the bottom of the tower is \(45^\circ\) and the angle of elevation of the top of the tower is \(60^\circ\). Find the height of the tower.

    Solution

    Given: building \(BC=20\) m, \(\angle CAB=45^\circ\), \(\angle DAB=60^\circ\), \(\angle ABD=90^\circ\). To find: the height of the tower \(CD=h\).

    Let the distance of the point from the building be \(AB=x\) m.

    Step 1 — use the \(45^\circ\) angle to find \(x\). In right \(\triangle ABC\),

    $$\begin{aligned} \tan 45^\circ&=\frac{BC}{AB}\\ 1&=\frac{20}{x}\\ x&=20\ \text{m} \end{aligned}$$

    Step 2 — use the \(60^\circ\) angle for the whole height. In right \(\triangle ABD\),

    $$\begin{aligned} \tan 60^\circ&=\frac{BD}{AB}\\ \sqrt3&=\frac{BD}{20}\\ BD&=20\sqrt3\ \text{m} \end{aligned}$$

    Step 3 — the tower is the part above the building.

    $$\begin{aligned} CD&=BD-BC\\ &=20\sqrt3-20\\ &=20(\sqrt3-1)\ \text{m} \end{aligned}$$

    The tower is \(20(\sqrt3-1)\) m ≈ 14.64 m high.

    \(45^\circ\) is always worth hunting for first: \(\tan45^\circ=1\) makes the height and the distance equal, and hands you the base of both triangles in one line.

    Q8

    A statue 1.6 m tall stands on the top of a pedestal. From a point on the ground, the angle of elevation of the top of the statue is \(60^\circ\), and from the same point the angle of elevation of the top of the pedestal is \(45^\circ\). Find the height of the pedestal.

    Solution

    Given: statue \(CD=1.6\) m, \(\angle CAB=45^\circ\), \(\angle DAB=60^\circ\), \(\angle ABD=90^\circ\). To find: the height of the pedestal \(BC\).

    Let \(BC=h\) m and \(AB=x\) m.

    Step 1 — the \(45^\circ\) triangle. In right \(\triangle ABC\),

    $$\begin{aligned} \tan 45^\circ&=\frac{BC}{AB}\\ 1&=\frac{h}{x}\\ x&=h \end{aligned}$$

    Step 2 — the \(60^\circ\) triangle. In right \(\triangle ABD\), \(BD=BC+CD=h+1.6\), and \(AB=x=h\).

    $$\begin{aligned} \tan 60^\circ&=\frac{BD}{AB}\\ \sqrt3&=\frac{h+1.6}{h}\\ \sqrt3\,h&=h+1.6\\ h(\sqrt3-1)&=1.6\\ h&=\frac{1.6}{\sqrt3-1} \end{aligned}$$

    Step 3 — rationalise. Multiply above and below by \(\sqrt3+1\):

    $$\begin{aligned} h&=\frac{1.6}{\sqrt3-1}\times\frac{\sqrt3+1}{\sqrt3+1}\\ &=\frac{1.6(\sqrt3+1)}{(\sqrt3)^2-1^2}\\ &=\frac{1.6(\sqrt3+1)}{2}\\ &=0.8(\sqrt3+1)\ \text{m} \end{aligned}$$

    The pedestal is \(0.8(\sqrt3+1)\) m ≈ 2.19 m high.

    The denominator \(\sqrt3-1\) is rationalised with its conjugate \(\sqrt3+1\), because \((\sqrt3-1)(\sqrt3+1)=3-1=2\). Expect this step in every question where two different angles are seen from one point.

    Q9

    A building and a tower stand on level ground. From the foot of the tower, the angle of elevation of the top of the building is \(30^\circ\); from the foot of the building, the angle of elevation of the top of the tower is \(60^\circ\). The tower is 50 m high. Find the height of the building.

    Solution

    Given: tower \(CD=50\) m, \(\angle BCA=30^\circ\) (from the foot \(C\) of the tower), \(\angle DAC=60^\circ\) (from the foot \(A\) of the building). To find: the height of the building \(AB=h\).

    Let the distance between them be \(AC=x\) m.

    Step 1 — find \(x\) from the tower. In right \(\triangle DCA\),

    $$\begin{aligned} \tan 60^\circ&=\frac{DC}{AC}\\ \sqrt3&=\frac{50}{x}\\ x&=\frac{50}{\sqrt3}\ \text{m} \end{aligned}$$

    Step 2 — use \(x\) for the building. In right \(\triangle BAC\),

    $$\begin{aligned} \tan 30^\circ&=\frac{AB}{AC}\\ \frac{1}{\sqrt3}&=\frac{h}{x}\\ h&=\frac{x}{\sqrt3}=\frac{1}{\sqrt3}\times\frac{50}{\sqrt3}\\ &=\frac{50}{3}\ \text{m}=16\tfrac23\ \text{m} \end{aligned}$$

    The building is \(\dfrac{50}{3}\) m \(=16\dfrac23\) m ≈ 16.67 m high.

    Notice that \(x\) never had to be simplified: \(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt3}\times\dfrac{50}{\sqrt3}=\dfrac{50}{3}\) straight away. Keeping a surd unrationalised inside a calculation is fine — only the final answer must be rationalised.

    Q10

    Two poles of equal height stand opposite each other on the two sides of a road 80 m wide. From a point on the road between the poles, the angles of elevation of the tops of the poles are \(60^\circ\) and \(30^\circ\). Find the height of the poles and the distance of the point from each pole.

    Solution

    Given: \(AB=CD=h\) (equal poles), \(AC=80\) m, \(\angle BPA=60^\circ\), \(\angle DPC=30^\circ\). To find: \(h\), \(AP\) and \(PC\).

    Let \(AP=x\) m, so \(PC=(80-x)\) m.

    Step 1 — the nearer pole. In right \(\triangle BAP\),

    $$\begin{aligned} \tan 60^\circ&=\frac{AB}{AP}\\ \sqrt3&=\frac{h}{x}\\ h&=\sqrt3\,x\qquad\ldots(1) \end{aligned}$$

    Step 2 — the farther pole. In right \(\triangle DCP\),

    $$\begin{aligned} \tan 30^\circ&=\frac{CD}{PC}\\ \frac{1}{\sqrt3}&=\frac{h}{80-x}\\ \sqrt3\,h&=80-x\qquad\ldots(2) \end{aligned}$$

    Step 3 — put (1) into (2).

    $$\begin{aligned} \sqrt3(\sqrt3\,x)&=80-x\\ 3x&=80-x\\ 4x&=80\\ x&=20 \end{aligned}$$

    Step 4 — back-substitute in (1).

    $$h=\sqrt3\times20=20\sqrt3\ \text{m}$$

    So \(AP=20\) m and \(PC=80-20=60\) m.

    Each pole is \(20\sqrt3\) m ≈ 34.64 m high; the point is 20 m from one pole and 60 m from the other.

    Check: \(\tan 60^\circ=\dfrac{20\sqrt3}{20}=\sqrt3\) ✓ and \(\tan 30^\circ=\dfrac{20\sqrt3}{60}=\dfrac{\sqrt3}{3}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt3}\) ✓. The point is nearer the pole seen at the bigger angle — always true.

    Q11

    A TV tower stands vertically on a bank of a canal. From a point \(C\) on the opposite bank, directly opposite the tower, the angle of elevation of the top of the tower is \(60^\circ\). From another point \(D\) on the same bank, 20 m from \(C\) along the straight line through \(C\) and the foot of the tower, the angle of elevation of the top is \(30^\circ\). Find the height of the tower and the width of the canal.

    Solution

    Given: \(\angle ACB=60^\circ\), \(\angle ADB=30^\circ\), \(DC=20\) m, \(\angle ABD=90^\circ\). To find: the height \(AB\) and the width \(CB\).

    Let \(AB=h\) m and \(CB=x\) m. Then \(DB=DC+CB=(x+20)\) m.

    Step 1 — the \(60^\circ\) triangle. In right \(\triangle ABC\),

    $$\begin{aligned} \tan 60^\circ&=\frac{AB}{CB}\\ \sqrt3&=\frac{h}{x}\\ h&=\sqrt3\,x\qquad\ldots(1) \end{aligned}$$

    Step 2 — the \(30^\circ\) triangle. In right \(\triangle ABD\),

    $$\begin{aligned} \tan 30^\circ&=\frac{AB}{DB}\\ \frac{1}{\sqrt3}&=\frac{h}{x+20}\\ \sqrt3\,h&=x+20\qquad\ldots(2) \end{aligned}$$

    Step 3 — put (1) into (2).

    $$\begin{aligned} \sqrt3(\sqrt3\,x)&=x+20\\ 3x&=x+20\\ 2x&=20\\ x&=10 \end{aligned}$$

    Step 4 — back-substitute in (1).

    $$h=\sqrt3\times10=10\sqrt3\ \text{m}$$

    The tower is \(10\sqrt3\) m ≈ 17.32 m high and the canal is 10 m wide.

    Both triangles share the same vertical side \(AB\). That is why calling it \(h\) once and writing two equations in \(h\) and \(x\) works every time two angles are taken from two points in a line with the foot.

    Q12

    From the top of a building 7 m high, the angle of elevation of the top of a cable tower is \(60^\circ\) and the angle of depression of its foot is \(45^\circ\). Find the height of the tower.

    Solution

    Given: building \(AB=7\) m, angle of elevation of the top \(D\) is \(60^\circ\), angle of depression of the foot \(C\) is \(45^\circ\). To find: the height of the tower \(CD\).

    Draw \(AE\) horizontal from \(A\), meeting the tower at \(E\). Then \(ABCE\) is a rectangle, so

    $$CE=AB=7\ \text{m},\qquad AE=BC=x\ \text{m}$$

    Step 1 — the depression \(45^\circ\). Since \(AE\parallel BC\), the angle of depression of \(C\) equals \(\angle ACB\) [alternate angles], so \(\angle ACB=45^\circ\). In right \(\triangle ABC\),

    $$\begin{aligned} \tan 45^\circ&=\frac{AB}{BC}\\ 1&=\frac{7}{x}\\ x&=7\ \text{m} \end{aligned}$$

    Step 2 — the elevation \(60^\circ\). In right \(\triangle AED\), with \(AE=x=7\) m,

    $$\begin{aligned} \tan 60^\circ&=\frac{DE}{AE}\\ \sqrt3&=\frac{DE}{7}\\ DE&=7\sqrt3\ \text{m} \end{aligned}$$

    Step 3 — add the two pieces of the tower.

    $$\begin{aligned} CD&=CE+DE\\ &=7+7\sqrt3\\ &=7(\sqrt3+1)\ \text{m} \end{aligned}$$

    The cable tower is \(7(\sqrt3+1)\) m ≈ 19.12 m high.

    The moment a question says “from the top of …”, draw the horizontal line at that top. It creates the rectangle that gives you \(CE=AB\) and \(AE=BC\) free of charge.

    Q13

    As observed from the top of a lighthouse 75 m high above sea level, the angles of depression of two ships are \(30^\circ\) and \(45^\circ\). The two ships are on the same side of the lighthouse and one is exactly behind the other. Find the distance between the two ships.

    Solution

    Given: lighthouse \(AB=75\) m, angles of depression of the ships \(C\) and \(D\) are \(45^\circ\) and \(30^\circ\). To find: the distance \(CD\).

    The horizontal at \(A\) is parallel to the sea level \(BD\), so the angles of depression equal the angles of elevation at the ships [alternate angles]:

    $$\angle ACB=45^\circ,\qquad \angle ADB=30^\circ$$

    Step 1 — the nearer ship. In right \(\triangle ABC\),

    $$\begin{aligned} \tan 45^\circ&=\frac{AB}{BC}\\ 1&=\frac{75}{BC}\\ BC&=75\ \text{m} \end{aligned}$$

    Step 2 — the farther ship. In right \(\triangle ABD\),

    $$\begin{aligned} \tan 30^\circ&=\frac{AB}{BD}\\ \frac{1}{\sqrt3}&=\frac{75}{BD}\\ BD&=75\sqrt3\ \text{m} \end{aligned}$$

    Step 3 — subtract, because both distances start from \(B\).

    $$\begin{aligned} CD&=BD-BC\\ &=75\sqrt3-75\\ &=75(\sqrt3-1)\ \text{m} \end{aligned}$$

    The ships are \(75(\sqrt3-1)\) m ≈ 54.9 m apart.

    If the ships were on opposite sides of the lighthouse you would add: \(BC+BD\). Reading which side they are on decides between \(+\) and \(-\).

    Q14

    A girl 1.2 m tall spots a balloon moving with the wind along a horizontal line at a height of 88.2 m above the ground. The angle of elevation of the balloon from her eyes at that instant is \(60^\circ\). After some time the angle of elevation reduces to \(30^\circ\). Find the distance travelled by the balloon during this interval.

    Solution

    Given: height of the balloon's path \(=88.2\) m, girl's height \(FE=1.2\) m, \(\angle AEC=60^\circ\), \(\angle BED=30^\circ\). To find: the distance \(AB\) travelled by the balloon.

    Step 1 — work from eye level. \(C\) and \(D\) are on the girl's eye-level line, so

    $$AC=BD=88.2-1.2=87\ \text{m}$$

    Step 2 — the first position. In right \(\triangle ACE\),

    $$\begin{aligned} \tan 60^\circ&=\frac{AC}{CE}\\ \sqrt3&=\frac{87}{CE}\\ CE&=\frac{87}{\sqrt3}=\frac{87\sqrt3}{3}=29\sqrt3\ \text{m} \end{aligned}$$

    Step 3 — the second position. In right \(\triangle BDE\),

    $$\begin{aligned} \tan 30^\circ&=\frac{BD}{DE}\\ \frac{1}{\sqrt3}&=\frac{87}{DE}\\ DE&=87\sqrt3\ \text{m} \end{aligned}$$

    Step 4 — the balloon moved from \(C\) to \(D\) horizontally, so \(AB=CD\).

    $$\begin{aligned} AB&=CD=DE-CE\\ &=87\sqrt3-29\sqrt3\\ &=58\sqrt3\ \text{m} \end{aligned}$$

    The balloon travelled \(58\sqrt3\) m ≈ 100.46 m.

    \(ABDC\) is a rectangle (\(AC\) and \(BD\) are both vertical and equal), so the horizontal distance \(CD\) between the feet is exactly the distance \(AB\) flown by the balloon.

    Q15

    A straight highway leads to the foot of a tower. A man standing at the top of the tower sees a car at an angle of depression of \(30^\circ\); the car is approaching the foot of the tower at a uniform speed. Six seconds later the angle of depression of the car is \(60^\circ\). Find the time the car takes to reach the foot of the tower from this point.

    Solution

    Given: tower \(AB\); the car is at \(C\) when the depression is \(30^\circ\) and at \(D\), 6 seconds later, when the depression is \(60^\circ\); the speed is uniform. To find: the time to go from \(D\) to \(B\).

    Let the height of the tower be \(AB=h\) m. The horizontal at \(A\) is parallel to the highway \(BC\), so [alternate angles]

    $$\angle ACB=30^\circ,\qquad \angle ADB=60^\circ$$

    Step 1 — the far position \(C\). In right \(\triangle ABC\),

    $$\begin{aligned} \tan 30^\circ&=\frac{AB}{BC}\\ \frac{1}{\sqrt3}&=\frac{h}{BC}\\ BC&=\sqrt3\,h \end{aligned}$$

    Step 2 — the near position \(D\). In right \(\triangle ABD\),

    $$\begin{aligned} \tan 60^\circ&=\frac{AB}{BD}\\ \sqrt3&=\frac{h}{BD}\\ BD&=\frac{h}{\sqrt3}=\frac{\sqrt3\,h}{3} \end{aligned}$$

    Step 3 — the distance covered in 6 seconds.

    $$\begin{aligned} CD&=BC-BD=\sqrt3\,h-\frac{\sqrt3\,h}{3}\\ &=\frac{3\sqrt3\,h-\sqrt3\,h}{3}=\frac{2\sqrt3\,h}{3} \end{aligned}$$

    Step 4 — compare the two distances. The speed is the same throughout, so the time is proportional to the distance:

    $$\frac{BD}{CD}=\frac{\dfrac{\sqrt3\,h}{3}}{\dfrac{2\sqrt3\,h}{3}}=\frac12$$

    So \(DB\) is half of \(CD\), and the car needs half of 6 seconds:

    $$\text{time}=\frac12\times6=3\ \text{seconds}$$

    The car reaches the foot of the tower 3 seconds after passing \(D\).

    If you prefer speed: \(\text{speed}=\dfrac{CD}{6}=\dfrac{2\sqrt3\,h}{18}=\dfrac{\sqrt3\,h}{9}\) m/s, and then \(\text{time}=\dfrac{BD}{\text{speed}}=\dfrac{\sqrt3\,h/3}{\sqrt3\,h/9}=3\) seconds. The unknown \(h\) cancels either way — which is why the height of the tower is never needed.

    On level ground the shadow of a vertical pole is exactly as long as the pole itself. The angle of elevation of the Sun at that moment is

    1. \(30^\circ\)
    2. \(45^\circ\)
    3. \(60^\circ\)
    4. \(90^\circ\)
    Answer & solution

    Remember the pair: shadow = height means \(45^\circ\); shadow longer than the pole means a smaller angle.

    A tower is 30 m high and the length of its shadow on the ground is \(30\sqrt3\) m. The angle of elevation of the Sun is

    1. \(30^\circ\)
    2. \(45^\circ\)
    3. \(60^\circ\)
    4. \(75^\circ\)
    Answer & solution

    \(\tan\theta=\dfrac{30}{30\sqrt3}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt3}=\tan30^\circ\Rightarrow\theta=30^\circ\). (a) \(30^\circ\)

    \(60^\circ\) is what you get by writing the ratio upside down, \(\dfrac{\text{shadow}}{\text{height}}\).

    A kite is flying at a height of 45 m above the ground. The string is tied to a point on the ground and makes an angle of \(30^\circ\) with the ground. If the string has no slack, its length is

    1. 22.5 m
    2. \(30\sqrt3\) m
    3. 90 m

    22.5 m comes from multiplying by \(\sin30^\circ\) instead of dividing; \(45\sqrt3\) m is the horizontal distance (that is \(\tan30^\circ\)).

    A ladder 10 m long rests against a vertical wall with its foot 5 m away from the wall. The angle the ladder makes with the ground is

    1. \(30^\circ\)
    2. \(45^\circ\)
    3. \(75^\circ\)
    4. \(60^\circ\)

    Heights and Distances

    The three words

    • Line of sight: eye → the point looked at
    • Angle of elevation: line of sight above the horizontal
    • Angle of depression: line of sight below the horizontal
    • Both are measured from the horizontal, never from the vertical

    One right triangle

    • Object vertical, ground horizontal ⇒ right angle at the foot
    • Height and ground distance ⇒ \(\tan\theta\)
    • Height and line of sight ⇒ \(\sin\theta\)
    • Ground distance and line of sight ⇒ \(\cos\theta\)

    Depression = elevation

    • Horizontal at the top \(\parallel\) the ground
    • Line of sight is the transversal
    • Alternate interior angles are equal
    • So bring the angle down to the object and work as usual

    Two points, one object

    • Two triangles share the same vertical side \(h\)
    • Write \(h\) from each: \(d_1=\frac h{\tan\theta_1}\), \(d_2=\frac h{\tan\theta_2}\)
    • Same side ⇒ subtract; opposite sides ⇒ add
    • \(30^\circ\) and \(60^\circ\) ⇒ the distances are in the ratio \(3:1\)

    Object on top of object

    • Flagstaff on a tower, statue on a pedestal, antenna on a building
    • Both angles from the same point ⇒ one base \(d\), two heights
    • Small triangle: \(h\); big triangle: \(h+\ell\)
    • Answer often needs rationalising, e.g. \(\frac{6}{\sqrt3-1}\)

    Motion and shadows

    • Distance covered = difference of the two ground distances
    • Speed = distance ÷ time; \(\text{m/s}\times\frac{18}{5}=\text{km/h}\)
    • Sun lower ⇒ angle smaller ⇒ shadow longer
    • Shadow \(=\frac{h}{\tan\theta}\): at \(30^\circ\) it is \(h\sqrt3\), at \(45^\circ\) it is \(h\), at \(60^\circ\) it is \(\frac h{\sqrt3}\)

    Formulas and facts to remember

    • Angle of elevationAngle made by the line of sight with the horizontal when the object is above the horizontal level.
    • Angle of depressionAngle made by the line of sight with the horizontal when the object is below the horizontal level.
    • Depression = elevationThe angle of depression of a point from the top equals the angle of elevation of the top from that point (alternate angles).
    • Height from distance\(h=d\tan\theta\)
    • Distance from height\(d=\dfrac{h}{\tan\theta}\)
    • Line of sight\(\ell=\dfrac{h}{\sin\theta}=\dfrac{d}{\cos\theta}\)
    • Shadow of a vertical pole\(s=\dfrac{h}{\tan\theta}\), where \(\theta\) is the Sun's altitude.
    • Observer's heightIf the eye is \(e\) m above the ground, work with \(h-e\) and add \(e\) back at the end.
    • Rationalising\(\dfrac{a}{\sqrt3-1}=\dfrac{a(\sqrt3+1)}{2}\), \(\ \dfrac{a}{\sqrt3+1}=\dfrac{a(\sqrt3-1)}{2}\), \(\ \dfrac{a}{\sqrt3}=\dfrac{a\sqrt3}{3}\).
    • Speed\(\text{speed}=\dfrac{\text{distance covered}}{\text{time}}\); \(1\ \text{m/s}=\dfrac{18}{5}\) km/h; \(1\ \text{m/min}=\dfrac{3}{50}\) km/h.

    The only values you need

    \(\theta\)\(\sin\theta\)\(\cos\theta\)\(\tan\theta\)
    \(30^\circ\)\(\tfrac12\)\(\tfrac{\sqrt3}2\)\(\tfrac1{\sqrt3}\)
    \(45^\circ\)\(\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\)\(\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\)\(1\)
    \(60^\circ\)\(\tfrac{\sqrt3}2\)\(\tfrac12\)\(\sqrt3\)

    Approximations the paper may ask you to use: \(\sqrt3=1.73\) or \(1.732\), \(\ \sqrt2=1.414\). Rationalise first, substitute the decimal last.

    Which ratio when

    You know / you wantUse
    height and ground distance\(\tan\theta\)
    height and line of sight (ladder, string, rope, slide)\(\sin\theta\)
    ground distance and line of sight (broken tree, ladder foot)\(\cos\theta\)

    Ask yourself: which two sides does this sentence mention? That decides the ratio — nothing else does.

    Mistakes that cost marks

    • Measuring the angle from the vertical tower instead of the horizontal.Both angles are always measured from a horizontal line.
    • Multiplying by \(\tan\theta\) when you should divide.Height \(=d\tan\theta\); distance \(=\dfrac h{\tan\theta}\). Check the size: at \(60^\circ\) the distance must be smaller than the height.
    • Drawing the angle of depression inside the triangle at the top.Draw the horizontal at the top first, then use alternate angles to bring the equal angle down to the object.
    • Forgetting the observer's height.Subtract the eye height first, then add it back to the final height.
    • In a broken-tree question, taking the broken part as the ground distance.The broken part is the hypotenuse; height of the tree = standing part + broken part.
    • Adding when the two points are on the same side (or subtracting when they are on opposite sides).Same side ⇒ difference of distances; opposite sides ⇒ sum.
    • Substituting \(\sqrt3=1.73\) too early and then dividing by a decimal.Rationalise to the form \(a\sqrt3\) or \(a(\sqrt3\pm1)\) first — the arithmetic becomes one multiplication.
    • Losing the mark for the figure, or leaving the answer without a unit.A neat labelled figure is worth about 1 mark. Every answer ends in m, km/h, seconds or degrees.

    What the board usually asks

    This chapter belongs to the Trigonometry unit and is almost always worth 5–6 marks on its own. A typical paper carries:

    • 1 MCQ on a single right triangle or on a shadow — see Q2, Q7, Q12.
    • Sometimes an assertion–reason item on elevation versus depression — see Q23, Q25.
    • A 2-mark question: one triangle, one unknown, often with an observer's height — see Q30, Q34.
    • A 3-mark question of the two-angle type: walking towards a tower, two poles across a road, two ships from a lighthouse — see Q36, Q37, Q43.
    • A 5-mark question combining two triangles: flagstaff on a tower, a moving car and its speed, a building and a tower — see Q46, Q47, Q49, Q52.
    • Often a case study set in real life — a drone, a balloon, a monument, a lighthouse — see Q53–Q56.

    Only \(30^\circ\), \(45^\circ\) and \(60^\circ\) ever appear, so every answer is a clean surd or a decimal obtained with \(\sqrt3=1.73\).
