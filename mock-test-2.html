Mock Test 2

80 marks · 3 hours · 38 questions, set in the exact board pattern and strictly inside the syllabus. Every question has a model answer and a step-wise marking scheme, so you can sit the paper with a clock and then mark it yourself, honestly.

Sit this paper the way you will sit the real one: one sitting, three hours, no calculator, no notes, no peeping at a solution. Write the question number, show every step, and only then open the solutions. The marking scheme tells you exactly where each mark sits.

Mathematics (Standard) — Mock Test 2Time: 3 hours · Maximum marks: 80

General instructions

  1. This question paper has 38 questions. All questions are compulsory.
  2. The paper is divided into five sections — A, B, C, D and E.
  3. Section A has 20 questions of 1 mark each: Q1–Q18 are multiple-choice questions and Q19–Q20 are assertion–reason questions.
  4. Section B has 5 questions of 2 marks each.
  5. Section C has 6 questions of 3 marks each.
  6. Section D has 4 questions of 5 marks each.
  7. Section E has 3 case-study questions of 4 marks each, with sub-parts of 1, 1 and 2 marks.
  8. There is no overall choice. An internal choice is given in 2 questions of Section B, 2 questions of Section C, 2 questions of Section D and in the 2-mark part of each case study. Attempt only one of the two alternatives.
  9. Draw neat figures wherever they are needed. Take \(\pi=\dfrac{22}{7}\) unless the question states otherwise.
  10. Use of a calculator is not allowed.

MCQ1 mark

If \(p\) and \(q\) are two distinct prime numbers, then \(\text{HCF}\left(p^3q^2,\ p^2q^4\right)\) is

  1. \(p^2q^2\)
  2. \(p^3q^4\)
  3. \(pq\)
  4. \(p^2q^4\)
Answer & solution

For the HCF take each common prime to its smallest power.

\(p\): smaller of \(p^3,p^2\) is \(p^2\).   \(q\): smaller of \(q^2,q^4\) is \(q^2\).

So the HCF is \(p^2q^2\). (\(p^3q^4\) is the LCM.) (a) \(p^2q^2\)

The pair of equations \(3x-5y=7\) and \(6x-10y=k\) has infinitely many solutions when \(k\) equals

  1. 7
  2. 14
  3. \(-14\)
  4. every value of \(k\)
Answer & solution

For infinitely many solutions \(\dfrac{a_1}{a_2}=\dfrac{b_1}{b_2}=\dfrac{c_1}{c_2}\).

\(\dfrac36=\dfrac{-5}{-10}=\dfrac12\), so \(\dfrac7k=\dfrac12\Rightarrow k=14\).

The first two ratios alone are not enough: for any other \(k\) the third ratio differs and the pair has no solution. (b) 14

Find a quadratic polynomial the sum and the product of whose zeroes are \(-3\) and 2 respectively. Hence find its zeroes.

Answer & solution

A quadratic polynomial with zeroes \(\alpha,\beta\) is \(x^2-(\alpha+\beta)x+\alpha\beta\).

\(p(x)=x^2-(-3)x+2=x^2+3x+2\).

\(x^2+3x+2=(x+1)(x+2)=0\Rightarrow x=-1\) or \(x=-2\).

\(p(x)=x^2+3x+2\); zeroes \(-1\) and \(-2\)

Given that \(\sqrt5\) is irrational, prove that \(2\sqrt5-3\) is an irrational number.

ORTwo numbers are in the ratio 5 : 6 and their LCM is 480. Find their HCF and the two numbers.

Answer & solution

Assume, to the contrary, that \(2\sqrt5-3\) is rational. Then \(2\sqrt5-3=\dfrac ab\), where \(a,b\) are integers and \(b\ne0\).

So \(\sqrt5=\dfrac{a+3b}{2b}\). The right-hand side is rational, because \(a\) and \(b\) are integers.

Then \(\sqrt5\) would be rational, which contradicts the given fact. Hence \(2\sqrt5-3\) is irrational. Hence proved.

½ for the assumption · 1 for isolating \(\sqrt5\) · ½ for the contradiction and the conclusion.

OR: Let the numbers be \(5k\) and \(6k\). Since 5 and 6 are coprime, HCF \(=k\) and LCM \(=30k\).

\(30k=480\Rightarrow k=16\). So HCF \(=16\) and the numbers are \(5\times16=80\) and \(6\times16=96\).

HCF = 16; the numbers are 80 and 96 Check: \(\frac{80\times96}{16}=480\).

1 mark for taking the numbers as \(5k\) and \(6k\) with LCM \(30k\) · 1 mark for \(k\), the HCF and the numbers.

Short answer3 marksBoard favourite

A fruit seller has 264 bananas, 396 apples and 462 oranges. He wants to pack all of them into boxes so that each box holds fruit of one kind only, every box holds the same number of fruits, and no fruit is left over. Find the greatest number of fruits he can put in one box, and the total number of boxes he then needs.

Answer & solution

The number of fruits in a box must divide 264, 396 and 462 exactly, and it has to be as large as possible. So it is HCF (264, 396, 462).

$$\begin{aligned}264&=2^3\times3\times11\\ 396&=2^2\times3^2\times11\\ 462&=2\times3\times7\times11\end{aligned}$$

Taking the smallest power of each common prime: HCF \(=2\times3\times11=66\).

Number of boxes \(=\dfrac{264}{66}+\dfrac{396}{66}+\dfrac{462}{66}=4+6+7=17\).

66 fruits in a box; 17 boxes in all

1½ marks for the three prime factorisations · 1 mark for the HCF · ½ mark for the number of boxes.

The product of two consecutive positive integers is 306. Form a quadratic equation for this situation and use it to find the two integers.

ORFind the values of \(k\) for which the quadratic equation \((k+4)x^2+(k+1)x+1=0\) has two equal roots.

$$\begin{aligned}x(x+1)&=306\\ x^2+x-306&=0\end{aligned}$$

\(x^2+18x-17x-306=0\Rightarrow(x+18)(x-17)=0\).

So \(x=17\) or \(x=-18\). The integers are positive, so \(x=-18\) is rejected.

The integers are 17 and 18 Check: \(17\times18=306\).

Prove that \(\ \dfrac{\cos A}{1+\sin A}+\dfrac{1+\sin A}{\cos A}=2\sec A\).

ORIf \(\sec\theta+\tan\theta=5\), find the value of \(\sin\theta\).

Answer & solution

Take the LHS over a common denominator:

$$\begin{aligned}\text{LHS}&=\frac{\cos^2A+(1+\sin A)^2}{(1+\sin A)\cos A}\\&=\frac{\cos^2A+1+2\sin A+\sin^2A}{(1+\sin A)\cos A}\end{aligned}$$

Now \(\cos^2A+\sin^2A=1\), so the numerator is \(2+2\sin A=2(1+\sin A)\).

$$\begin{aligned}\text{LHS}&=\frac{2(1+\sin A)}{(1+\sin A)\cos A}\\&=\frac{2}{\cos A}=2\sec A=\text{RHS}\end{aligned}$$

Hence proved.

1 mark for the common denominator · 1 mark for using \(\sin^2A+\cos^2A=1\) · 1 mark for cancelling and finishing.

OR: Use the identity \(\sec^2\theta-\tan^2\theta=1\), which factorises as

$$(\sec\theta+\tan\theta)(\sec\theta-\tan\theta)=1$$

Since \(\sec\theta+\tan\theta=5\), this gives \(\sec\theta-\tan\theta=\dfrac15\).

Adding and subtracting the two equations:

$$\begin{aligned}2\sec\theta&=5+\tfrac15=\tfrac{26}{5}\Rightarrow\sec\theta=\tfrac{13}{5}\\ 2\tan\theta&=5-\tfrac15=\tfrac{24}{5}\Rightarrow\tan\theta=\tfrac{12}{5}\end{aligned}$$

So \(\cos\theta=\dfrac{5}{13}\) and \(\sin\theta=\tan\theta\times\cos\theta=\dfrac{12}{5}\times\dfrac{5}{13}\).

\(\sin\theta=\dfrac{12}{13}\)

1 mark for \(\sec\theta-\tan\theta=\tfrac15\) · 1 mark for \(\sec\theta\) and \(\tan\theta\) · 1 mark for \(\sin\theta\).

Short answer3 marks

Two dice are thrown together once. Find the probability that

  1. the sum of the numbers on the two dice is 8;
  2. the sum of the numbers is a prime number;
  3. the two dice show different numbers.
Answer & solution

Two dice give \(6\times6=36\) equally likely outcomes.

(i)

Sum 8: \((2,6),(3,5),(4,4),(5,3),(6,2)\) — 5 outcomes.

\(P=\dfrac5{36}\).

(ii)

The possible prime sums are 2, 3, 5, 7 and 11, with 1, 2, 4, 6 and 2 outcomes respectively — 15 in all.

\(P=\dfrac{15}{36}=\dfrac5{12}\).

(iii)

The two numbers are the same in the 6 doublets \((1,1),(2,2),\ldots,(6,6)\), so they are different in \(36-6=30\) outcomes.

\(P=\dfrac{30}{36}=\dfrac56\).

\(\dfrac5{36},\ \dfrac5{12},\ \dfrac56\)

1 mark for each part (½ for the favourable outcomes, ½ for the probability).

A school spent ₹ 9000 on prizes for ten students of Class X. Each prize was ₹ 100 less than the prize before it. Find the first prize and the last prize, and also find how much the top three students received together.

ORHow many terms of the AP \(27,\ 24,\ 21,\ \ldots\) must be taken so that their sum is 132? Explain why there are two answers.

Answer & solution

The prizes form an AP with first term \(a\), common difference \(d=-100\) and \(n=10\), and \(S_{10}=9000\).

$$\begin{aligned}S_{10}&=\frac{10}{2}\bigl[2a+9(-100)\bigr]\\ 9000&=5\,(2a-900)\\ 1800&=2a-900\\ a&=1350\end{aligned}$$

Last prize \(=a_{10}=a+9d=1350-900=450\).

Top three \(=1350+1250+1150=3750\).

First prize ₹ 1350, last prize ₹ 450; the top three received ₹ 3750 together

Check: the ten prizes 1350, 1250, …, 450 add up to \(\frac{10}{2}(1350+450)=9000\).

1 mark for identifying \(a,\ d,\ n\) and the sum · 2 marks for the equation and \(a\) · 1 mark for the last prize · 1 mark for the total of the top three.

OR: Here \(a=27\) and \(d=-3\), and we need \(S_n=132\).

$$\begin{aligned}\frac n2\bigl[54+(n-1)(-3)\bigr]&=132\\ \frac n2\,(57-3n)&=132\\ 57n-3n^2&=264\end{aligned}$$

Dividing by 3 and rearranging:

$$\begin{aligned}n^2-19n+88&=0\\ (n-8)(n-11)&=0\end{aligned}$$

So \(n=8\) or \(n=11\); both are whole numbers, so both are acceptable.

The reason: the terms are \(27,24,\ldots\), and \(a_9=3,\ a_{10}=0,\ a_{11}=-3\). These three terms add up to \(3+0-3=0\), so adding them to the first 8 terms does not change the sum.

\(n=8\) or \(n=11\)

1 mark for \(a,\ d\) and the sum formula · 2 marks for the quadratic in \(n\) · 1 mark for both roots · 1 mark for the explanation.

Prove that the lengths of the two tangents drawn from an external point to a circle are equal.

Using this result, find \(AF\), \(BD\) and \(CE\) in the figure below, where a circle is inscribed in \(\triangle ABC\) and touches \(BC\), \(CA\) and \(AB\) at \(D\), \(E\) and \(F\) respectively, given that \(AB=12\) cm, \(BC=8\) cm and \(CA=10\) cm.

Answer & solution

Given: A circle with centre \(O\) and an external point \(P\); \(PQ\) and \(PR\) are tangents touching the circle at \(Q\) and \(R\). To prove: \(PQ=PR\). Construction: Join \(OQ\), \(OR\) and \(OP\).

Proof. \(OQ\perp PQ\) and \(OR\perp PR\) [the tangent at a point of a circle is perpendicular to the radius through that point].

In \(\triangle OQP\) and \(\triangle ORP\):

$$\begin{aligned}\angle OQP&=\angle ORP=90^\circ\\ OQ&=OR\quad[\text{radii}]\\ OP&=OP\quad[\text{common}]\end{aligned}$$

So \(\triangle OQP\cong\triangle ORP\) [RHS congruence], and therefore \(PQ=PR\) [CPCT]. Hence proved.

Using the result. Tangents from \(A\), \(B\) and \(C\) give

\(AF=AE=x,\qquad BF=BD=y,\qquad CD=CE=z\).

$$\begin{aligned}x+y&=AB=12\\ y+z&=BC=8\\ z+x&=CA=10\end{aligned}$$

Adding: \(2(x+y+z)=30\Rightarrow x+y+z=15\).

$$\begin{aligned}z&=15-12=3\\ x&=15-8=7\\ y&=15-10=5\end{aligned}$$

\(AF=7\) cm, \(BD=5\) cm, \(CE=3\) cm

½ for the figure with given and to prove · ½ for the construction · 1½ for the congruence with reasons · ½ for CPCT and the conclusion · 2 for the application (1 for the three equations, 1 for the three lengths).

From the top \(A\) of a building \(AB\) of height 60 m, the angles of depression of the top \(D\) and the foot \(C\) of a tower \(CD\) standing on the same level ground are \(30^\circ\) and \(60^\circ\) respectively. Find the height of the tower and the distance between the building and the tower. \(\bigl(\)Use \(\sqrt3=1.73\bigr)\)

ORThe angle of elevation of the top \(P\) of a tower \(PQ\) from a point \(C\) on the ground is \(30^\circ\). On walking 40 m from \(C\) towards the foot of the tower, to a point \(D\), the angle of elevation becomes \(60^\circ\). Find the height of the tower and the distance \(CQ\). \(\bigl(\)Use \(\sqrt3=1.73\bigr)\)

Answer & solution

Let \(BC=d\) be the distance between the building and the tower and \(CD=h\) the height of the tower. Draw \(DM\perp AB\), meeting \(AB\) at \(M\). Then \(BCDM\) is a rectangle, so \(DM=BC=d\) and \(MB=CD=h\), giving \(AM=60-h\).

In right \(\triangle ABC\) (angle of depression of \(C\) is \(60^\circ\), so \(\angle ACB=60^\circ\)):

$$\begin{aligned}\tan60^\circ&=\frac{AB}{BC}=\frac{60}{d}\\ \sqrt3&=\frac{60}{d}\\ d&=\frac{60}{\sqrt3}=20\sqrt3\end{aligned}$$

In right \(\triangle AMD\) (angle of depression of \(D\) is \(30^\circ\), so \(\angle ADM=30^\circ\)):

$$\begin{aligned}\tan30^\circ&=\frac{AM}{DM}=\frac{60-h}{20\sqrt3}\\ \frac1{\sqrt3}&=\frac{60-h}{20\sqrt3}\\ 60-h&=20\Rightarrow h=40\end{aligned}$$

Height of the tower = 40 m; distance \(=20\sqrt3=34.6\) m

1 mark for the labelled figure with both angles at \(A\) · 1½ for \(\triangle ABC\) and \(d\) · 1½ for \(\triangle AMD\) · 1 for both final answers with units.

OR: Let \(PQ=h\) and \(DQ=x\), so \(CQ=x+40\).

In right \(\triangle PQD\):

$$\tan60^\circ=\frac hx\Rightarrow x=\frac{h}{\sqrt3}$$

In right \(\triangle PQC\):

$$\tan30^\circ=\frac{h}{x+40}\Rightarrow x+40=h\sqrt3$$

Substituting for \(x\):

$$\begin{aligned}h\sqrt3-\frac{h}{\sqrt3}&=40\\ \frac{3h-h}{\sqrt3}&=40\\ 2h&=40\sqrt3\\ h&=20\sqrt3=34.6\end{aligned}$$

\(CQ=h\sqrt3=20\sqrt3\times\sqrt3=60\) m.

Height \(=20\sqrt3=34.6\) m; \(CQ=60\) m

1 mark for the labelled figure · 1½ for the equation from \(\triangle PQD\) · 1½ for the equation from \(\triangle PQC\) · 1 for solving and both answers.

A tent is in the shape of a right circular cylinder with a right circular cone standing on it. The diameter of the base is 14 m, the height of the cylindrical part is 5 m and the height of the conical part is 24 m. Find

  1. the slant height of the cone;
  2. the area of the canvas used for the tent (the floor is not covered);
  3. the volume of air inside the tent;
  4. the cost of the canvas at ₹ 120 per square metre.

\(\bigl(\)Use \(\pi=\tfrac{22}{7}\bigr)\)

Answer & solution

Radius \(r=\dfrac{14}{2}=7\) m, cylinder height \(h=5\) m, cone height \(H=24\) m.

(i)

$$l=\sqrt{r^2+H^2}=\sqrt{49+576}=\sqrt{625}=25\ \text{m}$$

(ii)

Canvas \(=\) curved surface of the cylinder \(+\) curved surface of the cone.

$$\begin{aligned}&=2\pi rh+\pi rl\\&=2\times\tfrac{22}{7}\times7\times5+\tfrac{22}{7}\times7\times25\\&=220+550=770\ \text{m}^2\end{aligned}$$

(iii)

$$\begin{aligned}V&=\pi r^2h+\tfrac13\pi r^2H\\&=\tfrac{22}{7}\times49\times5+\tfrac13\times\tfrac{22}{7}\times49\times24\\&=770+1232=2002\ \text{m}^3\end{aligned}$$

(iv)

Cost \(=770\times120=92\,400\).

\(l=25\) m; canvas \(=770\ \text{m}^2\); air \(=2002\ \text{m}^3\); cost \(=\) ₹ 92 400

1 mark for the slant height · 1½ for the canvas area · 1½ for the volume · 1 for the cost.

A survey of outdoor play. The physical education teacher of a school asked the 50 students of Class X to record, on one particular day, the number of minutes each of them spent playing outdoors. The recorded times are grouped below.

Minutes of play0–2020–4040–6060–8080–100
Number of students6820124
  1. Write the modal class and the median class of this distribution. 1
  2. Find the mean number of minutes of outdoor play. 1
  3. Find the median of the distribution. 2ORFind the mode of the distribution.
Answer & solution
(i)

The greatest frequency is 20, so the modal class is 40–60.

The cumulative frequencies are 6, 14, 34, 46, 50 and \(\dfrac N2=\dfrac{50}{2}=25\). The first cumulative frequency that is at least 25 is 34, which belongs to the class 40–60. So the median class is also 40–60.

(ii)

Class marks: 10, 30, 50, 70, 90.

$$\begin{aligned}\textstyle\sum f_ix_i&=60+240+1000+840+360\\&=2500\end{aligned}$$

Mean \(=\dfrac{2500}{50}=50\) minutes.

(iii)

For the median class 40–60: \(l=40,\ cf=14,\ f=20,\ h=20\).

$$\begin{aligned}\text{Median}&=l+\frac{\frac N2-cf}{f}\times h\\&=40+\frac{25-14}{20}\times20\\&=40+11=51\ \text{minutes}\end{aligned}$$

OR: For the modal class 40–60: \(l=40,\ f_1=20,\ f_0=8,\ f_2=12,\ h=20\).

$$\begin{aligned}\text{Mode}&=l+\frac{f_1-f_0}{2f_1-f_0-f_2}\times h\\&=40+\frac{20-8}{40-8-12}\times20\\&=40+12=52\ \text{minutes}\end{aligned}$$

Modal class and median class 40–60; mean 50 min; median 51 min (OR mode 52 min)

1 mark for (i) (½ + ½) · 1 mark for (ii) · 2 marks for (iii): 1 for the formula with the correct values, 1 for the answer.

Case study4 marks

The sports-day badge. The art club is designing a circular badge of radius 14 cm for the school sports day. Two radii \(OA\) and \(OB\) are drawn at right angles to each other, and the quarter of the badge between them is coloured blue. The rest of the badge is left white. \(\bigl(\)Take \(\pi=\tfrac{22}{7}\bigr)\)

  1. Find the area of the blue part of the badge. 1
  2. Find the length of the arc \(AB\) of the blue part. 1
  3. Find the area of the white part of the badge. 2ORThe club now draws the chord \(AB\) and colours dark blue only the region between the chord \(AB\) and the arc \(AB\). Find the area of this region.
Answer & solution

Here \(r=14\) cm and the central angle is \(\theta=90^\circ\).

(i)

$$\begin{aligned}\text{Area of sector}&=\frac{\theta}{360^\circ}\times\pi r^2\\&=\frac14\times\frac{22}{7}\times14\times14\\&=154\ \text{cm}^2\end{aligned}$$

(ii)

$$\begin{aligned}\text{Arc }AB&=\frac{\theta}{360^\circ}\times2\pi r\\&=\frac14\times2\times\frac{22}{7}\times14=22\ \text{cm}\end{aligned}$$

(iii)

Area of the whole badge \(=\pi r^2=\dfrac{22}{7}\times196=616\ \text{cm}^2\).

White part \(=616-154=462\ \text{cm}^2\).

OR: The region asked for is the minor segment cut off by the chord \(AB\).

\(\triangle OAB\) is right-angled at \(O\) with \(OA=OB=14\) cm, so its area is \(\tfrac12\times14\times14=98\ \text{cm}^2\).

$$\begin{aligned}\text{Segment}&=\text{sector}-\triangle OAB\\&=154-98=56\ \text{cm}^2\end{aligned}$$

(i) \(154\ \text{cm}^2\) · (ii) 22 cm · (iii) \(462\ \text{cm}^2\) (OR segment \(=56\ \text{cm}^2\))

1 mark for (i) · 1 mark for (ii) · 2 marks for (iii): 1 for the area of the circle (or of \(\triangle OAB\)), 1 for the subtraction and the answer.

Case study4 marks

Shadows on the school ground. One sunny morning the games teacher points out that the sun's rays fall on every upright object at the same angle, so every upright object and its shadow make the same shape of triangle. At that moment the school's flag pole, 6 m high, casts a shadow 8 m long on the level ground, while the school's water tower casts a shadow 40 m long.

  1. Name the two similar triangles in the figure and state the criterion of similarity used. 1
  2. Find the height of the water tower. 1
  3. Find the distance between the top of the water tower and the tip of its shadow. 2ORA student 1.2 m tall is standing on the same ground at that moment. Find the length of her shadow.
Answer & solution

Let the flag pole be \(PQ\) with shadow \(QR\), and the water tower be \(TU\) with shadow \(UV\).

(i)

\(\angle PQR=\angle TUV=90^\circ\) [both stand upright on level ground] and \(\angle PRQ=\angle TVU\) [the sun's rays make equal angles with the ground].

So \(\triangle PQR\sim\triangle TUV\) by the AA criterion.

(ii)

Corresponding sides of similar triangles are proportional:

$$\begin{aligned}\frac{PQ}{TU}&=\frac{QR}{UV}\\ \frac{6}{TU}&=\frac{8}{40}\\ TU&=\frac{6\times40}{8}=30\ \text{m}\end{aligned}$$

(iii)

The tower, its shadow and the ray from the top to the tip form a right triangle \(TUV\), right-angled at \(U\).

$$\begin{aligned}TV&=\sqrt{TU^2+UV^2}\\&=\sqrt{30^2+40^2}=\sqrt{2500}=50\ \text{m}\end{aligned}$$

OR: The student and her shadow give a triangle similar to the same two, so

$$\begin{aligned}\frac{1.2}{s}&=\frac{6}{8}\\ s&=\frac{1.2\times8}{6}=1.6\ \text{m}\end{aligned}$$

(i) \(\triangle PQR\sim\triangle TUV\) by AA · (ii) 30 m · (iii) 50 m (OR shadow \(=1.6\) m)

1 mark for (i) (½ for the triangles, ½ for AA) · 1 mark for (ii) · 2 marks for (iii): 1 for the correct relation, 1 for the answer with units.

How to mark yourself

Mark your paper the way an examiner does: step marks first, answer marks last. If a step is correct but the arithmetic after it is wrong, you still keep the step marks — so read the marking scheme line under each solution before you cut a mark.

  1. Give the mark shown in the marking scheme for each step you actually wrote, not for what you “knew”.
  2. In an MCQ there are no part marks: it is 1 or 0.
  3. Where you attempted both alternatives of an internal choice, mark only the first one.
  4. A missing unit, a missing figure in a geometry answer or a missing reason costs ½ mark each — exactly as in the board.
  5. Add the section totals separately: A __ /20, B __ /10, C __ /18, D __ /20, E __ /12.
